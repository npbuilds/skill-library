# The Errand

Korin had been in the city three weeks, and the errand was the kind of thing that, from inside, was Tuesday. He took the note the way you take a knife, the way you take bad news, the way you take a coin by weight. His hands had already folded it before he decided to fold it. He filed the address in the drawer where he kept the things that might get him killed, which was getting crowded, and set off — gleam to the crowd, dark to himself, the way he liked it.

The fence's stall smelled of wax. His feet had chosen the long route before he chose it, and his mouth was smiling before he told it to. "You're late," the fence said, and Korin filed that too, in the drawer, with the rest. A debt is a door, he thought. A door is a debt. Every drawer is a little grave for the things a careful man doesn't say.

He paid, and the paying felt like something, the way spending always felt like something, the way a burn feels like a lesson. Three weeks in the city and every lesson cost the same. The fence counted the coins the way a priest counts sins. Outside, the Veil rolled overhead, gleam to the crowd, dark to itself.

He told himself the job was clean, and told himself again on the bridge, and told himself the whole way home, which was how he knew it was real. He climbed the stairs. His hand had already found the lock-pick before he decided the door looked wrong. Nothing inside but the dark, and the dark was fine; the dark was Tuesday. He sat down and felt what a man feels when a long errand ends, which was fear. A careful man keeps a drawer for that as well. He filed it, and slept, and that was how he knew it was real.
