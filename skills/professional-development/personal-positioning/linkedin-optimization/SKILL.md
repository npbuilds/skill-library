# LinkedIn Optimization — The Search Engine of Professional Life

LinkedIn is not social media; it is the search index that recruiters, partners, founders, and operators use to discover, evaluate, and triage talent. A profile optimized for the wrong target audience surfaces against the wrong searches. A profile that reads inconsistently with the rest of the user's positioning makes the user look careless. This skill treats LinkedIn as a deliberate artifact of the named trajectory, not as a digital business card.

## Key Concepts

### The Six Surfaces of LinkedIn

Every LinkedIn profile is read through six surfaces, each with distinct optimization rules:

| Surface | Visible When | Optimization Goal |
|---|---|---|
| **Photo** | Always | Recent, professional, looks-like-you-on-a-good-day, neutral background |
| **Headline** | Search results, comments, DMs, suggestion cards | Convey trajectory in 220 characters; combine current title + thesis + target |
| **About** | On profile page, expanded | First 3 lines visible without "see more" — must hook; rest is structured story |
| **Featured** | On profile page | 1–4 pinned items that prove the thesis (publications, talks, GitHub, posts) |
| **Experience** | On profile page | Outcome-led bullets, not responsibility-led; each role a 3–5 bullet exec summary |
| **Activity** | On profile page, in feed | Posts, comments, reactions — read by partners and recruiters as "is this person engaged with the field" |

### The Headline Formula

A high-leverage headline for a senior professional in transition combines three elements:

`[Current Title] | [Thesis Sentence Hook] | [Target Signal]`

Example:
> "Director, Clinical Scientist @ Formation Bio | Physician-operator running biomarker-stratified Phase 2 readouts | Building toward early-stage biotech investing"

This headline:
- Is searchable (recruiters search for "Director Clinical Scientist," "Phase 2," "Formation Bio")
- Signals seriousness (the thesis hook is specific, not generic)
- Telegraphs trajectory (target signal lets people know where to route opportunities)

### The About Hook (First 3 Lines)

LinkedIn truncates the About at ~3 lines on mobile. Those 3 lines must hook a reader who is skimming 30 profiles in a recruiting session. Effective About openers:

- Lead with the thesis sentence (from `narrative-architecture`)
- Avoid corporate-speak openers ("Strategic, results-driven leader passionate about...")
- Avoid third-person formality ("Dr. [name] is a clinical scientist with...")
- Use first-person, present-tense, specific

### Recruiter Visibility Mechanics

LinkedIn Recruiter ranks profiles for a recruiter's search by a combination of: keyword match, completeness, mutual connections, recent activity, "open to work" signal (visible only to recruiters when enabled), and profile-views-by-recruiters in last 7 days. Levers under user control:

1. **Keyword density** — work the role-specific keywords from target JDs into Headline, About, and Experience bullets (naturally; not as a keyword soup)
2. **Completeness** — fill every section LinkedIn surfaces; incomplete profiles rank lower
3. **Activity** — reacting to or commenting on relevant posts every 7–14 days surfaces the profile in recruiter activity feeds
4. **Open-to-work signal** — for senior searches, prefer the recruiter-only signal (avoids the public green frame, which is sometimes pattern-matched as desperation by partners)

### Featured Section Strategy

The Featured section is the highest-leverage real estate on the profile after Headline and About. It accepts up to 4 items. For a venture-targeting senior MD, the most-used composition:

1. A flagship publication (the trial readout or the methods paper)
2. A talk or podcast appearance
3. A linked GitHub or portfolio (proof-of-thinking artifact)
4. A long-form post (LinkedIn article) on a thesis-relevant topic

## Self-Coaching Track

**For your situation (MD → biotech VC/operator):**

1. **Headline rewrite.** Use the three-element formula. Draft 3 variants. Pick one. Edit until it fits 220 characters and surfaces the trajectory.

2. **About first-three-lines rewrite.** Draft so the first 3 lines hook without requiring "see more." Lead with your thesis sentence (from `narrative-architecture`). Then expand into a structured About:
   - Para 1: Thesis (what you do, where you're going, why now)
   - Para 2: One load-bearing piece of work in operator vocabulary
   - Para 3: What you're looking for / how to reach you

3. **Featured section audit.** Open Featured. If it's empty or showing stale content, fix it. Pin (a) your highest-stakes publication, (b) a talk or appearance if you have one, (c) a link to your public GitHub portfolio (this skill library), (d) a thesis-aligned post you've written.

4. **Experience bullets rewrite.** For your current role, replace responsibility-bullets ("Managed clinical trial...") with outcome-bullets ("Owned the [trial] readout that informed [decision], including the [hard call]"). Keep to 3–5 bullets per role.

5. **Activity hygiene.** Every 7–14 days, leave a substantive comment on a thesis-aligned post (a partner's essay, a deal announcement, a new paper). Substantive ≠ "Great post!" — add a specific take.

6. **Recruiter visibility check.** Enable "Open to" with recruiter-only visibility for the role types you're targeting. Specify role titles (associate, principal, operating partner, EIR, etc.) precisely so the matching ranks you correctly.

## Teach / Mentor-Others Track

**When coaching a junior or peer through LinkedIn optimization:**

1. **Anchor on the trajectory before touching the profile.** If they don't have a named target, the optimization is premature. Send them to `narrative-architecture` first.

2. **Audit the Headline together.** It is the highest-leverage 220 characters on the profile. Ask: if a recruiter searched for [target role] in [city], would this headline win against the top 20 competitors? Usually no. Iterate.

3. **Reframe "About" from biography to thesis.** Many mentees treat About as a third-person biography. Reframe to first-person, present-tense, thesis-led. Show one example.

4. **Common failure: vanity Featured items.** Mentees often pin items that flatter their past, not items that prove their thesis. Audit each pinned item against the question "does this support the trajectory I'm aiming at?" Demote the ones that don't.

5. **Activity is uncomfortable for many; coach gently.** Many senior professionals find public engagement awkward. The coaching move is to lower the bar — one substantive comment every 2 weeks, not posts. Comments compound social capital with low ego cost.

6. **The "Open to" public signal warning.** Junior professionals often turn on the public green frame; for senior mentees, coach the recruiter-only setting. The public signal is sometimes pattern-matched as desperation by partner-level audiences.

## When This Applies

- Refreshing the profile for a new search
- After a narrative architecture session, to re-tune the surfaces
- When recruiter contact volume is too low (often the headline or activity is the problem)
- After a role change, to re-optimize for the new trajectory
- Annual hygiene pass
