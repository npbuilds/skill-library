# Panel and Case — Multi-Interviewer Dynamics and Live Problem-Solving

Panels and cases test something solo interviews cannot: how you behave when multiple stakeholders are watching, time pressure is real, and the answer must be constructed live. These formats are over-represented in senior loops — VC IC simulations, executive-team panels, board-candidate screens, working-session interviews. The candidate who treats them as harder versions of solo interviews usually loses; the candidate who prepares for the format-specific dynamics wins.

## Key Concepts

### Panel Dynamics

In a panel interview, the candidate is being read by 3–6 people simultaneously, who often have different roles, agendas, and interaction styles. Common panel patterns:

| Panel Member Type | Behavior | What They're Testing |
|---|---|---|
| **The senior** | Asks broad, open questions | Strategic judgment, presence |
| **The skeptic** | Pushes back on specifics | Resilience under challenge |
| **The peer-to-be** | Tests collaboration vibe | Cultural fit |
| **The note-taker** | Quiet, watching | Reads body language and listening behavior |
| **The host** | Moves the conversation along | Logistics; usually less weight |

The candidate must engage all of them — not just the senior person. Eye contact rotation, addressing the original asker by name, returning to a question someone earlier asked. These are visible behaviors that the panel will discuss after.

### Live Case Mechanics

A live case typically follows this arc:

1. **Setup** (5–10 min) — Interviewer presents the situation, hands over materials
2. **Clarifying questions** (5–10 min) — Candidate asks; quality of questions is part of the test
3. **Structured thinking** (15–25 min) — Candidate works through the problem out loud or on paper/whiteboard
4. **Recommendation** (5–10 min) — Candidate states a recommendation with explicit reasoning
5. **Pushback round** (10–15 min) — Interviewer probes the reasoning; candidate adapts or defends

Common live case domains in biotech VC:
- "Diligence this asset" (an SEC filing, a deck, a one-pager)
- "Build an investment memo for this company"
- "Triangulate the value of this clinical readout"
- "Argue for/against investing in this company"

For executive interviews:
- "How would you structure the clinical strategy for [pipeline]?"
- "Walk through how you'd allocate $50M across these programs"
- "Build a 90-day plan for [scenario]"

### Take-Home Case Mechanics

Take-home cases are typically 8–40 hours of work over 3–7 days, producing a written deliverable (often a 5–15 page memo or 10–20 slide deck). Senior take-homes test: structured thinking, written communication, prioritization under time constraint, and the courage to make a recommendation under uncertainty.

Best practices:

- **Time-box.** Decide the budget; respect it. Most take-homes can be done well in 8–15 hours; more usually means re-work, not better work.
- **Front-load the recommendation.** First page is BLUF. The grader will scan; the structure must serve the scan.
- **Show the work.** Appendix or middle section walks the diligence. The grader wants to see your reasoning, not just your conclusion.
- **Surface the uncertainty.** "Here's what would change my view" — sophisticated graders value this.

### Clarifying Questions in Live Cases

The questions you ask in the first 5 minutes of a live case set the frame. Strong clarifying questions:

- Identify the actual decision being asked ("Are you asking whether to invest, or how much to invest?")
- Surface the constraint ("How much time do I have? What outputs do you want?")
- Surface the missing data ("What's the cap table? What's the cash runway?")
- Avoid trivial clarifications that buy time but don't sharpen the work

Weak clarifying questions:

- Restate the prompt in different words
- Ask for information the materials clearly contain
- Hedge the candidate's commitment to a recommendation

### Common Failure Modes

| Failure | Looks Like | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Engaging only the senior | Eye contact and answers directed at one person | Deliberate rotation; address by name |
| Defending under pushback | "I think I was right because..." | Adapt or update based on new information |
| No recommendation in case | Lists tradeoffs without committing | Force yourself to make a call, even if uncertain |
| Overrunning the time | Long answers in panels; over-engineered cases | Time-box explicitly |
| Rambling clarifying questions | 10 minutes of clarifying for a 60-min case | 3–5 sharp questions, then commit |

## Self-Coaching Track

**For your situation (MD → biotech VC/operator):**

1. **Practice live case format.** Pick 3 public biotech companies. For each, do a 60-minute live diligence — ideally with someone playing interviewer. Time-box. Produce an oral recommendation at minute 60.

2. **Build take-home case templates.** A reusable structure for: investment memo (5 pages), pipeline strategy (10 slides), 90-day plan (5 pages). Have the structure ready before any take-home arrives.

3. **Practice clarifying-question discipline.** Have a friend present a case to you. Ask 3 clarifying questions in 5 minutes. Practice until your default questions are sharp.

4. **Rehearse panel rotation.** With 3 friends acting as a panel, practice eye-contact rotation, addressing the asker by name, returning to earlier questions. The behaviors are awkward at first; they become natural with rehearsal.

5. **Rehearse the pushback round.** Have a mock interviewer push back hard on your recommendation. Practice the "you're right that X is a concern; let me update" move. This is the senior-confidence move and it's hard for many physician candidates.

6. **Time-box every case practice.** The format-specific muscle is time discipline. Don't allow yourself unlimited time in practice; the actual interview is time-bounded.

## Teach / Mentor-Others Track

**When coaching a junior or peer through panel and case prep:**

1. **The panel behaviors are visible and trainable.** Mentees often think "panels are just bigger interviews." They're not — the multi-rater dynamic requires specific behaviors. Teach the rotation, name-using, and earlier-question-return.

2. **Live cases require structured-thinking practice.** Coach mentees to think out loud, structure their thinking visibly (on paper/whiteboard), and produce a recommendation by minute 50 of a 60-minute case.

3. **Force a recommendation, even under uncertainty.** Mentees often hedge. Coach them to commit: "Given what I have, I'd recommend X; here's what would change my view." This is the senior move.

4. **Take-home cases benefit from a template.** Walk mentees through a generic investment-memo structure or pipeline-strategy template they can populate quickly. Templates remove decision fatigue.

5. **Coach against the "study every detail" failure.** Take-home cases tempt mentees to over-research. Coach time-boxing — 50% of effort produces 80% of quality.

6. **Pushback handling is where senior loops are won.** Practice it deliberately. Mentees often become defensive; the win move is to update or defend with new information, calmly.
