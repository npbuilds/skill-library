# Public Speaking — A Trainable Craft, Not a Personality Trait

The most-watched speakers in any field — TED talkers, conference keynoters, podcast guests known for their delivery — share an unsexy secret: they rehearse extensively. The "naturalness" the audience experiences is the product of structure, repetition, and deliberate craft. For a senior MD building a public profile in biotech, public speaking is one of the highest-leverage signals — but only if the talks are well-prepared. A bad public talk creates the wrong public artifact and is harder to remediate than no talk.

## Key Concepts

### Talk Types and Their Distinct Logic

| Type | Length | Structure |
|---|---|---|
| **Conference keynote** | 30–45 min | One big idea, structured journey, memorable close |
| **Conference panel** | 60–75 min, ~10 min per speaker | Quick intro, 2–3 sharp takes, generous to other panelists |
| **Podcast interview** | 45–90 min | Conversational, host-led; have 3–5 stories ready |
| **Fireside chat** | 30–60 min | Conversational, interviewer-led; tighter than podcast |
| **Internal all-hands** | 10–20 min | Concrete, practical, decision-relevant |
| **Investor presentation** | 20–30 min | BLUF, structured deck, questions-friendly |
| **Lecture / academic talk** | 45–60 min | Structured, methodologically rigorous |

Format determines structure. Don't repurpose a keynote as a panel takes-3.

### The Spine — One Big Idea

The strongest talks have one big idea. Not three; not seven. One. Everything in the talk serves it. The audience should be able to repeat the idea to a friend the next day.

For a senior MD speaking on biotech topics, examples of one-big-idea talks:

- "Why physician-judgment is a structural edge in early-stage biotech investing"
- "What we learned about endpoint selection from the [Trial X] readout"
- "The 5 questions that should drive every clinical-development diligence"

The one-big-idea constraint forces priority. If you have three big ideas, you have three talks; pick one.

### Talk Structure (Keynote-Length)

A 30-minute keynote, structured:

| Section | Time | Function |
|---|---|---|
| **Hook** | 1–2 min | A specific story, image, or question that opens the audience's attention |
| **Thesis** | 2–3 min | Your one big idea, named explicitly |
| **Reasoning** | 15–20 min | 2–3 supporting arguments, each with a story or example |
| **Implications** | 4–5 min | What this means for the audience; what they should do differently |
| **Close** | 1–2 min | Memorable line; call to action; thank you |

Q&A is its own thing — don't include it in the talk-time count.

### Delivery Mechanics

| Element | Practice |
|---|---|
| **Pace** | 130–150 words/min for keynote; 170–200 for conversation |
| **Pause** | After key points; after questions before answering |
| **Eye contact** | Rotate across audience zones (left / center / right) |
| **Hands** | Use them; don't grip the podium |
| **Voice** | Vary pitch and volume; avoid monotone |
| **Slides** | Sparse; one idea per slide; no walls of text |

These are trainable. Record yourself; play back; adjust.

### Rehearsal Protocol

For a high-stakes talk:

1. **Outline before slides.** Outline the talk as bullets; verify the structure works before designing slides.
2. **Slides as visual companion, not script.** Slides support the talk; the talk doesn't read the slides.
3. **Solo rehearsal aloud, 3–5 times.** Out loud — silent reading doesn't expose pace, length, or awkward transitions.
4. **Time it.** A 25-minute talk in solo rehearsal often expands to 35 minutes live; rehearse with the time discipline.
5. **Friendly audience rehearsal at least once.** A real listener exposes blind spots solo doesn't.
6. **Q&A prep separately.** Anticipate the 5–10 most-likely questions; have answers ready.

The night before, do not over-rehearse. One quiet read-through; sleep.

### Panel Performance

Panels are different from keynotes. The performance markers:

- **Be brief.** Panel members who answer at 4 min when 90 sec is ideal lose audience and panelists.
- **Sharp takes.** A clear, possibly contrarian, 90-second answer beats a balanced 4-minute answer.
- **Generous to other panelists.** Acknowledge their points; build on them; don't preen.
- **Follow the moderator's lead.** Don't pivot away from their questions.
- **Have 3–4 prepared talking points** for the topics likely to come up; route from any question to the talking points.

### Common Failure Modes

| Failure | Looks Like | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Multiple big ideas | Audience can't summarize the talk | Cut to one |
| Reading the slides | Speaker faces screen; reads text | Slides as visual; talk is yours |
| No hook | Opens with "I'm pleased to be here" | A specific story, image, or question |
| Over-runs | 35 min talk in 25 min slot | Time-discipline rehearsal |
| Long-winded panel | 4-min panel answer | 90-second discipline |
| No close | Talk ends with "any questions?" | Memorable close before Q&A |
| Q&A unprepared | Stuck on the first question | Pre-anticipate the 5–10 questions |

## Self-Coaching Track

**For your situation (MD → biotech VC/operator, building public profile):**

1. **Build your "one big idea" library.** What 3–5 talks would you give if asked? Each is one big idea with structured reasoning. Have these ready; opportunities surface unpredictably.

2. **Per opportunity, pick the best fit.** When invited to speak, pick the talk from the library whose one-big-idea fits the venue. Don't invent a new talk under time pressure.

3. **Outline → rehearse aloud → adjust → slides.** Don't draft slides first. The outline is the talk.

4. **Time yourself in rehearsal.** Use the actual venue's time slot. Cut ruthlessly.

5. **Record at least one rehearsal.** Watch it. The watch-back is uncomfortable but high-leverage.

6. **For panels, prepare 3 sharp takes per likely topic.** When asked, route from question to one of your prepared takes in 90 seconds.

7. **Post-talk, capture the lessons.** What landed? What didn't? What would you do differently? These compound over time.

## Teach / Mentor-Others Track

**When coaching a junior or peer through public speaking:**

1. **The one-big-idea constraint is the highest-leverage lesson.** Most mentees try to cover too much. The constraint forces priority.

2. **Coach against slide-driven talks.** Mentees often build slides first. Reframe: outline → rehearse → slides. The order matters.

3. **Solo rehearsal aloud is non-negotiable.** Mentees often think silent rehearsal is enough. It isn't. The aloud rehearsal exposes problems silent reading hides.

4. **Time discipline.** Mentees almost always over-run. Coach time-rehearsal from day 1; cut content if needed to fit the slot.

5. **The watch-back is uncomfortable but irreplaceable.** Coach mentees to record and watch; they'll see issues no one else can point out.

6. **Panel mentorship: 90-second discipline.** Mentees on panels often answer too long. Practice 90-second responses to common questions until it becomes the default.
