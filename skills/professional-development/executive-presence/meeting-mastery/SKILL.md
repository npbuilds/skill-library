# Meeting Mastery — The Visible Senior Skill

A meeting is a small organizational system: limited time, multiple stakeholders, a goal that may or may not get achieved. The senior person in the room is the one who notices when the system is failing and intervenes — who names the unaddressed disagreement, who time-boxes the digression, who closes the meeting with a decision rather than letting it drift to "let's discuss again next week." This skill is the visible part of executive presence; everyone in the room sees it happen.

## Key Concepts

### Meeting Types and Their Distinct Logic

| Type | Purpose | Right Length | Right Frame |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Decision meeting** | Make a specific decision | 30–60 min | What's the decision; who decides; what's the criteria |
| **Information meeting** | Share or gather information | 15–30 min (often should be a doc) | What's the info; who needs it; how is it landing |
| **Brainstorming** | Generate options | 60–90 min | What's the question; what are the constraints; how do we evaluate |
| **Status update** | Surface progress and risks | 15–30 min | What's done; what's at risk; what asks for the room |
| **Strategy session** | Align on direction | 90–180 min | What's the situation; what are the options; what's the path |
| **1:1** | Manage relationship + work | 30 min recurring | What's on your mind; what do you need from me |

The most-common meeting failure is type confusion — running a decision meeting as a brainstorm, or treating a strategy session as a status update.

### The Agenda

A good agenda has three properties:

1. **Specific objective** — "By the end of this meeting, we will have decided X" — not "discuss Y"
2. **Time-allocated** — Each segment has a budget
3. **Pre-read identified** — What people should have read before arriving; without this, the first 15 min are spent reading

Send the agenda 24+ hours in advance. People who come unprepared self-select.

### Opening Moves (First 5 Minutes)

The first 5 minutes set the meeting's frame:

1. **State the objective.** "We're here to decide X."
2. **Name the constraints.** "We have 45 minutes; we need to land on a decision today."
3. **Confirm the room.** "Did everyone read the pre-read? Any questions before we dive in?"
4. **Acknowledge the elephant** if there is one. "I know we've been circling this for two weeks; today we're closing it out."

These moves take 5 minutes and pay back across the entire meeting.

### Decision Protocols

When the meeting is a decision meeting, the senior move is to name the decision protocol:

| Protocol | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| **Consensus** | Everyone agrees; rare and slow |
| **Consultative** | One person decides after hearing others |
| **Voting** | Majority rules |
| **Owner** | One person owns the decision; meeting informs |
| **CEO call** | CEO decides; meeting surfaces options |

Naming the protocol up front prevents the disagreement at the end about whether the decision was actually made.

### Derailment Recovery

Meetings derail in predictable ways. The senior moves to recover:

| Derailment | Recovery Move |
|---|---|
| Deep dive into a tangent | "Hold that for offline; let's come back to the main thread" |
| Two people in a side argument | "Let me table this for the two of you to resolve and report back" |
| One person dominating | "I want to hear from [other person] on this" |
| Stuck on a non-decision | "Let me reframe; what we're actually deciding is X" |
| Out of time | "We have 5 minutes left; let's lock the decision and follow-ups" |

The pattern: name what's happening, redirect, move on. Naming is the senior move; tolerating is the staff move.

### Closing Moves (Last 5 Minutes)

A meeting that ends without explicit close drifts into the next meeting. Closing moves:

1. **Confirm the decision.** "So we've decided X. Anyone disagree?"
2. **Confirm the owner.** "Who owns the next step?"
3. **Confirm the timeline.** "By when?"
4. **Confirm the next checkpoint.** "When do we re-touch?"

Without these four, "follow-up confusion" is guaranteed.

### The Follow-Up Note

Within 24 hours, a brief note (Slack or email) to the meeting attendees:

- Decisions made
- Owners and timelines
- Items tabled
- Next checkpoint

The follow-up note is the artifact that makes the meeting durable. It also signals senior-org-discipline to everyone who reads it.

### Common Failure Modes

| Failure | Looks Like | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| No agenda | Meeting opens with "what should we discuss?" | Pre-distributed agenda 24h+ in advance |
| Type confusion | Decision meeting run as brainstorm | Name the type; run accordingly |
| No objective | Meeting opens with "let's chat about X" | State decision/output as objective |
| No close | Meeting ends; no decision recorded | Closing 4-step ritual |
| No follow-up | No note after the meeting | Within-24h follow-up |
| Tolerating derailment | Letting tangents play out | Name them; redirect |

## Self-Coaching Track

**For your situation (MD → biotech VC/operator):**

1. **Audit your meeting calendar.** For your last 10 meetings, classify each by type. How many were the right type? How many had an agenda 24h in advance? How many ended with a follow-up note?

2. **Build agenda templates per meeting type.** Decision-meeting template, status-update template, 1:1 template. Reuse the structure; vary the content.

3. **Practice the opening 5 minutes.** This is muscle that gets better with reps. State objective, constraints, confirm pre-read, address elephant.

4. **Name the decision protocol when running decision meetings.** The 30 seconds spent naming the protocol pays back enormously in the meeting's quality.

5. **Practice the derailment moves.** Have responses ready: "hold that for offline," "let me hear from X," "let me reframe." When the moment arrives, you don't have time to invent them.

6. **Calendar 5 minutes after each meeting for the follow-up note.** Without the calendar block, the note doesn't get sent.

7. **Per high-stakes meeting, do a 10-minute pre-mortem.** "What's the most likely way this meeting fails? How do I prevent that?"

## Teach / Mentor-Others Track

**When coaching a junior or peer through meeting mastery:**

1. **Type-classification is the first lesson.** Mentees often run meetings without naming the type. Walk through the table; have them classify their last 5 meetings.

2. **The agenda discipline transfers fast.** Coach the 24h-in-advance standard. Once it's a habit, meeting quality improves immediately.

3. **The opening 5 minutes are trainable.** Show one good opening; rehearse with the mentee. Mentees often skip these and lose 10 minutes per meeting.

4. **Decision protocol naming is high-leverage.** Mentees often skip this and end meetings with ambiguous outcomes. Coach the 30-second naming.

5. **The derailment moves are language-trainable.** Walk through the recovery moves; rehearse the language. Without rehearsal, mentees freeze when derailment happens.

6. **The follow-up note is a non-negotiable habit.** Mentees often skip; the meetings then drift. Insist on the 24h follow-up; show one good example.
