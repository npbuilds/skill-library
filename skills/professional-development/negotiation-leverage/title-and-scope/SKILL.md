# Title and Scope — The Structural Levers Beyond Comp

Comp gets the most attention in offer negotiations; title and scope often matter more for the long-term trajectory. A high comp at a low title with narrow scope under-leverages the next role. A modest comp at a senior title with broad scope over-leverages it. The senior negotiator addresses these explicitly, knowing that title shapes external perception and scope shapes daily reality.

## Key Concepts

### Title vs Scope vs Reporting

Three distinct structural elements:

| Element | What It Means | Affects |
|---|---|---|
| **Title** | Your level / signal to the outside world | Future market position, recruiter rankings, external credibility |
| **Scope** | What you actually own — programs, headcount, budget, charter | Daily work, internal influence, learning rate |
| **Reporting** | Who you report to | Day-to-day relationship, board exposure, decision authority |

These are negotiated separately and trade against each other. Higher title sometimes comes with narrower scope; broader scope sometimes comes with lower title.

### When Title Matters More

| Scenario | Why |
|---|---|
| **Next-role anticipation** | The next title-step matters; "Director" → "VP" is a step-up move |
| **External signaling** | Industry-facing role where title appears in press, board bios, etc. |
| **Recruiter ranking** | LinkedIn searches and recruiter pipelines weight by title |
| **Comp band ceiling** | Some companies cap comp by title; level matters for future raises |

### When Scope Matters More

| Scenario | Why |
|---|---|
| **Building credibility** | Owning a P&L or large team is what makes the next role possible |
| **Skill compounding** | Broader scope = more learning across functions |
| **Organizational influence** | Scope determines how much you matter inside the company |
| **Boardroom presence** | Heads-of-function with broad scope tend to present to boards |

### The Title-Inflation Trap

Some companies, especially smaller startups, inflate titles ("VP of Clinical Strategy" for a mid-level individual contributor). This is a trap when:

- The next employer doesn't recognize the inflated title
- The internal scope doesn't match the external signal
- The user gets stuck at "VP" too long because the underlying work doesn't justify "SVP"

The honest read: a title that's two levels above the actual work hurts the next move more than it helps the current one.

### Reporting Line as Hidden Lever

Reporting line is often more negotiable than title and often more consequential:

- Reporting to the CEO vs reporting to a VP shapes daily access, board exposure, and information flow
- Reporting to the CSO vs reporting to the COO shapes which decisions you participate in
- Cross-reporting (to two execs) is sometimes negotiated; rare but powerful

For a Head of Clinical Development role, the right reporting line might matter more than a title bump.

### Negotiable Elements of Scope

| Element | Description |
|---|---|
| **Programs / portfolio** | Which programs you own |
| **Headcount** | How many direct and indirect reports |
| **Budget** | What you control / approve |
| **Charter** | What kinds of decisions are yours alone |
| **External rep** | Whether you represent the company externally (conferences, investor calls) |
| **Board access** | Whether you regularly present to or attend board meetings |
| **Cross-functional authority** | Decisions you can make that affect other functions |

Each is negotiable; few candidates negotiate them explicitly.

### How to Argue Title and Scope

The argument is always grounded in the role and the market, not personal need:

- **Title argument**: "Looking at comparable roles at companies of similar stage, this scope is typically titled 'VP'; could we adjust the title to match?"
- **Scope argument**: "For this role to be successful, I'll need ownership of [specific scope]; can we lock that into the role definition?"
- **Reporting argument**: "Given the strategic nature of [program], I think direct CEO reporting will be more effective than reporting through [VP]; would you consider it?"

### Common Failure Modes

| Failure | Looks Like | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Accept title without scrutiny | Take whatever title is offered | Audit against market for similar scope |
| Negotiate title, ignore scope | Win the title, lose the actual role | Negotiate both |
| Accept inflated title | Take the "VP" without the underlying scope | Be honest about what hurts you next |
| Skip reporting-line negotiation | Accept whoever you're assigned to report to | Reporting is often the most-leverage lever |
| No written charter | Verbal scope agreement | Get the scope in writing in the offer letter |

## Self-Coaching Track

**For your situation (MD → biotech VC/operator):**

1. **For your offer (or candidate offer), audit the title against the market.** Look at 10 LinkedIn profiles of people at peer companies in similar scope; what are they titled? If your offered title is lower, that's negotiable.

2. **List the scope elements that matter to you.** Programs, headcount, budget, board access, external rep. Negotiate the ones that compound your trajectory most.

3. **Audit reporting line.** Is the offered reporting line optimal for your trajectory? If not, the negotiation might focus there rather than on title.

4. **Avoid title-inflation traps.** If a smaller startup offers a title two levels above your actual work, weigh the long-term cost — does it help or hurt you next?

5. **Get scope in writing.** Verbal scope agreements drift. The offer letter or follow-up email should specify scope: programs owned, headcount, budget, charter.

6. **Cross-load with `optionality-architecture`.** Title and scope choices have option-set consequences; evaluate them through that frame.

7. **For current role, audit scope expansion opportunities.** Sometimes title and scope can be negotiated mid-tenure when responsibilities have grown beyond the original definition.

## Teach / Mentor-Others Track

**When coaching a junior or peer through title and scope:**

1. **The three-element decomposition is the first lesson.** Mentees often conflate title, scope, and reporting. Walk through the table.

2. **Title-inflation trap is non-obvious to junior mentees.** Many would happily take an inflated title. Walk through the cost; show examples of stuck careers from accepting inflation.

3. **Scope negotiation is undertaught.** Mentees often negotiate title only. Coach the explicit scope conversation; have them list the scope elements before the negotiation.

4. **Reporting-line awareness changes outcomes.** Most mentees accept reporting line as given. Reframe: it's negotiable; it's often the highest-leverage structural lever.

5. **Coach getting scope in writing.** Mentees often accept verbal scope agreements; they drift. Insist on offer-letter or follow-up-email documentation.

6. **Mid-tenure renegotiation is real.** When responsibilities have grown, title and scope can be renegotiated. Coach mentees to surface this proactively rather than waiting for promotion cycles.

## When This Applies

- Evaluating an offer
- Mid-tenure when scope has grown beyond original definition
- During a re-org when roles are being redefined
- Negotiating return-from-leave terms
- Coaching others through their negotiations
