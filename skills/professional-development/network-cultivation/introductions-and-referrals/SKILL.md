# Introductions and Referrals — The Warm-Intro Economy

Most senior career moves happen through warm intros. The data is stark: cold outreach converts at 25–40% when done well; warm intros convert at 70–90%. The math means that the small number of warm intros you can request is more valuable than the large number of cold messages you can send. The craft is in asking well, giving generously, and treating the social capital of your connectors as something you borrow rather than spend.

## Key Concepts

### The Double Opt-In Protocol

The professional standard for warm intros is the double opt-in:

1. **Requester asks connector** if the connector would be comfortable introducing them to a target. Requester includes a short forwarding-ready blurb.
2. **Connector pings target** with the blurb, asking if target is open to the intro. Target can say yes, no, or "not now" silently.
3. **If target says yes**, connector makes the formal introduction.
4. **If target says no or no response**, connector quietly drops it and tells the requester. No social cost to the target; the connector has preserved the relationship.

The double opt-in protects the connector's social capital and the target's time. It is the protocol senior professionals expect; using it signals senior literacy.

### The Forwarding-Ready Blurb

The blurb is a 2–4 sentence paragraph the connector can paste into their ping. It must be self-contained — the connector should not have to rewrite it. Components:

1. **Who you are** in one sentence, target-audience-tuned
2. **What you'd want to discuss** — a specific topic, not "career advice"
3. **The specific ask** — 20-minute conversation, single question, etc.
4. **Light optionality for the target** — "Happy to meet whenever works"

Example blurb:

> *"Nirav is a Director-level clinical scientist at Formation Bio who's been leading our Phase 2 oncology readout and is exploring a move into early-stage biotech VC. He'd love 20 minutes with [Partner Name] specifically about how clinical-development judgment shows up in early-stage diligence at [Firm]. Happy to find time in the next 3 weeks; flexible on format."*

The blurb is the artifact the entire intro flow depends on. Spend time on it.

### When to Ask for an Intro

Reserve warm-intro asks for targets where:

- The target is genuinely hard to reach cold
- The connector has actual relationship with the target (not just LinkedIn connection)
- The conversation will be substantively useful to you
- You can articulate why this specific person, not just any person at the firm

Don't ask for intros to people you could reasonably reach cold (it wastes the connector's social capital), to people the connector doesn't really know (it doesn't help), or with vague topic asks (the connector won't know how to set it up).

### When to Decline Giving an Intro

You will be asked for intros you shouldn't give. Decline gracefully when:

- The requester is asking for an intro to someone you don't actually know well
- The topic is one you can't represent (you don't understand what they'd discuss)
- The requester hasn't earned the credibility for the target's time
- You have reservations about the requester's professionalism

The decline: "I don't think I'm the right connector here — I don't actually know [target] well / I haven't worked with [target] in that area / I'd want to know more before making the intro." Don't fake-make intros you don't want to make; the target will notice and your social capital will leak.

### The Reciprocity Ledger

A senior professional makes more intros than they ask for. The rough working ratio: give 3–5 intros for every 1 you ask for. Reasons:

- Connectors are remembered as generous; generosity returns
- Giving intros builds your "warm node" status in the ecosystem
- Reciprocity is durable when it flows in both directions

Track this informally. If your ratio is upside down (you ask for many, give few), you're spending social capital faster than you build it.

### Tracking Outstanding Intros

When you've asked for an intro and it's pending, track:

- Who you asked
- Who the target is
- The blurb you sent (so you can reference it)
- The date of the request
- Status (asked, accepted, intro made, conversation done)

After 10–14 days with no movement, a light nudge to the connector is appropriate. "Want to check in on the [target] intro — no pressure either way." After that, drop it.

### Common Failure Modes

| Failure | Looks Like | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Asking without a blurb | "Could you intro me to [target]?" | Always provide forwarding-ready blurb |
| Asking too many connectors at once | Asking 5 people to intro you to the same target | Pick the strongest connector; if they decline, try one more |
| Skipping double opt-in | Demanding the connector "just intro me" | Always offer double opt-in; senior connectors expect it |
| No follow-up to connector | Intro happens, requester never reports back | Always tell connector how it went; they invested social capital |
| Asking strangers for intros | Treating LinkedIn 1st-degree as if it implies relationship | Ask only people who actually know the target |
| Hoarding intros | Never offering intros to others | Reciprocity ledger goes upside down; ask flow eventually dries up |

## Self-Coaching Track

**For your situation (MD → biotech VC/operator):**

1. **Build your blurb library.** For each target archetype (VC partner, founder, advisor, recruiter), have a 2–4 sentence blurb ready. Tune per target before sending.

2. **Audit your connector network.** From your relationship map, identify the 10–20 people who could realistically introduce you to the partners and operators on your target list. These are your highest-leverage connectors.

3. **Be generous-first.** Before asking for intros for yourself, look for intros you can make — your network includes people who'd benefit from each other. Make 3–5 intros before making your first ask.

4. **Use double opt-in always.** Even with close connectors. It's the senior protocol; using it signals you're senior; not using it occasionally costs you intros.

5. **Track requests.** Simple Notion or spreadsheet. Connector, target, blurb, date, status. Without tracking, you lose visibility on what's outstanding and over-pester connectors.

6. **Close the loop with connectors.** After every intro happens, send the connector a one-paragraph follow-up: how it went, what came of it, thank you.

## Teach / Mentor-Others Track

**When coaching a junior or peer through intros and referrals:**

1. **The double opt-in is the most-leverage idea.** Many mentees don't know it. Walk them through the protocol with a worked example.

2. **The blurb is the artifact that determines conversion.** Mentees often don't provide one or write a bad one. Coach the four-component structure; practice on a real intro request.

3. **The reciprocity ledger framing changes behavior.** Mentees often think about intros as one-directional (asking). Reframe: the senior pattern is giving more than asking. The shift takes time but is durable.

4. **Coach against bulk-asking.** Mentees sometimes ask multiple connectors for intros to the same target. This is bad form and bad strategy. Coach: pick the strongest connector first.

5. **The loop-closing thank-you is a habit, not a one-time decision.** Coach mentees to automatically send a thank-you within 48 hours of every intro happening. Most mentees forget.

6. **Saying no to intros gracefully is undertaught.** Mentees often fake-make intros to be polite, then regret it. Coach the graceful decline language.
