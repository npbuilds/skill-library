# Mentorship Design — Structure for an Otherwise-Fragile Relationship

Most mentorships fail because they are unstructured. The mentee asks vague open questions; the mentor gives vague open advice; both feel awkward; the relationship dies. The mentorships that compound have structure: a defined topic or transition, an agreed cadence, specific asks each session, reciprocity that flows in both directions, and an honest exit when the work is done. This skill teaches that structure — both for finding mentors and for being one.

## Key Concepts

### What Mentorship Actually Provides

The four kinds of value a mentor delivers:

1. **Pattern recognition** — They've seen the situation before; they can name what's likely to happen
2. **Network access** — They can introduce you to the people you need to know
3. **Calibration** — They can tell you whether your judgment of a situation is accurate
4. **Sponsorship** — In strong cases, they actively advocate for you in rooms you're not in

Different mentors deliver different mixes. The mentee's job is to know what kind of value they're seeking from each relationship.

### Sponsor vs Mentor vs Advisor vs Coach

| Role | What They Do | Cadence | Compensation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Mentor** | Counsel, share patterns, occasional intros | Quarterly to monthly | Free |
| **Sponsor** | Active advocacy, opens doors, takes risk on you | Embedded in current org | Free (you owe results) |
| **Advisor** | Specific domain advice; sometimes equity-compensated | Monthly | Often equity (early-stage) |
| **Coach** | Structured personal development; trained role | Weekly to monthly | Paid ($300–$1000+/hour) |

Mentees often ask for "mentorship" when they actually need a coach (specific structured development) or an advisor (specific domain question). Naming the actual ask matters.

### Asking Someone to Be Your Mentor

The blunt "will you be my mentor?" ask usually fails — it's too big, too vague, and signals that the user wants the relationship more than the work. The high-conversion approach:

1. **Start with one specific ask.** Not "will you mentor me," but "would you be open to a 30-minute conversation about [specific topic]?" If the conversation goes well, propose a specific follow-up.
2. **Earn the relationship over 2–3 conversations.** Show up prepared, ask substantive questions, follow through on suggestions. Demonstrate that the mentor's time will be well-used.
3. **Then propose structure.** "Would you be open to a quarterly conversation? I have a few areas where I'd really value your perspective."
4. **Don't use the word "mentor" in the ask.** It's a heavy word. Let the relationship become mentorship over time.

### Designing the Cadence

A working mentorship has a cadence the mentor doesn't experience as burden. Common patterns:

| Cadence | Format | Best For |
|---|---|---|
| Quarterly 60-min | Phone or in-person | Most senior mentorships |
| Monthly 30-min | Phone | High-touch relationships in transition periods |
| Annual deep-dive | Half-day or longer | Strategic / long-term mentorships |
| Ad-hoc on triggers | Email or short call | Mature relationships where you've built trust |

Always: agenda set in advance by the mentee. Always: a thank-you with one concrete follow-up after the conversation.

### Reciprocity Design

Mentorships die when they become one-directional. Even at significant seniority gaps, the mentee can offer:

- **Field intelligence** — what younger operators / founders are seeing
- **Specific intros** — to people the mentor wouldn't otherwise meet
- **Useful artifacts** — papers, posts, decks the mentor would want to read
- **Follow-up reports** — when their advice played out, tell them what happened
- **Discretion** — being trustworthy with what they share is itself reciprocity

A mentee who never reciprocates is a mentee whose mentorships will dry up.

### The Exit

Mentorships have natural arcs. When the mentee has internalized what they came to learn, or the mentor's pattern doesn't match the new situation, the relationship should evolve to lower-cadence acquaintance — not be forced through artificial perpetuity.

The graceful exit:
1. Name what you learned (in a substantive thank-you)
2. Acknowledge that the focus area has shifted
3. Offer to remain in touch on lower cadence
4. Honor the relationship — they invested in you

### Being a Mentor Yourself

Once a senior professional becomes the mentor, the mirror skills apply:

- Set scope (what you'll mentor on, what you won't)
- Set cadence (don't over-promise)
- Be honest (the most-valuable mentor is the one who tells the truth)
- Make introductions (your network is one of the highest-value things you provide)
- Know when to graduate them (a mentor who never lets the mentee outgrow them is stuck)

## Self-Coaching Track

**For your situation (MD → biotech VC/operator):**

1. **Name what you need.** Coach? Sponsor? Mentor? Advisor? Different needs require different relationships. For a venture transition, you likely need 2–3 mentors (one VC partner, one operator who made a similar transition, one ecosystem connector) and possibly a coach (paid, structured).

2. **Build the candidate list.** For each mentor type, identify 5–10 candidates. Use `network-cultivation/ecosystem-mapping` to source.

3. **First-conversation ask, not mentor ask.** For each candidate, design a specific 30-minute conversation. Use `network-cultivation/cold-outreach` craft.

4. **After 2–3 conversations, propose structure.** With the relationships that worked, propose a quarterly cadence. Don't propose with the relationships where the chemistry isn't there.

5. **Build reciprocity from day 1.** What can you offer? Field intelligence on Formation Bio's space? Intros from your physician network? An early read on the public artifacts you're building? Reciprocity makes the mentorship sustainable.

6. **Become a mentor for 1–2 juniors.** Once you're 6+ months into the transition, deliberately mentor someone earlier in the journey. The teaching reinforces your own learning, and the practice itself is part of becoming senior.

## Teach / Mentor-Others Track

**When coaching a junior or peer through mentorship design:**

1. **Diagnose what they actually need.** Many mentees say "I need a mentor" when they need a coach, an advisor, or a specific domain conversation. Name the right relationship type.

2. **Coach against the "will you be my mentor" ask.** Show them the specific-conversation alternative. The shift is high-leverage.

3. **Reciprocity is non-obvious for junior mentees.** They often think the seniority gap means they have nothing to offer. Coach them: field intelligence, intros, follow-up reports, and discretion are all real reciprocity.

4. **The agenda discipline.** Mentees often show up to mentor calls without an agenda. Coach: every call has an agenda set 24 hours in advance, even if just three bullets. The discipline pays back the mentor's time.

5. **The thank-you-with-follow-up is a high-leverage habit.** After every mentor conversation, a substantive thank-you within 48 hours, with one concrete thing the mentee did with the advice. Builds trust quickly.

6. **The graceful exit is undertaught.** Many mentees feel they should stay in mentorships forever. Coach: relationships have arcs; honoring the arc is the senior move.

## When This Applies

- Re-evaluating existing mentor relationships
- Becoming a mentor for a junior colleague
- Re-warming a mentorship that's gone dormant
- Auditing whether the mentorships you have are delivering value
