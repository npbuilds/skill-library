# Performance Review Craft — The High-Stakes Annual Artifact

Most senior professionals treat the performance review as bureaucratic theater — fill in the form, attend the meeting, move on. The reality is different: the self-assessment is read by leveling committees, comp calibration meetings, and (sometimes) board members. The review conversation often determines promotion timing, raise size, and bonus award. The artifact and conversation are both negotiable instruments; treating them as such produces materially different outcomes.

## Key Concepts

### What the Review Actually Influences

The review feeds into multiple downstream decisions:

| Decision | When | Who Reads It |
|---|---|---|
| **Salary increase** | Year-end | Manager, HR, calibration committee |
| **Bonus award** | Year-end | Manager, HR, sometimes CEO |
| **Promotion consideration** | Promotion cycle | Manager, leveling committee, sometimes board |
| **Stretch assignment / scope expansion** | Throughout year | Manager, senior leadership |
| **Calibration against peers** | Calibration meetings | Cross-manager calibration committee |
| **Talent reviews** | Quarterly senior-team reviews | Senior team, sometimes board |

The self-assessment is the input the user controls. Investing in it pays back across all of these.

### The Self-Assessment Structure

A strong self-assessment has four sections:

1. **Outcomes / accomplishments** — Specific, quantified, outcome-led. Not "led trial activities" but "owned the [Trial X] readout including the Type C interaction that converted endpoint pathway from accelerated to full approval, informing $80M Series B."

2. **Behaviors / leadership** — How you did the work. Specific examples of the company's leadership behaviors or values; not platitudes.

3. **Development areas / growth** — Honest naming of what you're working on, with evidence of progress. This is where most self-assessments fail (either by skipping or by listing unrelated improvements).

4. **Forward-looking / next year** — What you're aiming at; how it fits the org's strategy. This is the section managers most value because it shapes the next year's planning.

### The Outcome-Led Bullet

Same craft as resume bullets:

`[Strong verb] [scope] [outcome with metric]`

Bad: "Worked on Phase 2 protocol amendment."
Good: "Led the Phase 2 protocol amendment for [program], adding the 200-patient biomarker-stratified cohort, which increased PoS by ~15% and informed the $40M Series B raise."

### Managing Up the Review

Strong reviewees do work between cycles to shape the year-end review:

- **Quarterly check-ins with the manager.** Don't wait for year-end; surface progress and concerns through the year.
- **Build the evidence trail.** Save artifacts (board updates, emails, recognitions) that document your work.
- **Reset expectations early.** If a goal becomes unrealistic, renegotiate it with the manager before year-end.
- **Surface visibility moments.** Senior decisions are influenced by visibility; structure your work to include moments senior leaders see.
- **Calibrate against peer set.** Discreetly understand where you sit relative to peers; surface the right work for differentiation.

### The Calibration Frame

Most companies do "calibration" — managers meet across functions to ensure ratings are consistent. The user's review is being compared against peers in real-time. Implications:

- The strongest accomplishments aren't enough on their own; they need to be expressed in language calibration uses
- Outcomes that span functions (cross-functional impact, scope beyond your direct authority) calibrate better than purely-functional work
- Quantified impact ($X raised, Y% PoS uplift, Z months saved) calibrates better than qualitative narrative

### Common Failure Modes

| Failure | Looks Like | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Modesty under-claim | Listing work without surfacing ownership | Use the credibility-translation matrix |
| Unquantified bullets | "Improved trial enrollment" | Specific metrics; if no metric, name the qualitative outcome |
| Missing development section | Skipping the growth-area discussion | Honest naming + evidence of progress |
| No forward-looking section | Review only looks backward | Next-year section is high-leverage |
| Late preparation | Drafting the night before due | 2–4 hours over multiple days |
| No managing-up | Year-end review is the first time manager hears certain things | Quarterly visibility through the year |

### The Review Conversation

The conversation is its own artifact:

1. **Be specific.** Bring examples, not generalities.
2. **Listen first.** Hear the manager's read before reacting.
3. **Engage with feedback.** Don't accept passively; ask clarifying questions; engage on disagreement.
4. **Negotiate the rating.** If you disagree with a rating, surface it with evidence.
5. **Discuss next year.** The forward-looking part of the conversation often matters most.
6. **Take notes.** The conversation has follow-ups; capture them.

### Promotion Discussions

When the review is also a promotion case:

- **Build the case explicitly.** Promotion criteria are usually documented; map your accomplishments to each criterion.
- **Provide evidence at the criterion level.** Not "I led trials" but "Criterion 3 [strategic scope]: led the X program from Phase 2 to FDA submission, including..."
- **Ask the manager what's missing.** If the case isn't strong enough yet, what would close the gap?
- **Be patient if the answer is "next cycle."** Promotion timing is partly out of your control; managing the gap is the work.

## Self-Coaching Track

**For your situation (MD → biotech VC/operator):**

1. **Build the evidence trail year-round.** Keep a quarterly "wins" doc — accomplishments, recognitions, key decisions you owned. This is the raw material for year-end.

2. **Draft early.** 2–4 hours over 3–5 days, not the night before. The early draft surfaces gaps; the iteration polishes.

3. **Use outcome-led bullets.** Cross-load with `resume-craft`. Bullet-by-bullet: verb, scope, outcome with metric.

4. **Don't skip development areas.** Honest naming + evidence of progress is the senior move. Modest claimers under-perform here.

5. **The forward-looking section is high-leverage.** Make it strategic; tie to org direction. Managers carry your forward-section into planning.

6. **Practice the conversation.** Mock with a peer or coach; rehearse handling pushback, asking clarifying questions, negotiating ratings.

7. **For promotion cycles, build the explicit case doc.** Map accomplishments to criteria. Make the manager's job easier; they're advocating for you in calibration.

## Teach / Mentor-Others Track

**When coaching a junior or peer through performance review craft:**

1. **Reframe from bureaucratic theater to artifact.** Many mentees treat the review as a checkbox. Walk through what the artifact actually influences.

2. **The quarterly "wins" doc is high-leverage.** Mentees usually don't keep one. Coach the habit; the year-end review becomes 10x easier.

3. **Outcome-led bullets transfer the resume skill.** Mentees often use responsibility-led bullets. Walk through the conversion with one example.

4. **The development section discipline is non-obvious.** Many mentees skip it or list unrelated items. Coach honest naming + evidence.

5. **Coach for the calibration frame.** Mentees often write for their manager; the review is read more broadly. Reframe: write for the calibration committee.

6. **Promotion case discipline.** When promotion is on the line, coach the explicit case doc. Mapping accomplishments to criteria is half the work.
