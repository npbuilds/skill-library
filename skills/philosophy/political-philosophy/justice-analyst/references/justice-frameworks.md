# Justice Frameworks

Quick-reference for the justice-analyst.

## Rawlsian Justice (A Theory of Justice, 1971)

**Core idea**: Justice is what rational people would choose behind a "veil of ignorance" — not knowing their position in society.

**Three principles (lexical order)**:
1. Equal basic liberties for all
2. Fair equality of opportunity
3. Difference principle: inequalities permitted only if they benefit the least advantaged

**Strengths**: Provides a procedural test (veil of ignorance) rather than just asserting principles. Balances liberty and equality.
**Weaknesses**: Assumes risk-averse rational agents. Doesn't account for desert or effort. Demands extensive state apparatus.

## Libertarian Justice (Nozick, Anarchy, State, and Utopia, 1974)

**Core idea**: Justice is about respecting individual rights, especially property rights acquired through legitimate processes.

**Three principles**:
1. Just acquisition (legitimately coming to own unowned things)
2. Just transfer (voluntary exchange preserves justice)
3. Rectification (correcting past injustices in acquisition or transfer)

**Strengths**: Respects individual autonomy. Clear, process-based criteria. Limits state power.
**Weaknesses**: Ignores starting conditions (inherited advantage). Doesn't address market failures. "Just acquisition" is historically rare.

## Utilitarian Justice (Bentham, Mill, Singer)

**Core idea**: The just arrangement is the one that maximizes total well-being.

**Variants**: Total utilitarianism (maximize sum), average utilitarianism (maximize per-capita), prioritarianism (extra weight to worse-off).

**Strengths**: Consequentialist clarity. Inclusive (all sentient beings count). Adaptable to new information.
**Weaknesses**: Can justify sacrificing minorities for majorities. Measurement of utility is contested. Ignores distribution.

## Communitarian Justice (Sandel, Walzer, MacIntyre)

**Core idea**: Justice depends on shared social meanings and community values, not abstract universal principles.

**Key claims**: Different goods belong to different "spheres" (Walzer). Identity is constituted by community. Universal frameworks ignore context.

**Strengths**: Respects cultural diversity. Acknowledges that abstract principles need interpretation. Grounds justice in lived experience.
**Weaknesses**: Can entrench local prejudice. Difficulty with cross-cultural justice. Who speaks for "the community"?

## Capabilities Approach (Sen, Nussbaum)

**Core idea**: Justice requires ensuring everyone has real freedoms (capabilities) to live a flourishing life.

**Nussbaum's 10 central capabilities**: Life, bodily health, bodily integrity, senses/imagination/thought, emotions, practical reason, affiliation, other species, play, control over environment.

**Strengths**: Focuses on what people can actually do and be. Goes beyond income. Applicable cross-culturally. Extends to non-human entities.
**Weaknesses**: List of capabilities is debated. Threshold level is contested. Resource-intensive to implement.
