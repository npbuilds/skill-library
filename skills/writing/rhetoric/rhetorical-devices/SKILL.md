# Rhetorical Devices — The Workhorses

Hundreds of named rhetorical devices exist. Most are curiosities. This skill covers only the ones that working prose writers actually deploy — the devices that earn their place through use, not through their entry in a classical taxonomy.

## Key Concepts

### Repetition Devices (The Power Category)

**Anaphora** — Repetition at the beginning of successive clauses or sentences. Creates momentum, emphasis, and incantatory power. This is probably the single most useful rhetorical device for prose — it feels natural, builds rhythm, and is hard to overdo. Baldwin, King, and Obama use it constantly.

> *We shall fight on the beaches, we shall fight on the landing grounds, we shall fight in the fields...*

**Epistrophe** — Repetition at the end of successive clauses. Creates a hammering, emphatic closing effect.

> *...of the people, by the people, for the people.*

**Anadiplosis** — Repeating the last word of one clause as the first of the next. Creates chain-link logic — each idea leads inevitably to the next.

> *Suffering breeds character; character breeds faith; faith will not disappoint.*

These three are the repetition workhorses. Use anaphora for building momentum, epistrophe for landing a conclusion, anadiplosis for logical chains.

### Structural Devices

**Tricolon** — Three parallel phrases. The "rule of three" is deeply embedded in Western prose because three elements create a sense of completeness — two feels incomplete, four feels like a list.

> *Veni, vidi, vici.* (I came, I saw, I conquered.)

The third element often carries the most weight: the first two establish the pattern, the third delivers the punch or the twist.

**Antithesis** — Juxtaposing contrasting ideas in balanced structures. Creates memorable, pithy formulations that lodge in the reader's mind.

> *It was the best of times; it was the worst of times.*

Antithesis makes complex ideas feel graspable by presenting them as binary contrasts. Powerful for argument; dangerous when it oversimplifies.

**Chiasmus** — Reversing the order of elements in parallel phrases (AB:BA pattern). Creates an elegant, closed-loop feeling.

> *Ask not what your country can do for you — ask what you can do for your country.*

Chiasmus is powerful but can feel showy if overused. Deploy for the line you most want remembered.

### Rhythm and Pace Devices

**Asyndeton** — Omitting conjunctions between items in a series. Speeds up prose, creates urgency, eliminates hierarchy among items.

> *I came, I saw, I conquered.* (no "and")

**Polysyndeton** — Deliberately adding extra conjunctions. Slows prose down, creates a piling-up effect, conveys abundance or overwhelm.

> *...and the rain and the wind and the dark and the cold...*

These two are opposites and create opposite effects. Asyndeton accelerates; polysyndeton accumulates. Choose based on whether you want the reader to feel urgency or weight.

### Deployment Principles

1. **One device per passage.** Stacking multiple devices in the same paragraph creates a rhetorical traffic jam. Let each device breathe.
2. **Earn the device.** Rhetorical devices call attention to themselves. Use them at moments that deserve attention — climactic points, key arguments, emotional peaks. A tricolon about grocery shopping is wasted power.
3. **The device should be invisible on first read and visible on second.** If the reader notices the technique before the content, the device is fighting the prose instead of serving it.
4. **Repetition devices need at least three instances.** Two is a coincidence; three is a pattern. For anaphora and epistrophe, three instances is minimum; five to seven for full incantatory effect.

## When This Applies

- The user wants a specific line or passage to be more emphatic or memorable
- The user asks about specific devices by name (anaphora, parallelism, etc.)
- A passage needs rhetorical punch for a climactic moment
- The user is writing persuasive prose and wants to know which devices to deploy
- A passage overuses devices and needs restraint (the deployment principles apply)
