# Style DNA — The Genome of Prose

Every author's voice is a position in a 14-dimensional space. No two authors occupy the same point. The power of this model is that the dimensions are *orthogonal* — you can set each independently, which means you can combine Hobb's psychic distance with Asimov's velocity with VanderMeer's epistemic uncertainty. The result is a voice that no existing author occupies — something genuinely new.

## The 14 Dimensions

### 1. Sentence Rhythm Variance

**Metronomic** ◄─────────────────► **Syncopated**

The *music* of the prose. Not average sentence length, but the *pattern of variation* — does the writer hold a steady beat or alternate wildly between staccato and cascade?

- **Metronomic**: Asimov, Sanderson — clean, functional, consistent mid-length sentences. The prose stays out of the way.
- **Syncopated**: McCarthy, Melville — extreme variation. Three-word detonations followed by sixty-word waves. The rhythm itself carries emotional weight.
- **Mid**: Martin, Herbert, Hobb — variation exists but serves the content rather than being a signature.

**The craft lever**: Rhythm is the first thing a reader "hears." It operates below conscious attention, creating mood before meaning arrives.

### 2. Psychic Distance

**Telescopic** ◄─────────────────► **Microscopic**

Gardner's spectrum: how deep inside the character's consciousness is the narration? Every author has a *default focal distance* they return to, even if they move up and down.

- **Telescopic**: Tolkien (chronicler distance), Asimov (observing from orbit), Vonnegut (ironic remove)
- **Microscopic**: Hobb (lodged inside the nervous system), VanderMeer (close but sensorially alien)
- **Unique positions**: Herbert occupies psychic closeness + emotional distance (you're inside the character's analysis, not their feelings). McCarthy is mid-far but with somatic intensity (close to the body, far from the mind).

**The craft lever**: Controls whether the reader *thinks about* or *lives as* the character.

### 3. Information Density

**Sparse** ◄─────────────────► **Dense**

How much meaning is packed per sentence. Distinct from sentence length — a long sentence can be sparse (cumulative, repetitive) and a short sentence can be dense (compressed, allusive).

- **Sparse**: Camus (single observations, meaning in the gaps), McCarthy (plain statements, the landscape does the work)
- **Dense**: Melville (factual claim + metaphor + philosophy + allusion in one sentence), Herbert (aphorisms loaded with systemic implication)
- **Deceptively mid**: Vonnegut appears sparse but compresses enormous narrative time and ironic double meanings into simple structures.

**The craft lever**: Density controls the pace of *comprehension*, not the pace of *events*.

### 4. Ornamentation

**Austere** ◄─────────────────► **Baroque**

The surface texture — how decorated the language is. Distinct from density (ornamental prose can be low-density; dense prose can be plain).

- **Austere**: Camus (figurative language is rare, devastating when it appears), Vonnegut (deliberately stripped), Asimov (invisible), Butler (plain but never weak)
- **Baroque**: VanderMeer (metaphors layering on metaphors), Melville (elaborate rhetorical figures), Mieville (neologism-studded), Nabokov (every sentence a small performance), Morrison (incantatory, rhythmic)
- **Unique position**: McCarthy is austere in *diction* (Anglo-Saxon words, plain vocabulary) but baroque in *structure* (biblical parallelism, elaborate cadences). This makes him uniquely recognizable.

**The craft lever**: What most people mean when they say prose is "beautiful" or "functional."

### 5. Narrative Velocity

**Dilated** ◄─────────────────► **Compressed**

The ratio of story time to page time. Some authors spend pages on minutes; others cover years in paragraphs.

- **Dilated**: Martin (feasts and politics unfold in real time), Hobb (time expands around emotional pain), Morrison (memory loops stretch moments into meditations)
- **Compressed**: Asimov (decades between paragraphs), Vonnegut (entire lifetimes in a page), Borges (universes in five pages), Gibson (jump-cut cinematics)
- **Variable**: McCarthy, Melville — both dilate and compress dramatically within the same work.

**The craft lever**: Controls what the reader *experiences* versus what they are *told about*.

### 6. Lexical Register

**Demotic** ◄─────────────────► **Elevated**

The social altitude of the vocabulary and grammar. Pub talk or palace speech.

- **Demotic**: Vonnegut ("So it goes"), Chandler (wisecracking street-level), Palahniuk (instructional-manual register), Pratchett (chatty pub omniscience)
- **Elevated**: Tolkien (saga register), Melville (19th-century oratory), McCarthy (biblical), Herbert (aphoristic formality), Morrison (prophetic)
- **Mid/neutral**: Sanderson, Hobb, Martin, Le Guin — accessible without being aggressively casual.
- **Strange**: Mieville (elevated but through invented vocabulary), VanderMeer (elevated but through biological vocabulary)

**The craft lever**: Register is the single biggest signal of who the prose thinks it's *for*.

### 7. Authorial Presence

**Invisible** ◄─────────────────► **Performative**

Is the narrator a clear window or a personality? Distinct from psychic distance — a narrator can be very close to a character while being invisible (Hobb), or far from characters while being highly visible (Vonnegut).

- **Invisible**: Sanderson, Asimov, Hobb, Butler — the prose is a delivery system. You forget someone is telling you this.
- **Performative**: Vonnegut (addresses the reader, makes jokes, breaks the fourth wall), Melville (Ishmael is a personality you're aware of even during whale taxonomy), Pratchett (footnoted, digressive, opinionated), Nabokov (the narrator's verbal dexterity is their character)
- **Mid/tonal**: Tolkien (the chronicler is felt but not foregrounded), Chandler (the voice IS the narrator), Morrison (the communal voice emerges and retreats)

**The craft lever**: Controls whether the reader is *inside a story* or *being told a story by someone*.

### 8. Sensory Bandwidth

**Conceptual** ◄─────────────────► **Somatic**

What does the prose pay attention to? Ideas and events, or the physical texture of the world?

- **Conceptual**: Herbert (sensation filtered through analysis), Asimov (ideas are the texture), Borges (the mind is the only landscape)
- **Somatic**: VanderMeer (rot, growth, spores on skin), McCarthy (landscape with geological precision), Morrison (the body remembers what the mind suppresses), Gibson (surfaces, materials, brand textures)
- **Selective**: Hobb (emotional-physical — heartbreak felt in the chest), Martin (food, blood, weather as political atmosphere), Butler (the body under power)

**The craft lever**: Determines whether the reader's engagement is primarily intellectual or experiential.

### 9. Emotional Explicitness

**Submerged** ◄─────────────────► **Surfaced**

How does the text handle feelings? Named and explored, or implied through image and omission?

- **Submerged**: Camus (famously opaque), McCarthy (emotion exists in landscape, not in characters), Chandler (toughness performs vulnerability), Gibson (cool surfaces mask feeling)
- **Surfaced**: Hobb (pain is named, examined, revisited), Sanderson (characters articulate what they feel), Morrison (emotion made communal and explicit)
- **Analytical/mid**: Herbert (characters analyze their own emotions clinically), Martin (emotions rendered through physical correlates and internal contradiction)
- **Ironic**: Vonnegut (emotion is present but always at an ironic remove — you feel it because he won't let himself)

**The craft lever**: Controls reader effort. Submerged emotion asks more but can hit harder.

### 10. World-to-Story Ratio

**Story-dominant** ◄─────────────────► **World-dominant**

What does the text treat as primary? Does the world exist to serve narrative, or does narrative exist to explore the world?

- **Story-dominant**: Asimov (worldbuilding is functional), Camus (the world is a projection of consciousness), Vonnegut (the world is a setup for the punchline), Chandler (the city is a mirror for the detective), Palahniuk (the world is a symptom)
- **World-dominant**: Tolkien (the narrative is a path through a world that vastly exceeds it), VanderMeer (the ecosystem is the point), Melville (the whale is the point), Mieville (the city is the point)
- **Balanced**: Herbert (world revealed through story demands), Martin (story and world are co-equal), Le Guin (anthropological — the culture IS the story)

**The craft lever**: Determines what the reader comes away remembering — the story or the place.

### 11. Syntactic Complexity

**Paratactic** ◄─────────────────► **Hypotactic**

Grammar as architecture. Simple clauses joined by "and" and juxtaposition, or elaborate subordination with nested qualifications?

- **Paratactic**: Camus, Vonnegut, McCarthy (long paratactic chains — "and" stringing simple clauses), Palahniuk (instructional flatness), Chandler (the tough sentence)
- **Hypotactic**: Melville (clauses nesting inside clauses), Herbert (qualification modifying qualification), Morrison (subordination as rhythm), Nabokov (elaborate syntax as characterization)
- **Mid**: Martin, Hobb, Sanderson, Le Guin — functional syntax that doesn't call attention to itself.

**The craft lever**: Syntax is the skeleton. Parataxis implies equality between things (philosophical flatness). Hypotaxis implies hierarchy (one thing causes/contains/qualifies another).

### 12. Tonal Stability

**Monotonal** ◄─────────────────► **Polytonal**

Does the prose hold one emotional register, or shift rapidly between tones?

- **Monotonal**: McCarthy (relentlessly grave), Herbert (serious-analytical), Tolkien (elevated throughout), VanderMeer (sustained dread), Jackson (sustained domestic unease), Camus (flat affect)
- **Polytonal**: Vonnegut (comedy and devastation in one breath), Martin (bawdy humor to genuine pathos to political menace), Pratchett (jokes that become philosophy), Nabokov (beauty that becomes sinister)
- **Narrow range**: Hobb (dark emotional spectrum — pain to hope to pain, but rarely comic)

**The craft lever**: Monotonal prose lets the reader settle into a mood. Polytonal prose keeps them off-balance. The most powerful single move: a tonal shift in monotonal prose (rare, therefore devastating).

### 13. Dialogic Texture

**Transparent** ◄─────────────────► **Idiolectal**

Do all characters sound alike (dialogue as information exchange), or does each character have a distinct voice?

- **Transparent**: Asimov, Sanderson — characters speak to advance plot or ideas. Speech patterns are relatively interchangeable.
- **Idiolectal**: Martin (class, region, education encoded in speech), Chandler (the wisecrack IS the character), Pratchett (each character has a comic voice), Morrison (speech carries cultural history)
- **Register-idiolectal**: Tolkien (elves, hobbits, Rohan speak in different formality registers, not naturalistic speech)

**The craft lever**: Dialogue texture determines whether conversations feel like *scenes* or *information exchange*.

### 14. Epistemic Stance

**Certain** ◄─────────────────► **Uncertain**

Does the text know what's real? Is reality stable, knowable, and logically consistent — or slippery, unreliable, and contested?

- **Certain**: Sanderson (the magic has rules, the narrator can be trusted), Asimov (reality is a solvable puzzle), Tolkien (mythic certainty — good and evil exist)
- **Uncertain**: VanderMeer (what is happening? is the narrator reliable?), Borges (reality is a construction), Jackson (something is wrong but you can't name it), Nabokov (the narrator's beauty is a lie)
- **Meaning-uncertain**: Camus (events are clear, significance is not), Vonnegut (facts are known, but they don't help)
- **Systematically certain**: Herbert (the system is known, but so complex that certainty becomes its own trap)

**The craft lever**: Controls the reader's fundamental relationship to the text — solving a puzzle, or being dissolved by one.
