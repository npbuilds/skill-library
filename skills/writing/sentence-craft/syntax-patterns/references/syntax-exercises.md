# Syntax Exercises — Pattern Practice

Exercises organized by sentence pattern. Use for learning, warm-up, or diagnosing which patterns a writer defaults to vs. avoids.

## Exercise 1: Cumulative Sentence Building (Christensen Method)

Start with a base clause. Add free modifiers at successive levels of generality, each one zooming in or adding detail.

**Template:**
```
Level 1 (base clause): [Subject] [verb] [object/complement].
Level 2 (develops base): [modifier that adds detail to the base clause],
Level 3 (develops Level 2): [modifier that develops the Level 2 detail].
```

**Example build:**
```
Level 1: The dog sat on the porch.
Level 2: The dog sat on the porch, ears flat against its skull,
Level 3: the left one torn from some old fight nobody remembered.
```

**Practice**: Take any simple sentence from your draft. Add at least two levels of free modifiers. The result should feel textured, not padded — every modifier should add something the reader can see or feel.

**Diagnostic use**: If a writer cannot add free modifiers without padding, their base clauses may be too specific already (good sign) or they may struggle with the visual/sensory imagination that fuels cumulative writing.

## Exercise 2: Periodic Sentence Conversion

Take a cumulative (right-branching) sentence and convert it to periodic (left-branching) by moving the main clause to the end.

**Before (cumulative):**
> She finally understood the scope of the problem, after reading the report three times, after calling the team lead who refused to answer, after discovering the error buried in line 4,096.

**After (periodic):**
> After reading the report three times, after calling the team lead who refused to answer, after discovering the error buried in line 4,096, she finally understood the scope of the problem.

**What changes**: The periodic version creates suspense — the reader accumulates the conditions before receiving the resolution. The cumulative version creates a cascade — the reader gets the point first, then the elaboration.

**Practice**: Convert three cumulative sentences from your draft to periodic. Read both versions aloud. Which creates more emphasis? Which reads more naturally? Most will sound better cumulative — keep the periodic version only when emphasis is the goal.

## Exercise 3: Parallelism Drills

Build sentences using parallel structure. Start with two elements, then extend to three (tricolon).

**Two-element parallelism:**
> The work was tedious but essential.
> She wrote with precision and without mercy.

**Three-element tricolon:**
> He came, he saw, he conquered.
> Government of the people, by the people, for the people.
> She was smart, she was stubborn, and she was right.

**Antithesis (parallel structure, opposing content):**
> It was the best of times; it was the worst of times.
> Not that I loved Caesar less, but that I loved Rome more.

**Practice**: Find three sentences in your draft that list or compare ideas. Restructure them using explicit parallelism. The goal is grammatical symmetry — same part of speech, same clause structure, same rhythm.

## Exercise 4: Fragment Deployment

Fragments work by contrast. Practice placing them for emphasis.

**Template:**
> [Complete sentence establishing context.] [Complete sentence developing it.] [Fragment delivering the punch.]

**Example:**
> She had been preparing for this moment for six years. She had rehearsed every possible scenario, anticipated every objection, memorized every data point. None of it mattered.

**Practice**: Find a passage in your draft where a point needs emphasis. Write the buildup in complete sentences. Then deliver the key idea as a fragment. Read it aloud — the fragment should land like a dropped weight.

**The test**: Remove the fragment and replace it with a complete sentence. If the passage loses force, the fragment is working. If it reads the same, the fragment was unnecessary.

## Exercise 5: The First-Word Rewrite

Take a paragraph from your draft. List the first word of every sentence:

```
The → He → She → The → It → He → The
Diagnosis: Heavy on articles and pronouns. All subject-first openers.
```

Rewrite the paragraph so that no two adjacent sentences begin with the same part of speech. Use these opener types:

- **Gerund phrase**: "Running through the data, she noticed..."
- **Subordinate clause**: "When the results came back, the team..."
- **Prepositional phrase**: "In the silence that followed, no one..."
- **Adverb**: "Slowly, the pattern became clear."
- **Participial phrase**: "Exhausted from the argument, he..."
- **Question**: "What had she expected?"
- **Fragment**: "Nothing."
- **Conjunction (for informal register)**: "And then it all made sense."

**Constraint**: Don't sacrifice clarity for variety. A clear subject-first sentence is always better than a contorted opener used only for the sake of variation.

## Exercise 6: The Four-Pattern Paragraph

Write a single paragraph that uses all four core patterns: one cumulative sentence, one periodic sentence, one balanced/parallel sentence, and one fragment. The paragraph should read naturally — the structural variety should be invisible to the reader.

This is the hardest exercise. If the paragraph sounds forced, one of the patterns is fighting the content. Revise until the structure serves the meaning rather than displaying itself.

## Diagnostic: Which Patterns Does This Writer Default To?

Take a 500-word sample. Classify each sentence:

| Pattern | Count | Percentage |
|---------|-------|-----------|
| Cumulative (right-branching) | | |
| Periodic (left-branching) | | |
| Balanced/Parallel | | |
| Fragment | | |
| Simple (base clause only, no modifiers) | | |

**Expected distribution in skilled prose** (per Christensen's research):
- Cumulative: ~60-75%
- Simple: ~10-20%
- Periodic: ~5-10%
- Balanced: ~5-10%
- Fragment: ~2-5%

If a writer's distribution is heavily skewed (90%+ one pattern), that's the primary syntax problem. The fix is not to abandon their default — it's to add the missing patterns at points of emphasis.
