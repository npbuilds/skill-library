# Character Interiority — Academic Foundations

## Dorrit Cohn's Taxonomy (*Transparent Minds*, 1978)

The foundational academic framework for thought representation in third-person fiction:

| Cohn's Term | Standard Term | Narrator Control | Character Voice |
|---|---|---|---|
| Psycho-narration | Indirect/reported thought | High | Low |
| Narrated monologue | Free indirect discourse | Shared | Shared |
| Quoted monologue | Direct thought | Low | High |

**Psycho-narration** — The narrator reports on mental state in the narrator's own language. Includes **psycho-analogy**: using metaphor to evoke mental states that resist direct articulation ("fear uncurled itself in his stomach"). Good for conveying feelings that haven't reached verbal articulation — what Cohn calls "feelings without words."

**Narrated monologue** (Free indirect discourse) — Character thought transposed into narrative mode: third person, past tense, but using the character's idiom. Diagnostic test: can you convert to first person present tense? If yes, it's FID.

**Quoted monologue** (Direct thought) — Verbatim rendering of thought, typically italicized or tagged.

**Cohn's key insight**: These modes work best in ascending order — psycho-narration → narrated monologue → quoted monologue — mirroring how cognition operates from wordless emotion to articulated thought.

## FID Practitioners: Extended Notes

**Austen** — *Pride and Prejudice* filters through no fewer than nineteen centers of consciousness. FID creates dual voicing: the reader sees both the character's self-perception and the narrator's subtle critique simultaneously.

**Flaubert** — *Madame Bovary* (1856) is the landmark. FID immerses readers in Emma's psychological landscape while maintaining clinical distance. The narrator never overtly judges; the gap between Emma's romantic vocabulary and her actual situation produces devastating irony.

**Woolf** — Expanded FID into a vehicle for pre-verbal consciousness. In *Mrs Dalloway*, FID facilitates seamless shifts between characters' interiors, often within a single sentence. Creates collective interiority.

## Genre Fiction Interiority

**Robin Hobb** — Bifurcated first-person narrator: the Experiencing Self (young, reactive) and the Narrating Self (older, analytical). Creates dramatic irony. Signature technique: **emotional equity through mundane accumulation** — extensive passages on daily rhythms (stable chores, lessons) so that when trauma arrives, it devastates because the reader has lived through the calm.

**N.K. Jemisin** — Second person in *Broken Earth* as dissociative consciousness. The "you" is revealed to be a first-person narrator within the story speaking to Essun. Grammar becomes a vehicle for psychological content: the pronoun choice materializes dissociation and entangled identity.

**Le Guin** — Consciousness as culturally situated. In *The Left Hand of Darkness*, interiority is never purely psychological — it is ethnographic, showing how thought is shaped by the thinker's cultural categories. Multi-perspectival structure (personal account + field reports + myths) mirrors how consciousness is always embedded in frameworks.

## Common Mistakes (Extended)

1. **Stock physical reactions**: Hearts pounding, stomachs dropping, fists clenching. Universal reactions fail to illuminate specific character. Fix: idiosyncratic, character-specific responses.
2. **Over-narrating**: Explaining everything passing through a character's mind. Good interiority moves between layers selectively (surface observation → judgment → memory → deep emotion).
3. **Interiority without stakes**: Pages of reflection disconnected from dramatic pressure. Interiority is most powerful under tension, choice, or consequence.
4. **Filter words in deep POV**: "She saw," "he noticed," "she felt" — these insert narrator distance. Deep POV eliminates them, creating first person wearing a third-person mask.
