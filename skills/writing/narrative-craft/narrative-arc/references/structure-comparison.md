# Structure Frameworks: Side-by-Side Comparison

Five frameworks applied to the same simple story: **A character in a dead-end job discovers they can paint, struggles to make it work, and ultimately chooses art over security.**

## Three-Act Structure

| Beat | % | Story Moment |
|------|---|-------------|
| Setup / Status Quo | 0-10% | Mara works data entry. Monotonous. She doodles in meetings. |
| Inciting Incident | 10% | She stumbles into a community art class on a whim. |
| First Plot Point | 25% | Her painting wins a local exhibition. A gallery owner wants more. She can't go back to just doodling. |
| First Pinch Point | 37% | Her boss gives her an ultimatum: the late nights are affecting her work. |
| Midpoint | 50% | She sells her first painting — but the buyer wants commercial work, not what she loves. Mirror moment: is she trading one soul-crushing job for another? |
| Second Pinch Point | 62% | The gallery drops her after she refuses the commercial direction. |
| Crisis / All Is Lost | 75% | No gallery, no savings, job on the line. Her partner says she's being irresponsible. |
| Climax | 85% | She quits the job and paints what she actually wants. |
| Resolution | 90-100% | Smaller gallery, less money, but the work is hers. |

## Kishotenketsu

| Movement | Story Moment |
|----------|-------------|
| **Ki** (Introduction) | Mara works data entry. She doodles in meetings. Her life is orderly, quiet, small. |
| **Sho** (Development) | She starts painting at home. The paintings fill her apartment. She gives one to a friend, who hangs it in a cafe. People notice. |
| **Ten** (Twist/Contrast) | A child in the cafe asks Mara to paint her dog. Mara realizes she's been painting *for* something — approval, a gallery, a career — when the act itself was always the point. |
| **Ketsu** (Reconciliation) | Mara keeps her job. She keeps painting. The paintings are everywhere — cafes, friends' walls, her cubicle. Nothing changed externally. Everything changed internally. |

**Note**: No antagonist. No crisis. The "ten" is a shift in understanding, not a conflict. The story works through contrast between striving and being.

## Truby's Organic Structure (Key Steps)

| Step | Story Moment |
|------|-------------|
| **Weakness/Need** | Mara believes her worth comes from productivity and output. She needs to learn that creation has value independent of product. |
| **Desire** | She wants to become a successful artist (external desire driven by the weakness). |
| **Opponent** | The gallery owner who wants commercial work; her own internalized productivity metrics. |
| **Plan** | Paint what the gallery wants, build a portfolio, transition out of data entry. |
| **Battle** | The gallery drops her. She faces the gap between what she wants (success) and what she needs (authentic creation). |
| **Self-Revelation** | She realizes she was applying her data-entry mindset — optimize, produce, deliver — to art. The weakness was never about the job; it was about how she values herself. |
| **New Equilibrium** | She paints for its own sake. The career question becomes secondary. |

**Note**: The story is driven by Mara's internal weakness, not by plot events. The same events could produce a different story if Mara's weakness were different.

## Lisa Cron's Misbelief Model

| Element | Story Moment |
|---------|-------------|
| **Misbelief** | "If I'm not producing something useful, I'm wasting time." |
| **Ghost (Origin)** | Her father lost his business when she was twelve. The family mantra became: be practical, be productive, don't waste time on things that don't pay. |
| **Want** (driven by misbelief) | To turn painting into a career — to make it "useful." |
| **Need** (the truth) | To create without needing the creation to justify itself. |
| **Story as confrontation** | Every plot event forces Mara to choose between the misbelief (make art productive) and the truth (art has value without productivity). |
| **Climax** | She paints something with no commercial value, purely because she wants to. The misbelief breaks. |

**Note**: The external plot is identical to three-act. The difference is that Cron's model makes the *internal* journey primary — the plot exists to force the internal confrontation.

## Coyne's Five Commandments (Applied at Global Level)

| Commandment | Story Moment |
|-------------|-------------|
| **Inciting Incident** | Mara discovers she can paint and people respond to her work. |
| **Progressive Complication / Turning Point** | The gallery wants commercial work. Her job is threatened. Her partner objects. Each complication raises the stakes. |
| **Crisis (Dilemma)** | Best bad choice: Keep the job and paint on weekends (safe but soul-crushing) OR quit and paint full-time (authentic but financially terrifying). |
| **Climax** | She quits and paints what she wants. |
| **Resolution** | Smaller life, authentic work. |

**Note**: The Five Commandments are fractal — this same structure applies to every individual scene within the story. The global-level application shows the overall shape; scene-level application would show each scene's internal turning point.

## Key Differences

| Dimension | Three-Act | Kishotenketsu | Truby | Cron | Coyne |
|-----------|----------|---------------|-------|------|-------|
| **Driver** | Plot events | Perspective shift | Character weakness | Character misbelief | Dilemma/choice |
| **Requires conflict?** | Yes | No | Yes (internal) | Yes (internal) | Yes (dilemma) |
| **Ending type** | Resolution of external problem | Reconciliation of perspectives | Self-revelation | Misbelief broken | New equilibrium |
| **Best for** | Plot-driven stories | Contemplative/observational | Character-driven stories | Psychologically deep stories | Diagnosing broken scenes |
| **Weakness** | Can feel mechanical | Less suited to long-form | Complex to learn | Can undervalue external plot | Can over-systematize |
