# Narrative Arc — The Shape of Story

Every story has a shape — a pattern of rising and falling tension, of complication and resolution, of the reader's expectations being set and then fulfilled or subverted. Narrative arc is the study of those shapes. Multiple frameworks exist for describing and constructing them, and they genuinely disagree with each other. This skill presents them as lenses with different strengths rather than competing truths.

## Key Concepts

### Framework 1: Three-Act Structure (Detailed)

The most widely taught framework, far more detailed than "beginning, middle, end":

- **Act One (~25%)**: Establishes the status quo. Introduces the protagonist's world. Builds to the **First Plot Point** — the disruption that locks the protagonist into the story. The protagonist cannot go back to normal after this moment.
- **Act Two (~50%)**: The longest and most complex. Contains:
  - **First Pinch Point** (~37%) — the antagonistic force demonstrates its power
  - **Midpoint** (~50%) — a major reversal. K.M. Weiland calls this the "mirror moment" where the protagonist shifts from reaction to action
  - **Second Pinch Point** (~62%) — another reminder of what's at stake
  - **Crisis / All Is Lost** (~75%) — the dark moment where the protagonist's approach fails
- **Act Three (~25%)**: The protagonist acts on everything learned, building to the **Climax** and **Resolution**.

**Strengths**: Clear, widely understood, provides concrete percentage-based structural checkpoints. Works well for plot-driven stories.
**Limitations**: Can feel mechanical if applied rigidly. Truby argues it's "superimposed on the story" rather than growing from it.

### Framework 2: Kishotenketsu (Four-Act, No Conflict Required)

A structure from Chinese, Japanese, Korean, and Vietnamese narrative traditions:

- **Ki** (Introduction): Establish the world and characters
- **Sho** (Development): Deepen without introducing conflict
- **Ten** (Twist): An unexpected shift in perspective — *contrast*, not necessarily opposition
- **Ketsu** (Reconciliation): Harmonize the original with the new perspective

The crucial insight: conflict is not structurally required. The "ten" turn creates meaning through juxtaposition and surprise. This is significant for writers working outside Western dramatic traditions or writing stories about connection, observation, or understanding rather than opposition.

**Strengths**: Opens narrative possibilities beyond conflict-driven stories. Elegant structure for short fiction, vignettes, and contemplative writing.
**Limitations**: Less naturally suited to long-form, plot-heavy narratives.

### Framework 3: Truby's Organic Structure

John Truby (*The Anatomy of Story*) explicitly rejects three-act structure as mechanical. His 22 steps grow organically from character:

The core engine: the protagonist has a **great weakness** and a **deep need** they don't recognize. The story is the process of that need being addressed — or not.

Key concepts: the "moral argument" embedded in structure, the "desire line" (what the protagonist wants) vs. the "need" (what they actually require), the web of character relationships as structural architecture rather than plot points.

**Strengths**: Produces deeply character-driven stories. Forces the writer to understand *why* the story exists.
**Limitations**: Complex to learn. Can lead to over-internalized stories that lack external drive.

### Framework 4: Lisa Cron's Neuroscience Approach

Cron (*Wired for Story*, *Story Genius*) argues from cognitive science that readers' brains track a protagonist's *internal* struggle, not external plot events. Plot is merely the vehicle that forces internal change.

Her method: build from the protagonist's **misbelief** outward. What does the character believe about the world that is wrong? What formative event created that belief? How does the story force a confrontation with truth?

**Strengths**: Ensures every story has emotional and psychological depth. Aligns with how readers actually engage.
**Limitations**: Can undervalue external plot and world-building.

### Framework 5: Coyne's Five Commandments

Shawn Coyne's Story Grid identifies five elements that operate at every structural level — beat, scene, sequence, act, global story:

1. **Inciting Incident** — the disruption
2. **Progressive Complication / Turning Point** — things get harder
3. **Crisis** — a dilemma (the character must choose)
4. **Climax** — the action taken
5. **Resolution** — the new state

**Strengths**: Fractal — the same structure applies at every scale. Useful for diagnosing broken scenes as well as broken novels.
**Limitations**: Can over-systematize. Not all great scenes fit neatly into five boxes.

### Non-Linear Structure

Techniques for breaking chronology: flashbacks, flashforwards, multi-timeline braids, frame narratives, reverse chronology.

The craft requirement: every disruption of chronology must serve a purpose (suspense, thematic resonance, character revelation). Transition cues — recurring motifs, objects, locations — help readers navigate temporal shifts.

**When non-linear works**: When juxtaposing time periods creates meaning that linear telling would miss. When the mystery of chronological order is itself part of the experience.
**When it doesn't**: When it's used for novelty without structural purpose.

## When This Applies

- The user asks about plot, story structure, or "how to organize this narrative"
- A story feels shapeless or saggy — arc is likely the problem
- The user is planning a piece and wants structural guidance
- The user asks about specific frameworks (three-act, hero's journey, etc.)
- A story has momentum problems at the act level (not the scene level — that's pacing)
