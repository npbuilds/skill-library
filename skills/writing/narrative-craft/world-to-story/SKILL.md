# World-to-Story — The Translator

A world-bible is not a story. A culture document is not a scene. A magic system is not a plot. The gap between worldbuilding and narrative is where many projects stall — the world is complete, fascinating, internally consistent, and utterly inert on the page because no one has translated it into experience.

This skill provides the methodology for that translation: taking the outputs of worldbuilding (axioms, cultures, systems, histories) and converting them into the inputs of storytelling (scenes, conflicts, characters, revelations).

## The Iceberg Methodology

Hemingway's principle applied to worldbuilding: the reader should see 10-20% of the world on the page. The remaining 80-90% lives beneath the surface, giving the visible portion weight and credibility.

### What Shows (The Tip)

| Shows | How It Shows | Example |
|-------|-------------|---------|
| Rules the characters live by | Through character behavior, not exposition | Characters instinctively bow when passing a shrine — the reader infers the religion is pervasive |
| Scarcity and its consequences | Through what characters do to acquire what they need | The character counts coins before eating — the reader infers economic pressure |
| Cultural values | Through what characters notice, ignore, praise, or condemn | A character who doesn't flinch at violence but is horrified by waste — values encoded in reaction |
| History's weight | Through ruins, idioms, taboos, inherited behaviors | "The old quarter" is avoided at night — the reader senses history without a lecture |

### What Stays Below (The Mass)

| Stays Below | Why | When It Surfaces |
|-------------|-----|-----------------|
| Complete magic system rules | Characters don't explain their physics | When a rule is *broken* or a character encounters something they can't do |
| Full historical timeline | Characters live in the present | When the past directly affects present action |
| Cultural design documents | Characters don't anthropologize their own culture | When cultures clash and differences become visible |
| Economic modeling | Characters don't narrate macroeconomics | When economic pressure creates personal crisis |

### The Test
For every worldbuilding fact you want to include: **Does a character in this scene need to know this right now?** If yes, it earns its place. If it's context the reader might find interesting but no character is currently affected by it, it stays below the surface.

## Dramatizing Systems

The core translation problem: worldbuilding produces *systems* (magic, economy, politics). Stories need *experiences* (scenes, conflicts, moments). How to bridge the gap:

### System → Conflict
Every system creates winners and losers. Find the losers. Their experience IS the conflict.
- Magic system with a cost → character who can't afford the cost but needs the magic
- Caste system → character born in the wrong caste for their abilities
- Economic scarcity → character forced to choose between feeding their family and their principles

### System → Scene
A system becomes a scene when a character encounters a *specific instance* of the system's rules.
- Don't explain the magic system. Show a character using it and paying the price.
- Don't describe the political structure. Show a character navigating a political encounter.
- Don't narrate the history. Show a character discovering an artifact that contradicts what they were taught.

### System → Subtext
The deepest integration: the system operates through implication, not exposition.
- Characters who grew up in the system don't explain it — they enact it
- Cultural assumptions are visible in what characters DON'T question
- Economic reality shows in what characters can and can't afford, not in narrator commentary

## Revelation Architecture in Narrative

Working with the world-bible's revelation layers to control what the reader learns and when:

### The Three Channels

1. **Character experience**: The reader learns by watching a character encounter the world. The most immersive and the slowest.
2. **Character explanation**: One character explains to another (who genuinely doesn't know). Faster but risks "As You Know, Bob."
3. **Narrator exposition**: The narrator directly tells the reader. Fastest but breaks immersion if overused.

**Rule**: Use Channel 1 as default. Use Channel 2 when a newcomer character exists. Use Channel 3 only for essential context that no character would naturally explain.

### Revelation Pacing

Not all world-information arrives at once. The reader should discover the world gradually, in this order:
1. **Sensory reality first**: What does this world look, feel, smell like? (Ground the reader before explaining anything.)
2. **Social rules second**: How do people here treat each other? What's normal? (Establish behavioral norms through character interaction.)
3. **Systems third**: How does the magic/technology/economy work? (Reveal through use, not through lecture.)
4. **History last**: Why is the world this way? (History explains; it should arrive after the reader has questions the history answers.)

## Worldbuilding Exposition Techniques

| Technique | How It Works | When to Use |
|-----------|-------------|-------------|
| **The newcomer** | A character who genuinely doesn't understand asks questions | When the POV character is new to this world or society |
| **Conflict-driven revelation** | World rules are revealed because they create immediate problems | When the system actively threatens or constrains the character |
| **In-media-res immersion** | Drop the reader into the world with no explanation; let them orient through context clues | When the world is close enough to reality that readers can extrapolate |
| **Artifact discovery** | Characters find documents, inscriptions, or objects that reveal world history | When history matters but narration would be flat |
| **Cultural clash** | Two characters from different cultures clash, revealing both by contrast | When multiple cultures are in play |
| **The unreliable source** | A character explains the world incorrectly — the reader gradually discovers the truth | When the revelation architecture has layers to peel |

## Anti-Patterns

1. **The info-dump**: Three paragraphs of world history before anything happens. If you must exposition, weave it into a scene with active conflict.
2. **The travelogue**: Characters walk through the world noticing and describing things with no dramatic purpose. Description must serve character or conflict.
3. **"As You Know, Bob"**: Characters telling each other things they both already know for the reader's benefit.
4. **The lecture character**: A mentor, professor, or sage whose primary function is to explain the magic system to both the protagonist and the reader.
5. **World-dominant opening**: Starting with cosmology, geography, or history before introducing a character. The reader needs a person to care about before they'll care about a world.
6. **Parallel world and story**: The worldbuilding exists alongside the story but doesn't affect it — the character could have the same adventure anywhere. True integration means the story could ONLY happen in THIS world.

## Relationship to Other Skills

### From Worldbuilding
- **World-Bible**: The source material being translated. Contains axioms, revelation layers, faction web.
- **Character-Belief-Tracker**: Determines what each character knows at each point — essential for controlling revelation.
- **Lore-Writer**: Produces in-universe artifacts that can serve as revelation vehicles.
- **Extrapolation Engine**: Tests whether axioms have been fully developed — ensures the iceberg has real mass below the surface.

### From Writing
- **Scene Craft**: Scenes are the units of translation — each scene converts world-knowledge into experience.
- **Character Interiority**: The perception filter renders world-details through character consciousness rather than narrator exposition.
- **Micro-Tension**: World-details gain tension when characters have conflicting feelings about them.
- **Pacing**: Controls the rate of revelation — how quickly the reader learns about the world.
- **Concrete Detail**: The sensory specificity that makes world-details feel real rather than described.

## When This Applies

- Worldbuilding is done but the user doesn't know how to start the story
- Prose reads as travelogue or encyclopedia rather than narrative
- Characters explain their own world to each other unnaturally
- The world exists but doesn't affect the story — interchangeable setting
- The user asks "how do I show this world without info-dumping?"
- A draft has too much exposition in the first chapters
