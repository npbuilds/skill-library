# Micro-Tension — Maass Exercises & Extended Techniques

## Maass's Three Modes of Emotional Writing

- **Inner Mode**: Directly stating character emotions. Weakest alone — "her insides burned with anger" doesn't move the reader without craft around it.
- **Outer Mode**: Showing through behavior to provoke feelings matching the character's experience.
- **Other Mode**: A dialogue between reader and writer using combined elements (descriptions, dialogue, juxtaposition) to elicit emotional responses that aren't stated anywhere on the page. This is the most powerful mode.

## The "Effective Showing" Exercise

1. Find a moment where the protagonist is unsettled or facing a difficult choice.
2. List every hidden and unhidden emotion.
3. Write how the character acts these out — what symbolic actions emerge.
4. Delete all explicitly stated emotions, keeping only behavioral details.

The reader experiences the contradiction without it being named.

## The Clustering Exercise

Circle a nucleus word, then rapidly write associated words radiating outward. Work with:
- Word pairs: look/see, touch/feel
- Contraries: sweet/sorrow, cruel/kindness
- Polarities: power/helplessness, separation/union

This generates raw material for emotional contradiction.

## The Random Page Test

Maass's signature diagnostic: pick up any random page of your manuscript. Identify the driving emotion. Find its opposite. Layer both into the passage. If any single page can't hold a reader, the plot won't save it.

## Turning Phrases

Subvert reader expectations by finishing sentences in unexpected directions. Avoid clichéd emotional completions. Sentences should create anticipation before arrival, not state the conclusion and then explain it.

## Juxtaposing Rhythm with Meaning

Write sentences whose sonic rhythm conveys emotions opposite to the word-level meaning:
- A lilting, musical sentence describing something devastating
- A staccato, urgent rhythm describing contentment

The dissonance between form and content creates unease — a form of micro-tension at the sentence level that bridges to the `prose-rhythm` skill.

## Tension in Static Scenes — Extended Notes

**Robinson** (*Gilead*): Slow prose whose tension is existential — a dying man compressing a lifetime of meaning into a letter. Every sentence carries the weight of time running out, even when describing ordinary light on ordinary objects. The pared-down quality of the prose forces the reader to pause and reread.

**Cusk** (*Outline* trilogy): Characterization through negative space. Narrator Faye functions as a listening presence — novels consist of reported monologues from people she encounters. Tension comes from the reader's awareness that Faye uses other people's stories to avoid telling her own. The absence at the center creates momentum.

**Knausgaard** (*My Struggle*): Radical honesty applied to mundane experience. The gap between the banality of events and the intensity of consciousness applied to them. The discomfort of watching someone expose their inner life unfiltered.

**Shared technique**: All three create tension through **the quality of attention** — prose that demands active interpretation, pattern recognition, and emotional participation from the reader.

## Circular Worry Anti-Pattern

A character who repetitively frets about the same thing without emotional variation creates irritation, not tension. Worry needs to mutate — shifting between fear and anger, self-pity and determination, resignation and desperate hope. Each return to the worry should arrive from a different emotional angle.

## Melodrama vs. Micro-Tension

- **Melodrama**: Amplified singular emotions — screaming, throwing things, histrionics. Breaks believability.
- **Micro-tension**: Authentic conflicted emotions — quietly fighting back tears while congratulating a rival. The restraint IS the tension.
