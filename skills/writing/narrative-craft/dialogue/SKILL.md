# Dialogue — What Goes Unsaid

Good dialogue is not realistic speech transcribed. Real speech is full of ums, false starts, and meandering repetition. Good dialogue creates the *illusion* of natural speech while being compressed, purposeful, and loaded with subtext. Every line should advance plot, reveal character, or create tension — ideally all three simultaneously.

## Key Concepts

### Subtext: The Pinter/Mamet Principle

The most important concept in dialogue craft. Harold Pinter showed that characters use words to *obscure* their true intentions rather than reveal them. His trademark pauses and silences carry as much meaning as spoken lines — they represent the gap between what is said and what is felt.

David Mamet operates on the principle that "people only speak to get something from one another." Dialogue is always transactional — characters express themselves only as a means of obtaining what they want.

The practical rule: **characters rarely say what they mean.** They deflect, evade, attack, perform, charm, and manipulate. When a character says exactly what they think and feel, it should be a dramatic event — an act of unusual honesty or desperation.

### On-the-Nose Dialogue

The biggest dialogue flaw. On-the-nose dialogue has characters state exactly what they think, feel, or need:

> "I'm angry at you because you lied to me about the money, and now I feel betrayed."

No one talks like this. The subtext version:

> "Where did you say you were on Tuesday?"

The reader feels the anger and betrayal through what the character is doing — interrogating rather than accusing — without being told.

### Attribution: Said, Beats, and Silence

Three ways to identify the speaker, each with different effects:

**"Said" tags**: "Said" is functionally invisible to readers. It identifies the speaker without drawing attention to itself. Alternatives like "exclaimed," "retorted," "hissed" distract from the dialogue. This is widely accepted craft orthodoxy.

**Action beats**: "She set down her glass. 'I'm fine.'" Serve triple duty — identify speaker, ground the scene physically, and create subtext when actions contradict words.

**No attribution**: Works when two characters speak and their voices are distinct enough. Creates speed and intimacy.

Best practice: mix all three. Use the one that serves each specific line.

### Distinct Character Voices

Voice differentiation comes from *why* a character talks, not just *how*. Variables that create distinctiveness:

- **Sentence length and complexity** (reflecting education, temperament, emotional state)
- **Vocabulary range** (a mechanic and a professor use different words for the same concept)
- **Verbal tics and patterns** (a character who always answers questions with questions)
- **What they avoid saying** (silence and omission are voice markers too)
- **Rhythm and cadence** (short, punchy speech vs. flowing, subordinate-clause-heavy speech)

The test: cover the attribution tags. Can you tell who is speaking from the voice alone? If not, the voices are undifferentiated.

### The "As You Know, Bob" Problem

Exposition through dialogue — characters telling each other things they both already know for the reader's benefit. This is the second most common dialogue flaw after on-the-nose writing.

Fixes: have a character who genuinely doesn't know (a newcomer, a child, an outsider). Or deliver exposition through conflict — characters argue *about* the information rather than reciting it. Or cut the exposition entirely and trust the reader to infer from context.

## When This Applies

- Dialogue feels wooden, generic, or interchangeable between characters
- Characters state their feelings directly instead of showing them through behavior
- All characters sound like the author
- Dialogue is being used as an exposition vehicle
- The user asks about subtext, dialogue tags, or making characters sound distinct

## Cross-Domain Connections

- **Worldbuilding/faction-design**: Faction tensions surface through dialogue subtext. Characters from rival factions carry competing ideologies into conversation — the subtext IS the political dynamic. Load faction relationships before writing dialogue between politically positioned characters.
- **Worldbuilding/cultures-societies**: Cultural values constrain what characters can and can't say. A culture that values honor above all produces characters who speak in euphemisms about cowardice. Cultural communication norms shape dialogue conventions.
