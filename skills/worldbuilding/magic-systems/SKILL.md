# Magic Systems — The Rules of the Impossible

Every fantasy world has something that makes it not-Earth. Whether it's called magic, the Force, bending, alchemy, psionics, or something with no analog — it must be systematic if it's going to be satisfying. This skill provides the methodology for designing systems that feel real, create interesting constraints, and generate stories rather than solving them.

## The Spectrum: Hard to Soft

Fantasy systems exist on a spectrum from hard (fully rule-based, reader understands everything) to soft (mysterious, reader understands little). Most good systems sit somewhere in between.

| Type | Reader Understands | Used To | Narrative Effect | Examples |
|------|-------------------|---------|-----------------|----------|
| **Hard** | All rules, costs, limits | Solve problems, create puzzles | Intellectual satisfaction, fair stakes | Mistborn, Avatar, Full Metal Alchemist |
| **Hybrid** | Some rules, some mystery | Both solve and create wonder | Balanced engagement | Stormlight, Star Wars (Force), Jujutsu Kaisen |
| **Soft** | Very little | Create atmosphere, awe, dread | Emotional/tonal impact | Lord of the Rings, Southern Reach, Cowboy Bebop |

**Critical principle**: The harder your system, the more the reader must understand before the system solves problems. Soft systems should never solve problems — they create them, or create atmosphere. The worst mistake is a soft system that pulls deus ex machina solutions from its mystery.

## The Five Design Axes

Every fantasy system can be defined along five axes. You don't need to design all five in detail — soft systems intentionally leave axes ambiguous — but you should know where each axis sits.

### Axis 1: Source

Where does the power come from?

| Source Type | Implications | World Impact |
|-------------|-------------|--------------|
| **Ambient** (the world itself) | Anyone can potentially access it | Democratizing, environmental magic |
| **Inherited** (bloodline/species) | Power is genetic/racial | Aristocracies, breeding programs, chosen-one narratives |
| **Granted** (from entities) | Power has a patron | Religious structures, pacts, loyalty tests |
| **Extracted** (from materials) | Power is industrial | Mining, trade, monopolies, resource wars |
| **Learned** (from study/training) | Power correlates with education | Academies, master-apprentice systems, meritocratic hierarchies |
| **Emotional** (from feeling/will) | Power correlates with psychological state | Coming-of-age narratives, emotional stakes in combat |

### Axis 2: Cost

What does using it take? Cost is the most important design decision. It creates stakes, prevents deus ex machina, and generates interesting trade-offs.

**Hierarchy of interesting costs** (most to least narratively interesting):

1. **Something the character values** — memories, relationships, sanity, lifespan, identity. The cost forces genuine sacrifice.
2. **Something the world values** — a rare resource, environmental health, the balance of a system. The cost creates externalities.
3. **Physical toll** — exhaustion, pain, injury. Common and effective but can become rote.
4. **Time and effort** — training, preparation, ritual. Creates pacing constraints.
5. **Generic "energy"** — mana, stamina, MP. Functional but least interesting. The reader treats it as a meter, not a sacrifice.

**The best costs are asymmetric**: easy to pay small, devastating to pay large. This creates a natural drama curve where small uses are routine but big uses are agonizing decisions.

### Axis 3: Limits

What can it absolutely not do? Limits are more important than capabilities.

Good limits:
- **Conservation** — you can't create something from nothing (energy, matter, life)
- **Specificity** — the system does one category of thing well and nothing else
- **Reciprocity** — every action has an equal reaction or unintended consequence
- **Knowledge gates** — you can only do what you understand; power scales with comprehension
- **Emotional gates** — certain states of mind block or unlock abilities
- **Social limits** — some uses are possible but culturally forbidden, creating temptation

Bad limits (feel arbitrary):
- "It doesn't work on Tuesdays" — unless there's a reason rooted in the world's logic
- "You can only use it three times a day" — video game thinking, not world thinking
- Limits that exist only to prevent the plot from resolving too easily

### Axis 4: Access

Who can use the system? This axis determines the political landscape.

| Access Pattern | Political Implication |
|---------------|----------------------|
| Universal (anyone can learn) | Democratized power, guild systems, arms races |
| Rare (genetic/chosen) | Aristocracy, discrimination, eugenics, resentment |
| Economic (requires resources) | Plutocracy, class warfare, smuggling |
| Institutional (requires membership) | Church-like power structures, heresy, gatekeeping |
| Secret (hidden from most) | Underground networks, paranoia, witch hunts |
| Involuntary (can't be chosen) | Cursed/blessed narratives, fear of the empowered |

### Axis 5: Interaction with Physics

How does the system relate to the natural world?

- **Supplements physics** — magic does things physics can't, but respects physics otherwise. Thermodynamics still holds; magic adds new energy sources. This feels "harder."
- **Overrides physics** — magic can break physical laws within defined limits. More dramatic, less grounded.
- **Replaces physics** — the world doesn't run on our physics at all; the system IS the physics. Most alien, hardest to build consistently.

## The Propagation Test

The most important check for any fantasy system: does every design decision propagate through the world?

For each axis, ask:
- **Governance**: How does this affect who rules?
- **Economy**: How does this affect trade and wealth?
- **Military**: How does this affect warfare?
- **Religion**: How does this affect cosmology and worship?
- **Culture**: How does this affect daily life, art, values?
- **Ecology**: How does this affect the natural world?
- **History**: How has this shaped the past?

If the fantasy system doesn't affect at least four of these categories, it's decoration, not worldbuilding.

## Building a System

### Step 1: Start with Cost

Not source. Not capabilities. **Cost.** What does using this system take? The cost is where the drama lives. Design the cost first, then figure out what the cost buys you.

### Step 2: Define Three Limits

Three things the system absolutely cannot do. These limits will generate more stories than the capabilities ever will.

### Step 3: Choose Source and Access

Together these determine the political landscape. Who has power, why them, and what do the powerless think about it?

### Step 4: Build One Signature Ability

Don't catalog everything the system can do. Build one specific, detailed example of the system in action — a single spell, technique, or application — and develop it deeply. Show the cost, the limit, the source, the skill involved. One deep example teaches the reader (and you) more than a list of twenty shallow ones.

### Step 5: Propagation

Run the propagation test. Every category that isn't affected is a missed opportunity. You don't have to write all the implications immediately, but you should be able to sketch them.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Capabilities without costs.** If magic can heal, create food, build structures, and defend against all threats — why isn't everyone immortal and wealthy? Every capability needs a cost that explains why the world isn't a utopia.

2. **The special snowflake protagonist.** "Only the hero can do this unique thing." Fine for plot, but the world should have a *reason* — genetic, institutional, historical — and that reason should have implications beyond the hero.

3. **Magic as technology.** Treating spells like software — modular, predictable, endlessly scalable. Magic should feel different from technology, even in hard systems. There should be something about it that resists full systematization.

4. **Ignoring second-order effects.** "Healers exist." Okay — what does that do to medicine, aging, warfare casualty rates, the value of human life, the concept of disability, insurance? Second-order effects are where the richest worldbuilding lives.

5. **Multiple unrelated systems.** Three different kinds of magic that don't interact. Sanderson's Third Law: expand what you have before adding something new. One deep system beats three shallow ones.
