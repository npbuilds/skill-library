# Failure-Mode Catalog — Evidenced

Documented magic-system failures: each entry names the work, the reception evidence, and the design guardrail. Full citations and evidence tiers in the Obsidian vault: `skill-lab/2026-07-10-magic-systems-in-fantasy-research.md` §6. This catalog expands the SKILL.md "Common Mistakes" list from assertion to evidence.

## 1. The system-breaking power

**Case: balefire (Wheel of Time).** Community reception calls it "a totally broken power" and "the easy way out"; the canonical WoT FAQ documents that Jordan contradicted himself on its rules; the setting itself bans it in-text (White Tower prohibition). Casual late-series use deflated built-up threats — a major antagonist erased in a sentence.

**Guardrail:** an ability whose limits are vaguer than the system around it converts every use into a contested exception. If one power must exceed the system, spend explicit budget containing it — and never let the exception become routine.

## 2. The deus-ex-machina spell

**Cases with author admissions:** Tolkien's Eagles — "The Eagles are a dangerous 'machine'. I have used them sparingly, and that is the absolute limit of their credibility or usefulness" (Letter #210); Rowling's time-turner — "a very difficult invention... it created as many problems as it solved," patched by destroying the Ministry's stock.

**Guardrail:** the failure is managerial, not conceptual. A too-strong device can exist if the text pays its limiting budget (Tolkien's "sparingly"; Rowling's demolition). A capability introduced for one plot obligates every subsequent plot. Sanderson's First Law is the operating rule: unexplained magic must not resolve conflict.

## 3. Overcomplexity / worldbuilder's disease

Named by Sanderson (hard pole's self-diagnosis), theorized by Harrison (worldbuilding "numbs the reader's ability to fulfil their part of the bargain"), priced in reception by Malazan and Elden Ring — both deliberately opaque, both documented as polarizing.

**Guardrail:** how much the *text* asserts is a rhetorical stance with costs, independent of how much the author knows. Design deep, narrate selectively. If withholding is the strategy (Malazan, soulsborne), accept the documented admission price: a polarized audience.

## 4. Power creep

**Case: Dragon Ball Z power levels.** Quantified power made outcomes pre-judged; "the investment by the audience into the system is betrayed by plot convenience and power creep — usually resulting in power levels being removed later on." Vertical scaling structurally obsoleted the cast. Prose equivalent: WoT's Rand, "so overpowered in relation to the world around him that he really doesn't need anyone" (community reception). **Documented antidote:** Nen (HxH) — scaling on knowledge, efficiency, and tighter *conditions* rather than magnitude.

**Guardrail:** power creep is what happens when magnitude is the only progression axis. Build horizontal axes (new applications, narrower conditions, costlier vows) before the numbers run away.

## 5. Videogame-ification

**Two documented fronts:** Jemisin — "Game logic should not apply to magic, because it's fucking magic"; "I blame D&D for systematizing so many things that don't need to be... systematized." Michel traces the hard-worldbuilding norm to gaming culture, not literary tradition. The mana genealogy (Niven 1969 → Dungeon Master 1987 → Warcraft) documents the actual transmission channel.

**Guardrail:** a register error, not a rules error — metered magic reads as videogame when the meter is the most visible thing about it. Make the meter diegetic (Allomancy's metals = supply chains and class economics) rather than an interface.

## 6. The presentation trap

**Case: JJK binding vows.** A hard-*presenting* system judged by hard standards: vow costs criticized in reception as nominal ("characters achieve victory with minimal effort... lazy writing"), precisely because the quantified-cost framing invited the scrutiny. GRRM's fully-soft magic draws no such complaints.

**Guardrail:** softness is only punished where hardness was promised. Decide what the text advertises and honor it — or advertise less.

## 7. Metaphor overriding system

**Case: Buffy S6 magic-as-addiction.** Contemporary reception: "'Wrecked' may have hammered home the similarities to drugs too hard, but the analogy is still compelling" — the metaphor retconned a previously-established system's behavior. Counter-case: orogeny works as oppression metaphor *because* it works as seismology first (author-confirmed design intent).

**Guardrail:** build the system so it can carry the metaphor without being the metaphor. When the metaphor needs the system to behave differently than established, the metaphor loses.

## Caution: mischaracterized backlash

The most prominent "anti-hard-magic backlash" evidence — Wired's 2023 Sanderson profile — attacked his *prose*, not hard magic as a design pattern, and the mass response ran in Sanderson's defense. Do not design defensively against a critical consensus that, on the evidence, is one hostile profile plus blog discourse.
