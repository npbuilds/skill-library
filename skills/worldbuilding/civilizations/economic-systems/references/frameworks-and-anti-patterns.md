# Economic Systems — Extended Frameworks & Anti-Patterns

## The Catchphrase Test (Writing Excuses)

Create three catchphrases someone raised in your economy would know. These reveal economic worldview more efficiently than exposition:
- "There ain't no such thing as a free lunch" → scarcity-driven, transactional
- "See it, fix it" → communal responsibility, gift economy
- "The tide lifts all boats" → trickle-down belief system
- "What's mine is mine, what's yours is negotiable" → mercantile, zero-sum

## The Bread Standard

Use bread as a constant for pricing. In many medieval kingdoms, bread price remained stable even during war or drought. Set one silver piece = one loaf of bread, then build multiplier systems:
- A laborer earns 2-3 loaves/day
- A sword costs 100-200 loaves
- A horse costs 1,000-2,000 loaves
- A house costs 5,000-20,000 loaves

Solves "how much does a sword cost?" without modern currency confusion.

## Sources and Sinks (Game Design Framework)

Directly transferable to fiction:
- **Sources (Taps)**: Activities that generate resources/currency (mining, farming, trade, magic)
- **Sinks**: Systems where resources are consumed or destroyed (taxes, consumption, wear, tithes, war)
- Balance prevents inflation (too many sources) or stagnation (too many sinks)
- If currency enters the economy faster than it leaves, everything breaks

## The Three-Sector Model

- **Primary** (80-90% pre-industrial): Extraction — farming, mining, fishing
- **Secondary**: Manufacturing — smiths, weavers, builders
- **Tertiary**: Services — merchants, clergy, soldiers, entertainers

Fantasy anti-pattern: populating cities with adventurers and merchants without the 80-90% agricultural base to feed them.

## The Law of Unintended Consequences

When you design incentive structures, expect secondary effects. Example: paying testers to find bugs creates an underground economy where coders intentionally introduce bugs for kickbacks. Apply to your world: who profits from *maintaining* scarcity?

## Le Guin's *The Dispossessed* — Extended

**Anarres mechanics**: No money. DivLab database matches volunteer labor with work assignments. Syndicalist coordination, not markets. Subtitle "An Ambiguous Utopia" — she refuses to make it work perfectly. Scarcity isn't punishment; it's the cost of freedom. Abundance on Urras isn't reward; it's the product of exploitation.

**Le Guin's principle**: The economy is never backdrop — it IS the argument.

## Post-Scarcity Design

Even post-scarcity worlds need *something* scarce:
- Banks' Culture: social status, attention, meaningful work
- Star Trek: constantly reintroduces scarcity because stories need conflict
- Positional goods (stadium seats, prime real estate) remain scarce even when material needs are met

## Extended Anti-Patterns

**The Charcoal Blind Spot**: Metalsmithing requires enormous charcoal quantities, which requires significant forest resources. Medieval deforestation was driven by charcoal production. Fantasy worlds with thriving smithing and untouched forests have an unacknowledged contradiction.

**Cities Without Economic Purpose**: Economies thrive on specialization. Cities need exports, strategic position, or resource bases. Readers subconsciously notice when a city has no economic reason to exist.

**The Medieval Default**: "Medieval" wasn't one system. Mediterranean trade networks, Northern European feudalism, the Silk Road, Hanseatic League, and Islamic banking all existed simultaneously.

**Republic Credits Problem**: Currency acceptance depends on trust in the issuing authority. Fragmented worlds would not accept each other's currencies — this creates smuggling, money-changing, and arbitrage as potential story elements.

## Ostrom's 8 Principles for Commons Management

Elinor Ostrom proved communities can sustainably manage shared resources without privatization or government control. Her principles for fiction:
1. Define clear group boundaries
2. Match rules to local needs
3. Ensure affected parties can modify rules
4. Monitor compliance within the community
5. Graduated sanctions for violations
6. Low-cost conflict resolution
7. Recognized right to organize
8. Nested enterprises for larger systems

Useful for designing communal/anarchist economies that feel realistic rather than utopian hand-waving.
