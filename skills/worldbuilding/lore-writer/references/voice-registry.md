# Voice Registry

Detailed guide for each voice register available to the lore-writer. Each entry describes the voice's character, its constraints, and how to write in it.

## Epistolary

**Character**: Intimate, urgent, personal. The writer is addressing a specific person, not an audience. There's history between sender and receiver — shared references, inside language, things left unsaid because both parties already know.

**Constraints**:
- First person always
- Assumes shared context — doesn't explain what both parties know
- Emotional subtext lives in what's *not* said directly
- Length varies: a field dispatch is terse, a lover's letter is expansive
- Handwriting artifacts optional (crossed-out words, margin notes, postscripts)

**Sentence texture**: Varied. Short fragments when urgent. Longer, looping sentences when reflective. Questions addressed to the recipient. Imperatives when desperate.

**When it breaks**: If the letter starts explaining context the recipient would already know, the voice is broken. If it reads like exposition with "Dear X" attached, start over.

## Uncanny

**Character**: Precise observation of something that refuses to be understood. The observer is competent, trained, rational — and what they're describing defeats their framework. The horror isn't in what's described but in the gap between the observer's calm language and the wrongness of what they're observing.

**Constraints**:
- Third person or first person clinical ("I observed", "the team noted")
- No emotional language — the emotion comes from the reader, not the text
- Details are hyper-specific but don't add up to a coherent picture
- Measurements, timestamps, and precise descriptions that somehow make things *less* clear
- The text may contain corrections, redactions, or notes from reviewers who also can't explain it

**Sentence texture**: Controlled. Medium length. Precise subordinate clauses. The kind of writing where every word is chosen carefully and the effect is still confusion.

**When it breaks**: If the narrator expresses fear or wonder directly. If the strange thing is explained. The uncanny lives in the gap between observation and understanding — close that gap and the voice dies.

## Mythic

**Character**: The deep voice of a culture speaking about what matters most to it. Myths aren't stories — they're explanations for why the world is the way it is. The voice is archetypal, resonant, unhurried. It assumes the listener already believes.

**Constraints**:
- Third person or collective ("the people knew", "it is said")
- No technical or modern language — use the culture's own terminology
- Repetition is a feature, not a flaw (oral tradition patterns: tripling, refrains)
- Characters in myths are types, not individuals (The First King, The Betrayer, The Wanderer)
- Time is fluid — "in those days", "before the sky changed", "when the waters still spoke"

**Sentence texture**: Rhythmic. Often uses parallel structure. Sentences can be long and rolling or short and declarative like pronouncements. The pattern should feel like something meant to be spoken aloud.

**When it breaks**: If a specific date appears. If a character has psychological interiority. If causation is explained mechanistically rather than morally ("they fell because they were proud" not "their supply lines were overextended").

## Atmospheric

**Character**: Impression over information. This voice captures what a place or moment *feels* like, not what it *is*. Fragmentary. Sensory. The gaps between observations carry as much meaning as the observations themselves. Think of it as the camera lingering on an empty room after everyone has left.

**Constraints**:
- Point of view can shift or be absent entirely
- No obligation to be complete — fragments are valid
- Sensory details dominate (sound, light, temperature, texture, smell)
- Time may be compressed or dilated
- Can use white space, line breaks, and unconventional formatting

**Sentence texture**: Varied dramatically. Single-word fragments. Then a long, synesthetic sentence. Then silence. The rhythm is musical, not logical.

**When it breaks**: If it starts explaining. If it becomes a tour guide ("to the north lies..."). Atmosphere is felt, not narrated.

## Clinical

**Character**: The detached analytical voice. Someone studying the world as a system, documenting findings with professional rigor. The voice of intelligence agencies, academic institutions, forensic investigators, and bureaucracies. Information is categorized, assessed, and filed.

**Constraints**:
- Third person or institutional ("this office assesses", "analysis indicates")
- Structured: sections, subsections, numbered findings, confidence levels
- Emotional content is described as observable behavior, never felt
- May include classifications, distribution lists, or institutional headers
- Footnotes and cross-references to other documents (which may not exist yet)

**Sentence texture**: Medium to long, subordinated, precise. Heavy use of passive voice. Hedged language ("appears to", "is assessed with moderate confidence"). The kind of writing where you have to read a sentence twice to extract the actual claim.

**When it breaks**: If the analyst editorializes. If emotion leaks through (unless that's a deliberate character choice — the analyst cracking). If the structure loosens into narrative.

## Chronicle

**Character**: The weight of history being recorded. Someone writing for posterity — aware that they are shaping how the future understands the past. The chronicler has opinions but presents them as facts. They select what to include and what to omit, and those choices reveal their biases.

**Constraints**:
- Third person, past tense, with the chronicler's perspective embedded in word choice
- Named dates, rulers, places — specific and grounding
- Causation is always presented (even when the chronicler is wrong about causes)
- May quote other sources, speeches, or edicts
- The chronicler's civilization shapes what they notice and what they ignore

**Sentence texture**: Measured, authoritative. Longer sentences for context, shorter ones for impact. The rhythm of someone who expects to be quoted.

**When it breaks**: If it becomes omniscient. A chronicler doesn't know what people were thinking — they know what people *did* and what they *said*. Inner life is inferred, never stated.

## Visceral

**Character**: The body knows before the mind. This voice captures the physical experience of encountering power, violence, the supernatural, or extremity. It's not about what happened — it's about what it *felt like* to be there when it happened.

**Constraints**:
- First person or very close third person
- Present tense or immediate past — no retrospective distance
- Sensory overload: the body's response (heartbeat, breath, temperature, pain, adrenaline)
- Thought is fragmented — coherent analysis comes *after*, not during
- The fantasy system, when encountered, is felt physically before it's understood intellectually

**Sentence texture**: Staccato when action is fast. Run-on when overwhelmed. Incomplete sentences. The syntax mirrors the body's state — controlled breathing = controlled sentences, panic = fragmentation.

**When it breaks**: If the narrator stops to analyze mid-experience. If combat reads like a chess game instead of a fight. If the fantasy system is explained rather than endured.
