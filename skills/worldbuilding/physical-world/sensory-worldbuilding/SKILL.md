# Sensory Worldbuilding — What the World Feels Like

Your world has geography, cultures, magic systems, and political structures. Can you tell me what it *smells* like? What the air feels like on skin at dawn? What sounds fill the market square? What the local bread tastes like?

Sensory worldbuilding is the experiential layer — the difference between a world that is *understood* and a world that is *inhabited*. Structural worldbuilding (axioms, systems, cultures) creates the skeleton. Sensory worldbuilding gives it skin.

## The Five Sensory Channels

Most worldbuilding defaults to the visual. Rich sensory design engages all five channels, with each channel doing different work.

### Sound

What the world sounds like determines its emotional atmosphere before anything else registers.

**Design questions**:
- What's the ambient sound? (Wind through specific vegetation, water, insects, machinery, silence)
- What sounds mark time? (Bells, calls to prayer, factory whistles, birdsong at dusk)
- What sounds signal danger? (Not just alarms — the *absence* of birdsong, a specific insect sound)
- What music exists? What instruments? What does the culture's music reveal about its values?
- How loud is daily life? (A culture that values silence vs. one that fills every space with sound)

### Smell

The most memory-linked sense. A single smell can establish place more powerfully than a paragraph of visual description.

**Design questions**:
- What does the city/village/wilderness smell like? (Cooking spices, animal dung, salt air, ozone from magic, industrial chemicals)
- What smells are status markers? (Perfume vs. labor sweat, fresh bread vs. old meat)
- What smells mark seasons? (Harvest, rot, blooming, snow)
- What smells are culturally significant? (Incense, specific foods, ritual herbs)

### Touch/Texture

The physical experience of moving through the world.

**Design questions**:
- What are surfaces made of? (Stone worn smooth, rough-hewn timber, metal that conducts cold, organic materials that flex)
- What's the climate feel? (Humid air that sticks to skin, dry air that cracks lips, wind that carries grit)
- What do people wear against their skin? (Rough wool, smooth silk, armor that chafes, magical materials with unusual properties)
- What does the local food feel like to eat? (Texture of bread, crunch, chewiness, temperature)

### Taste

Used sparingly but powerfully — food and drink are among the most culturally specific sensory experiences.

**Design questions**:
- What does the local cuisine taste like? (Flavor profiles: spicy, fermented, bland, smoked, sweet)
- What do people drink? (Water quality, local alcohol, ceremonial beverages)
- What taste marks this place as different from anywhere else?
- What does the air taste like? (Salt near the sea, dust in the desert, metallic near forges or magic)

### Proprioception / Spatial Feel

The body's sense of itself in space — often overlooked but powerful for immersion.

**Design questions**:
- What does it feel like to move through this world? (Narrow alleys, vast plains, crowded markets, elevated walkways)
- Are there elevation changes? (Living on a mountain vs. in a valley vs. underground — gravity and air pressure differ)
- How does the fantasy system feel physically? (Does magic tingle, burn, create vertigo, alter spatial perception?)
- What's the scale? (Enormous architecture that makes humans feel small, intimate spaces, open horizons)

## The Sensory Signature

Every distinct location should have a **sensory signature** — a unique combination of 2-3 sensory details that immediately tells the reader "we are HERE."

| Location | Sensory Signature |
|----------|------------------|
| Desert trading city | Smell of cardamom and camel dung + sound of fabric snapping in wind + texture of sand in everything |
| Underground dwarven hold | Sound of hammers echoing through stone + smell of hot metal and mineral water + feel of constant cool temperature |
| Magical academy | Taste of ozone on the tongue + faint hum at the edge of hearing + prickling sensation on skin near active spells |
| Coastal fishing village | Salt air + seabird cries + rough rope texture on hands |

**The test**: Can a reader identify where they are from the sensory details alone, before any visual description or narrator identification?

## Sensory Design by Culture

Different cultures in the same world should produce different sensory environments — because their values, technologies, and relationships to the physical world differ.

A culture that values cleanliness smells different from one that doesn't. A culture with advanced metallurgy has different ambient sounds than an agrarian one. A culture that cooks with oil has a different smell-landscape than one that cooks with fire and smoke.

**The principle**: Sensory worldbuilding is not applied after cultural design — it's derived from it. The culture's values, technology, and environment produce specific sensory conditions.

## Relationship to Other Skills

### From Worldbuilding
- **Geography-Ecology**: Climate and biome determine the baseline sensory environment (humidity, temperature, ambient sounds, vegetation smells)
- **Cultures-Societies**: Cultural values and technology shape the built sensory environment
- **Technology-Progression**: Tech level determines ambient sound (pre-industrial vs. industrial), materials available, and the presence of artificial sensory elements

### From Writing
- **Concrete Detail**: Provides the technique for rendering sensory details vividly on the page. This skill designs WHAT the senses encounter; concrete-detail provides HOW to write it.
- **Character Interiority**: The perception filter determines which sensory details a character notices — shaped by their emotional state, background, and attention.
- **Figurative Language**: Sensory details become metaphorical when filtered through character consciousness — a city that "smells like old money" is sensory worldbuilding + figurative language.
- **World-to-Story**: Sensory details are the primary vehicle for immersive revelation — show the world through what characters smell, hear, and feel before explaining what they see.

## When This Applies

- A world is structurally complete but feels abstract or "described rather than experienced"
- Settings all feel the same despite being in different locations
- The user asks how to make a world feel "real" or "immersive"
- Prose relies on visual description only — needs multi-sensory grounding
- Different cultures in the world don't feel sensorially distinct
