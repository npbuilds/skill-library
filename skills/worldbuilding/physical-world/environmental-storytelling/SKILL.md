# Environmental Storytelling — The World as Text

A crumbling wall tells you about a siege. A worn path through tall grass tells you about foot traffic. A shrine with fresh flowers beside a burned building tells you about faith surviving destruction. The physical world is a text — every surface records the forces that shaped it.

Environmental storytelling makes the world itself a narrator. Instead of telling the reader "this city was conquered twice," you show them the layer of different stonework in the walls, the foreign-sounding street names in the old quarter, the monument with its inscription chiseled out and re-carved.

## Henry Jenkins' Four Types

### 1. Evocative Spaces
Environments that trigger pre-existing narrative associations. A dark forest, a gleaming tower, a labyrinthine market — these carry cultural weight before any character enters them. The reader's genre literacy does narrative work.

**Craft use**: Establish atmosphere instantly through spatial archetype. Subvert expectations by making the dark forest safe and the gleaming tower dangerous.

### 2. Embedded Narratives
Stories told through objects, documents, and environmental details — mise-en-scene as narrative. The player/reader pieces together what happened by examining what was left behind.

**Craft use**: Scatter evidence of past events through the environment. A table set for two but only one chair pushed back. Books stacked by the door. The story is assembled by the reader, not told by the narrator.

### 3. Enacted Stories
The environment as staging ground for character action — spaces designed to produce specific experiences. A narrow corridor creates claustrophobia. A vast plain creates vulnerability. The space shapes what happens in it.

**Craft use**: Design locations that constrain or enable specific types of conflict. A negotiation in a room with one exit feels different from one in an open courtyard.

### 4. Emergent Narratives
Stories that arise from the interaction between characters and environment — unplanned, systemic, surprising. The environment responds to character action, and the response creates new narrative.

**Craft use**: Design environments with interactive elements. A market that changes when a character's actions disrupt trade routes. Architecture that degrades as political power shifts.

## The Archaeology Method

Borrowed from Outer Wilds and Return of the Obra Dinn: the reader/player reconstructs history by examining physical evidence, not by reading exposition.

### Design Principles

**Layer time in physical space**: Different eras leave different materials, styles, and states of decay. A building with a stone foundation, timber upper floors, and a modern metal roof tells three centuries of history without a word.

**Leave traces of use**: Worn steps indicate heavy foot traffic. Polished handrails on one side show which hand people favor. Grease stains around a door handle show it's frequently used. These micro-details create the sense of a lived-in world.

**Show consequences of events**: A fire leaves soot patterns. A flood leaves a water line. A battle leaves scars on walls and missing sections of fence. The physical world remembers what happened to it.

**Let absence tell stories**: An empty pedestal tells you a statue was removed. A gap in a bookshelf tells you a volume was taken. The outline of a removed painting on a sun-faded wall tells you someone left.

**Architecture encodes power**: Who built this, and for whom? A temple that dominates the skyline tells you about religious authority. A palace behind walls tells you about fear. Housing that's uniform tells you about central planning. Housing that's chaotic tells you about organic growth.

## Environmental Storytelling in Prose

Game design creates environments players walk through. Prose creates environments readers imagine. The translation requires different techniques:

**The perception filter** (from `character-interiority`): A character doesn't see the environment neutrally. They notice what matters to them, ignore what doesn't, and interpret everything through their emotional state. Environmental storytelling in prose works through what a *specific character* notices about a space.

**Selective description**: Don't describe everything. Describe the 2-3 details that tell the story. The reader's imagination fills in the rest — and their version will be more vivid than exhaustive description.

**The "what happened here?" question**: For every important location, answer: what happened here before the characters arrived? What evidence remains? Which characters would notice that evidence, and how would they interpret it?

**Dynamic environments**: Environments should change as the story progresses. A market that's bustling in Act 1 and empty in Act 3 tells the reader about what happened between without explaining it.

## Anti-Patterns

1. **The tour guide**: Characters who walk through environments narrating the history of every building. Let the environment speak through evidence, not through character exposition.
2. **Static backdrop**: Environments that never change regardless of story events. If a war happened, the city should show it.
3. **Undifferentiated spaces**: Every tavern, every forest, every castle feels the same. Each location should have a specific identity derived from its history and use.
4. **Over-describing**: Cataloguing every object in a room. Environmental storytelling is about the *significant* details — the ones that tell stories.
