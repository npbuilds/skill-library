# The Five Axes — Worked

Each axis, rendered in three layers: a **craft question** you can act on with no theory, a **worked case** that shows the pattern, and the **theory underneath** (tucked in an aside, cross-linked for the reader who wants the rigor). Run the **stated/felt split** on every one — the gap between the rational face and the emotional driver is where the drama and the instability live.

Full citations and evidence tiers: Obsidian vault `skill-lab/2026-07-19-conflict-design-research.md`.

---

## 1. Stakes — what is this actually about, and can it be split?

**Craft question:** What does each side say it's fighting for — and what are they *really* fighting for? Then the decisive one: **is the stake divisible or indivisible?** A divisible stake (money, territory, a trade route) can be bargained; an indivisible one (a throne, a holy site, survival, *face*) forces the fight. Most stakes are divisible on paper — so ask what makes *this* one feel un-splittable.

**In practice —** In Legend of the Galactic Heroes the *stated* stake is the form of government (democracy vs. autocracy); the *felt* stake is Reinhard's need to destroy the dynasty that humiliated his family, and Yang's refusal to let one man's virtue decide history. The war is unbargainable not because power can't be shared but because neither felt driver can be. In Attack on Titan the stake looks like security; the felt stake is a cycle of inherited humiliation that makes coexistence "not plausible" — an emotionally indivisible stake dressed as a strategic one.

> <sub>**Under the hood** — Fearon's third rationalist cause of war is **issue indivisibility**, and he calls it the *weakest* of the three because side-payments can usually make an issue divisible. That's the design gold: indivisibility is rarely material. **Honor, identity, and existential fear are what make a divisible stake indivisible** — the felt layer converts a bargainable dispute into a war. → `cooperative-games` (bargaining range, BATNA).</sub>

## 2. Cost — who pays, in what currency, and does it propagate?

**Craft question:** War is *always* ex-post inefficient — both sides pay costs a deal could have avoided. So: what does this conflict cost each side, in blood, treasure, legitimacy, or moral standing? Does that cost land on the people making the decisions, or on someone else? And **does the cost propagate** — show up in the economy, the next generation, the survivors — or vanish between scenes?

**In practice —** In Gundam, Anaheim Electronics arms both sides and pays no cost at all — which is exactly why it's a *structural* actor, not a hero: the war's cost is displaced onto the colonies while the profit concentrates. The costed-conflict antidote is the opposite move: assign every character, high or low, a consequence of the war, so the reader feels the price the strategists are spending.

> <sub>**Under the hood** — the **Truffaut problem**: "Every film about war ends up being pro-war," because spectacle glamorizes even sincere anti-war material. Keeping the cost visible and *propagating* is the craft answer — otherwise the conflict reads as a bloodless strategy game (see `failure-modes.md`). The felt layer: **grievance is what makes people pay a price no cost-benefit would justify** (Petersen's resentment). → `empires-and-states` (the legitimacy-coercion cost trade-off).</sub>

## 3. Lifecycle — how does it escalate, and what holds it?

**Craft question:** Lay out the **rungs** — the discrete steps from cold dispute to total war — and mark the **thresholds** between them (the lines that, once crossed, can't be uncrossed). Then ask the two questions that decide the shape: what **equilibrium** holds the conflict at its current rung (deterrence? exhaustion? dependency?), and **which ratchet dominates** — the rational one (which self-corrects toward the off-ramp) or the emotional one (the cycle of reprisal, which self-amplifies away from it)?

**In practice —** A security-dilemma spiral is the engine of countless faction wars: two status-quo powers, each fearing the other, arm defensively; each reads the other's defense as aggression; the spiral produces the war both wished to avoid. The diagnostic that makes it *writable*: you often **cannot tell whether you're in a spiral (firmness inflames a fearful adversary) or a deterrence situation (softness emboldens a greedy one)** — and picking wrong is catastrophic. That uncertainty is the scene.

> <sub>**Under the hood** — Kahn's **escalation ladder** (44 rungs, named thresholds) discretizes intensity; **escalation dominance** means holding a rung so the opponent has more to lose from climbing. Jervis's **spiral vs. deterrence models** are opposite diagnoses of the same crisis. The ratchet is real: Fearon's **audience costs** make backing down progressively more humiliating (a lock-in horizon past which war is inevitable), and Axelrod's tit-for-tat shows one defection can detonate an "unending echo" of reprisals — the mechanized cycle of hatred, anchored in the anthropology of the blood feud (Boehm). → `classical-games`.</sub>

## 4. Legitimacy — how does each side justify itself, and to whom?

**Craft question:** For each faction, separate the **stated justification** (the legal, strategic, or historical case it makes) from the **emotional mobilizer** (what actually moves people to fight and die for it — identity, grievance, fear, hope, hatred). A conflict where both sides believe their own legitimacy reads truer than one with a designated villain. Ask what each side's propaganda *asserts* and what it *feels like* underneath.

**In practice —** 86's San Magnolia Republic is the sharpest case: its stated legitimacy is that it *invented democracy* and is fighting a casualty-free war; the reality is racialized conscription of a minority it has legally declared non-human. The stated legitimacy is the cover for the felt engine (racial resentment). McKee's "negation of the negation" names the shape exactly — the compound negative, **"war disguised as peace,"** that resists any clean good/evil reading.

> <sub>**Under the hood** — Weber's legitimacy typology (traditional / charismatic / legal-rational / performance / ideological) and the **legitimacy-coercion trade-off** give the stated face its taxonomy. The felt face is Petersen's four emotions — **fear, hatred, resentment, rage** — each a mechanism that "heightens the saliency" of one concern; across his cases, *resentment* (unjust group status) is the best predictor. Propaganda is emotional engineering: it manufactures the felt mobilizer and drapes it in the stated justification. → `empires-and-states`, `social-movements-and-identity`.</sub>

## 5. Termination — how could it end, and what seals each exit?

**Craft question:** List the exits — decisive victory, mutual exhaustion, outside intervention, negotiated settlement, transformation into a *different* conflict — and for each one your conflict could plausibly take, ask the real question: **what makes that off-ramp unacceptable to the people who'd have to walk it?** The deal both sides would prefer to fighting usually exists long before anyone can take it; find the emotional lock on the rational door.

**In practice —** Attack on Titan seals its off-ramp: coexistence is rationally available, but the cycle of hatred makes any settlement read as a humiliation no side survives politically — "not plausible." Contrast LOGH at Vermilion, where the off-ramp *is* taken because Yang's principle (civilian control of the military) outweighs the emotional cost of standing down. Same door; one conflict's felt layer bolts it shut, the other's unbolts it.

> <sub>**Under the hood** — termination is the three bargaining failures **in reverse**: fighting must reveal enough private information for expectations to converge (Reiter), and both sides must solve the **commitment problem** — believe the other won't renege (Walter: civil wars are hardest to end because disarming first is fatal, so third-party guarantees are usually required). Zartman's **ripe moment** = a Mutually Hurting Stalemate plus a perceived Way Out. Contested: whether negotiated settlements or decisive victories produce more durable peace (Toft/Licklider — victories last longer but risk atrocity; Luttwak's contrarian "give war a chance"). → `cooperative-games`.</sub>

## Using the axes together

The axes are not independent. Indivisible **stakes** raise the **cost** each side will bear, which lengthens the **lifecycle**, which hardens **legitimacy** into something that blocks **termination**. Run the stated/felt split down the whole column: a conflict where the felt drivers align across all five axes (LOGH: humiliation → willingness to pay → escalation → dynastic legitimacy → unbargainable) is a total war; one where they diverge (a mercenary war fought for divisible stakes with cheap exits) is a limited one. The pattern of alignment *is* the conflict's shape.
