# Conflict-Design Failure Modes

The documented ways a designed conflict goes wrong, each with the mechanism, a named example, and a guardrail. Full citations: `skill-lab/2026-07-19-conflict-design-research.md`. This expands the SKILL.md "Common Mistakes" from assertion to evidence.

## 1. The monolithic-evil conflict

**Mechanism:** one rootable side against one villainous side. It fails because the scholarship *and* the craft both hold that real conflict distributes sympathy and culpability — no camp is all virtue or all crime.

**Named contrast (done right):** LOGH makes the autocrat competent and fair and the democracy corrupt; 86 makes the "inventor of democracy" the genocidal party. McKee: "a protagonist can only be as compelling as the forces of antagonism make them," and the villain "is the hero of his own story."

**Guardrail:** give every faction a real **virtue** and a real **crime**, and put the crime inside the public/private gap. Give the opposing side a *legitimate grievance* — write them "making their case to God why they'd be allowed into Heaven" (Sorkin).

## 2. The bloodless strategy game

**Mechanism:** conflict as a chess problem — moves, counter-moves, territory — with no human price on the page. The stakes become notional; the reader plays along intellectually but feels nothing.

**Named example / theory:** the **Truffaut problem** — "every film about war ends up being pro-war," because spectacle without cost glamorizes. The mecha-round twin: Tomino's lament that fans consume his anti-war franchise as "military geeks."

**Guardrail:** assign every character, regardless of station, a **consequence of the war**, and make the cost *propagate* (economy, next generation, survivors). The cost is what converts a strategy game into a conflict.

## 3. Uncoupled variance (escalation without payoff)

**Mechanism:** emotion and stakes cranked past what the conflict can absorb — each beat louder than the last with nothing underneath, so escalation reads as the author flailing rather than the world breaking.

**Named example:** Gundam SEED Destiny (characterization collapses under the escalation) and Valvrave ("doesn't know when enough is enough... without the payoff").

**Guardrail:** variance must stay **coupled to stakes**. Bounded unpredictability is drama; unbounded is chaos. Turn the emotion knob up only as far as the established stakes justify.

## 4. Deus ex machina resolution

**Mechanism:** the conflict is ended by something outside the characters and the stakes established — an external rescue, an unforeshadowed power, a convenient death. It "robs the protagonist of the critical moment."

**Named theory:** the craft consensus generalizes **Sanderson's First Law** to all conflict resolution — "ability to solve conflict is proportional to how well the reader understands" the means. Turning points must come from character choice; resolution must be earned via cause-and-effect.

**Guardrail:** design the ending *backward* from the deal both sides would prefer, then the emotional block that keeps them from taking it — so the resolution turns on someone finally paying, or refusing to pay, that emotional cost. Earned, not imposed.

## 5. The rational-only conflict

**Mechanism:** a conflict modeled purely on interests and payoffs — correct, legible, and inert. Because it's a *solved* game, the ending is readable from the setup, and there's no tension.

**Named theory:** a rational actor should never execute a mutually-destructive threat, so a purely rational standoff never tips — it just resolves. Pauly & McDermott: credibility (and therefore the possibility of the thing actually happening) comes from a human "acting on emotion or bias."

**Guardrail:** layer the **biased variance** onto the rational skeleton — who is loss-framed and will gamble, who is honor-bound and can be provoked. The variance is what makes the outcome uncertain, and uncertainty is the story.

## 6. The un-tippable or instantly-tipping order

**Mechanism (two directions):** an equilibrium so stable nothing can disturb it (no story) — or one so fragile it collapses at the first touch (no structure, so the collapse means nothing). Both drain the metastable middle where conflict lives.

**Named theory:** metastability requires a real holding force (deterrence, dependency, exhaustion) *and* a real tipping variance. Turchin's PSI and Scheffer's critical-slowing-down give the tip a build-up — you show it coming as felt volatility rises.

**Guardrail:** name what *holds* the order and what would *tip* it, and make the tip a threshold someone's felt assessment crosses — foreshadowed by rising emotional variance (hotter reprisals, quieter moderates), not a switch flipped off-page.

## 7. The plot-trivializing strategist

**Mechanism:** a genius character with no variance to manage — no rival smart enough to model them back, no principle that constrains them — so every conflict resolves the moment they engage. The inverse failure (LOGH's own documented flaw) is "author cheating": genius asserted, or rivals made stupid.

**Guardrail:** bind the strategist with a **principle** (a self-imposed cap on their own volatility) that a lesser operator would violate, and give them a foil who caps their own variance too. The conflict becomes a contest of who reads whom — and the principle is where the plot can *cost* them (Yang at Vermilion). Show the genius through a concrete, unexpected move that exploits a known emotional tell, never through assertion.
