# Insurgency & Revolution — how rebellions build, fight, and corrupt

The special case that recurs most in fiction: the conflict fought from below. This reference covers how an insurgency forms, why its violence takes the shape it does, and — the part fiction most often gets wrong or right — how it corrupts. Full citations: `skill-lab/2026-07-19-conflict-design-research.md`. For the *stages* of revolution (Brinton, Skocpol), read `revolutions-and-regime-change` — this cross-links rather than restating.

## The fracture is built in: the principal-agent gap

**Craft question:** What does the rebellion *say* it's for, and what do its *leaders* actually want? The gap between the two is the insurgency's built-in fracture line — and its most likely betrayal. Design it on purpose.

Rebellions in fiction corrupt not through military defeat but through the divergence between the **stated cause** and the **leaders' actual drivers**:
- Code Geass's Black Knights collapse on the gap between "liberate Japan" and Lelouch's private revenge.
- Gundam's Zeon: a *democratic* independence movement captured by the Zabi family and inverted into a fascist principality — the cause becomes the captor's throne.
- Animal Farm dramatizes the mechanism directly (Squealer: "It is for your sake that we drink that milk"), ending at "some animals are more equal than others."

> <sub>**Under the hood** — this is a documented structure, not just a trope. Salehyan, Siroky & Wood (2014) model rebel groups as **agents of their sponsors**: externally-funded rebels "have weaker incentives to protect civilians because they depend less on... the local population," and the *sponsor's* characteristics shape the group's conduct. Weinstein's *Inside Rebellion*: resource-rich groups attract opportunists and abuse; resource-poor "activist" groups restrain. The stated cause and the actual (sponsor-shaped, resource-shaped) incentive diverge — and that gap is the drama. → `social-movements-and-identity`.</sub>

## Why the violence takes the shape it does

**Craft question:** Where is your insurgency's violence *selective* (targeted assassinations, denunciations) and where is it *indiscriminate* (massacres, collective punishment)? The answer should track **control**, not cruelty.

Insurgent and counter-insurgent violence is **strategic and control-driven** — not irrational brutality. Selective violence peaks in zones of *secure-but-incomplete* control (you have enough presence to act on information, not enough to be unchallenged); indiscriminate violence marks *fragmented* control (you can't identify individuals, so you punish the group). Crucially, selective violence is **jointly produced**: the armed actor wants information, and civilians supply denunciations to settle private scores through the macro-cleavage — the "intimacy of violence." A neighbor's grudge wears the war's colors.

> <sub>**Under the hood** — Kalyvas, *The Logic of Violence in Civil War* (2006): violence is "linked to the pursuit of political objectives" and tracks the "level of control." This is the antidote to writing atrocity as motiveless evil — it has a logic, and the logic is local. Contested at the margins (Petersen, Wood, Collins argue for expressive/non-strategic microdynamics too).</sub>

## The corruption arc: the revolution devours its own

**Craft question:** After the rebellion wins, what breaks the coalition that won it?

The structural arc: a diverse coalition of counter-elites unites to topple the regime, then fractures once in power as ideological and personal tensions surface — "the revolution devours its children" (Mallet du Pan, 1793). The moderates who made the revolution respectable are outflanked by radicals; the radicals purge each other; exhaustion produces a Thermidorian reaction and often a strongman. This is not cynicism about revolution — it's the documented dynamics of what happens when a broad "against" coalition has to become a governing "for" coalition.

> <sub>**Under the hood** — Brinton's *Anatomy of Revolution* (stages: old-order dysfunction → moderate phase → radicalization → Terror → Thermidor) and Skocpol's structural theory (state fiscal crisis + elite defection + popular autonomy) are fully treated in `revolutions-and-regime-change`. Turchin frames the coalition-fracture mechanism through elite overproduction. The insurgency's *emotional* fuel is Petersen's **resentment** — a sense of unjust group status — which is why toppling the hierarchy so often reproduces it with new occupants.</sub>

## Designing an insurgency: a checklist

1. **The bargaining failure that made rebellion rational** — usually a commitment problem (the state can't credibly promise not to exploit a disarmed minority) or indivisibility (recognition, dignity, self-rule).
2. **The principal-agent gap** — stated cause vs. the leaders' (and any sponsor's) actual drivers. This is your fracture line.
3. **The control map** — where control is secure, contested, or fragmented, because that determines where and how violence appears.
4. **The corruption trajectory** — who gets outflanked, who purges whom, what the "for" coalition can't agree on once the "against" is gone.
5. **The emotional engine** — fear, hatred, resentment, or rage as the mobilizer (resentment is the most common and the most self-perpetuating).
