# Conlang Craft — Language Shapes the World

The `naming-system` skill gives each culture a sound palette — enough to make names consistent and cultures audibly distinct. This skill goes deeper: morphology, syntax, and the principle that **language shapes how its speakers think about reality**. Tolkien didn't build languages to decorate his world — he built the world to give his languages a home.

Not every project needs a full conlang. This skill provides a depth gradient: from "just enough for names" (handled by naming-system) through "enough for key phrases and cultural flavor" to "full constructed language."

## The Depth Gradient

### Level 1 — Sound Palette (naming-system)
Phoneme inventory, syllable rules, naming conventions. Enough for consistent proper nouns. Already covered by `naming-system`.

### Level 2 — Cultural Flavor (this skill's sweet spot)
Add morphology, a few grammatical principles, and key vocabulary that reveals cultural values. Enough for:
- Key phrases characters speak in-language
- Idioms and proverbs that reveal worldview
- Untranslatable words that define the culture's unique concepts
- Place names that mean something in the culture's language

### Level 3 — Functional Grammar
Add syntax, verb systems, and enough grammar to write short passages in the language. David Peterson's level for Game of Thrones — enough for actors to speak it convincingly.

### Level 4 — Full Conlang
Complete grammar, extensive lexicon, historical linguistics showing how the language evolved. Tolkien's level. Only necessary if the language IS the project.

## Morphological Typology

How a language builds words determines how it *feels* to speakers and readers.

| Type | How It Works | Feel | Cultural Implication |
|------|-------------|------|---------------------|
| **Isolating** | One morpheme per word. No affixes. Word order carries meaning. | Clean, economical, precise | Culture values clarity, directness, efficiency |
| **Agglutinative** | Words built by stringing meaningful pieces together. Each piece = one meaning. | Elaborate, modular, systematic | Culture values precision, categorization, order |
| **Fusional** | Affixes carry multiple meanings simultaneously. One suffix = tense + number + person. | Compact, layered, ambiguous | Culture comfortable with complexity, hidden meanings |
| **Polysynthetic** | Entire sentences compressed into single words. Verbs incorporate subjects, objects, modifiers. | Dense, holistic, action-oriented | Culture sees events as unified wholes, not component parts |

**The fiction writer's shortcut**: You don't need to build a full morphological system. Choose a type and apply it to a handful of key words. If the culture is agglutinative, show how their word for "warrior-who-fights-in-mountains" is a single compound word. That one example tells the reader how the language works.

## The Sapir-Whorf Principle as Worldbuilding Tool

The hypothesis that language shapes thought is debated in linguistics, but it's a goldmine for fiction. In a constructed world, you can make it literally true.

### What a Language Has

Words that exist reveal what a culture notices and values:
- A desert culture with 30 words for sand (texture, color, stability, danger level)
- A seafaring culture with precise vocabulary for wind direction, wave type, current
- A culture with seven words for "honor" distinguishing between honor-given, honor-earned, honor-inherited, honor-challenged

### What a Language Lacks

Absent concepts are even more revealing:
- A culture with no word for "privacy" — what does this say about their social structure?
- A language where "I" and "we" are the same word — what does this imply about individualism?
- No future tense — does this culture think about the future differently?
- No passive voice — does this culture always assign responsibility?

### Grammatical Worldview

Grammar itself can encode values:
- **Evidentiality markers**: Some real languages (Quechua, Turkish) require speakers to mark whether information is firsthand knowledge, hearsay, or inference. A fictional culture with mandatory evidentiality would develop very differently around truth and rumor.
- **Honorific systems**: Japanese-style register shifts based on social relationship — every sentence encodes the power dynamic between speaker and listener.
- **Noun classification**: Not just "masculine/feminine" but classification by animacy, shape, sacredness, danger level. What a language classifies reveals what it considers important.
- **Tense systems**: A language with no past tense (only "completed/ongoing") produces a culture with a different relationship to history.

## The Conlang-to-Culture Pipeline

Tolkien worked language-first: invent the language, then derive the culture. This is a legitimate and powerful direction.

**How it works**: Design a language's quirks first, then ask what kind of culture would *need* this language.

1. Create a phonological palette (naming-system handles this)
2. Choose a morphological type and design a few example words
3. Add 2-3 grammatical quirks (evidentiality? no future tense? mandatory honorifics?)
4. Ask: what cultural values and blind spots do these linguistic features imply?
5. Build the culture outward from those implications

**The reverse direction** (culture-to-language) is what `naming-system` already does. This pipeline adds the forward direction.

## Practical Conlang for Fiction Writers

What you actually need, by project type:

| Project | Minimum Depth | What To Build |
|---------|--------------|--------------|
| Novel with one non-English culture | Level 2 | Sound palette + 20 key words + 5 idioms + naming conventions |
| Novel with multiple cultures | Level 2 per culture | Sound palettes that contrast + key vocabulary showing different values |
| Fantasy epic with deep linguistics | Level 3 for main culture | Functional grammar, 100+ word lexicon, in-language dialogue |
| Tolkien-scale language project | Level 4 | Full grammar, historical linguistics, related language family |
| Game/RPG setting | Level 2 | Sound palettes + place name generators + key terms |

## Writing Systems

The type of writing system a culture develops reveals its history:

| System | How It Works | Implies |
|--------|-------------|---------|
| **Pictographic** | Pictures represent objects | Early development; close connection to visual reality |
| **Logographic** | Symbols represent words/concepts (Chinese characters) | Long literate history; high barrier to literacy; cultural reverence for writing |
| **Syllabary** | Symbols represent syllables (Japanese kana) | Intermediate complexity; often adapted from contact with logographic system |
| **Alphabet** | Symbols represent individual sounds | High abstraction; relatively easy to learn; often spreads through trade |
| **Abugida** | Consonant base with vowel modifications (Devanagari) | Systematic, elegant; reflects linguistic analysis by the culture's scholars |

**Design question**: Who invented writing in this culture, and why? Merchants (for trade records)? Priests (for sacred texts)? Bureaucrats (for governance)? The origin shapes what gets written and who can read.

## Relationship to Other Skills

- **Naming-System**: This skill extends naming-system downward. Naming-system provides Level 1 (sound palette); this skill provides Levels 2-4.
- **Cultures-Societies**: Language shapes culture and culture shapes language. The Sapir-Whorf principle connects linguistic design to all seven pillars.
- **Religion-Design**: Sacred languages, liturgical registers, secret priestly vocabulary — religion often preserves archaic linguistic forms.
- **History-Builder**: Languages change over time. Historical events leave linguistic traces — loanwords from conquerors, archaic terms from founding myths.
- **Lore-Writer**: In-universe artifacts written in or quoting the constructed language need linguistic consistency.
- **Writing/diction**: When writing prose from a culture's perspective, the narrator's diction should harmonize with the culture's linguistic character.

## When This Applies

- A culture needs more linguistic depth than naming conventions
- The user asks about creating a fictional language
- Language itself is thematically important to the world
- A culture's unique concepts need untranslatable words
- In-universe dialogue or documents need to quote the language
- The user wants to work language-first (Tolkien method)
