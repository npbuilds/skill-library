# Cuisine Pairing — The World Table

**Type:** Knowledge
**Suite:** Bacchus

## Description
Maps wine pairing traditions and strategies across the world's major cuisine families. Goes beyond generic rules to understand how each cuisine's dominant flavors, cooking methods, and ingredient patterns demand different pairing approaches. Covers French, Italian, Japanese, Indian, Thai, Middle Eastern, Mexican, Korean, and cheese — with structural explanations for why each tradition works.

---

## French Cuisine
French cuisine is the historical anchor of Western wine pairing, and its regional specificity is its greatest pairing asset. The guiding principle is almost always regional match: the wine of the region was designed to accompany the food of the region.

**Classic regional matches:**
- **Burgundy → Pinot Noir:** coq au vin (made with Pinot Noir), boeuf bourguignon (same), game birds (duck, pheasant). The earthy, slightly gamey character of Burgundy Pinot matches the savory depth of these preparations.
- **Bordeaux → Cabernet-based blends:** lamb (Pauillac's classic match — the region's shepherds served lamb with what was being made around them), duck confit, aged beef. Cabernet's tannin + lamb protein = the textbook structural pairing.
- **Lyon bouchon → Beaujolais:** charcuterie (pâté, rillettes, saucisson), oeufs en meurette (eggs in red wine sauce), andouillette. Beaujolais's low tannin and bright fruit navigate the fatty, rich, sometimes acidic character of Lyonnaise charcuterie without the weight of Burgundy.
- **Alsace → Riesling/Gewürztraminer:** choucroute garnie (sauerkraut, pork, sausage), tarte flambée (crème fraîche, lardons, onion), baeckeoffe (meat and potato casserole). Alsatian wines — especially Gewürztraminer — have the aromatic weight and slight sweetness to match the Germanic richness of Alsatian food.
- **Provence → Rosé:** essentially everything. The rosés of Provence (Grenache, Cinsault, Mourvèdre-based) are designed to accompany the olive oil, garlic, tomato, herbs de Provence, and seafood that define the region's table. The rule in Provence: when in doubt, rosé.
- **Brittany → Muscadet:** oysters, moules marinières, langoustine. Muscadet's neutral, briny, sometimes slightly effervescent (sur lie) character is a direct echo of the Atlantic shellfish environment.

**Sauces determine the wine, not the protein:**
- Beurre blanc → white Burgundy (Meursault, Pouilly-Fuissé): butter + Chardonnay's natural butter affinity
- Red wine reduction → Pinot Noir or young Bordeaux: the sauce is made from the wine family, so match accordingly
- Cream sauce → white Burgundy, white Rhône (Roussanne/Marsanne-based): texture and weight match
- Provençal (tomato, olive, garlic) → Grenache-based rosé or light Southern Rhône red

## Italian Cuisine
The foundational principle of Italian pairing is **acid matching**. Italian food and Italian wine both operate at higher acidity levels than most other world cuisines and their accompanying wines. The result is a coherent, internally consistent system: Italian wines taste best with Italian food, and vice versa.

**Pasta:**
- **Tomato-based (pomodoro, arrabbiata, amatriciana):** Sangiovese (Chianti Classico, Rosso di Montalcino) — the wine's high acid matches tomato's acidity, its cherry fruit echoes the sweetness in cooked tomato. Barbera d'Asti and d'Alba work beautifully — very high acid, low tannin, round.
- **Cream-based (carbonara, alfredo):** the fat and richness shift the calculus. Fuller whites (Soave Classico, Falanghina, Arneis) work with delicate cream pastas. Light reds without aggressive tannin (Dolcetto, lighter Barbera) work with richer cream+meat combinations.
- **Pesto:** Vermentino, Pigato, Gavi — the herbaceous, grassy note in Ligurian whites echoes the basil, and the acid cuts through olive oil.
- **Pasta with mushrooms (porcini):** the earthiness calls for earthy wines — Pinot Nero (Alto Adige, Oltrepò Pavese), Barbera, or in the most intense preparations, young Barolo.

**Risotto:**
- **Seafood risotto:** Soave Classico (Garganega's texture and mineral), Lugana (Turbiana's weight and acid), Arneis
- **Mushroom risotto:** Pinot Nero, Barbera — earthiness mirrors earthiness
- **Saffron risotto (Milanese):** Franciacorta (sparkling, the regional match from Lombardy)
- **Truffle risotto:** Barolo or Barbaresco — the complexity and earthy depth require a serious wine

**Meat:**
- **Bistecca Fiorentina (T-bone, 1.5kg+, rare, no sauce):** Brunello di Montalcino or a quality Super Tuscan. The steak is so large and rich that only the biggest Sangiovese-based wines have the structure. Barolo is sometimes contested here — the Tuscan purist says Brunello, full stop.
- **Abbacchio (Roman roast lamb):** Cesanese del Piglio or a medium-weight Montepulciano d'Abruzzo
- **Osso buco (Milanese braised veal):** Barbaresco, or the traditional Milanese match is Sforzato di Valtellina

**Pizza:** Montepulciano d'Abruzzo (the great pizza wine of Italy — moderate tannin, high acid, dark fruit, priced for casual drinking), Primitivo, Negroamaro, anything with acid and moderate tannin that doesn't demand attention. Pizza is a backdrop, not a centerpiece — the wine should be honest, not precious.

**Seafood:** Vermentino (especially Sardinian — maritime, herbal, saline), Falanghina (Campanian, floral, citrusy), Greco di Tufo (fuller, mineral, great with shellfish), Vernaccia di San Gimignano (almond finish pairs with Tuscan seafood).

**The fish + Sangiovese question:** Traditional in many parts of Italy, especially with oily or strongly flavored fish (sardines, anchovies). Lower-tannin, higher-acid expressions of Sangiovese reduce the metallic interaction with fish oils. The logic: the wine's acid cuts the oiliness, and the lighter body versions don't impose tannin. This is a case where regional tradition has worked around the structural constraint.

## Japanese Cuisine
Japanese cuisine's dominant taste is **umami** — the savory depth created by glutamates and inosinates from soy sauce, dashi (kombu + bonito), miso, and aged fish. The pairing challenge is that high umami in food makes many wines taste bitter, metallic, and harsh. Tannin is particularly problematic: tannin + umami = amplified bitterness on both sides.

**The pairing solutions:**
- **Champagne (the single best wine for Japanese cuisine):** autolysis during extended lees aging produces amino acids and nucleotides — the building blocks of umami. Champagne essentially contains its own umami. The acid is high and cleansing. Bubbles reset the palate. The Maillard/yeast character bridges to soy and dashi. This is not coincidence — it's chemical convergence.
- **Alsatian Pinot Gris:** broad, textured, lower in aggressive fruit, off-dry or dry with weight. The textural bridge with dishes like tempura and braised proteins works well.
- **Chablis:** steely, mineral, no oak, high acid. Pure cut without competing flavors.
- **Sake:** the parallel umami universe — sake contains its own glutamates from rice fermentation. Not technically wine, but the natural pairing.

**Specific dishes:**
- **Sushi/sashimi:** Champagne (NV Brut or Blanc de Blancs), Chablis, delicate Pinot Gris. Avoid any red wine — tannin on raw fish = metallic. Avoid full whites — they overwhelm delicate raw fish flavors.
- **Tempura:** Champagne (bubbles + acid = same logic as fried food pairing everywhere)
- **Ramen:** depends entirely on the broth
  - *Shio (salt-based, lightest):* Grüner Veltliner, Pinot Gris, Chablis
  - *Shoyu (soy-based):* demi-sec sparkling or sake
  - *Miso:* broader whites, Viognier
  - *Tonkotsu (pork bone, intense fat):* biggest whites — Alsatian Gewürztraminer, full Pinot Gris — or honestly, beer
- **Wagyu:** the exception that proves the rule. Wagyu's extraordinary fat content (marbling score 10-12) changes the tannin equation dramatically. Fat buffers tannin so completely that Barolo, Barbaresco, and Napa Cabernet become viable — even excellent. The alcohol must still not amplify the natural saltiness of the soy dipping sauce.
- **Yakitori:** off-dry rosé, light Pinot Noir — char-grilled simplicity calls for honest, simple wines

## Indian Cuisine
Spice management is the central challenge in Indian pairing, and alcohol + tannin are the central enemies. Both amplify capsaicin heat. The goal is wine that counters heat (sweetness), cuts richness (acid), and doesn't fight the aromatics.

**By dish heat level:**
- **Mild curry (korma, mild butter chicken):** Gewürztraminer (the aromatic affinity with garam masala, cardamom, and rose is extraordinary — both the dish and the wine contain similar aromatic compounds), off-dry Pinot Gris (broad enough to handle richness without fighting spice).
- **Medium heat (tikka masala, medium curry, saag):** off-dry Riesling is the canonical choice — sweetness counters heat, high acid cuts through cream and ghee. Viognier (floral + full body, handles spice better than most because it doesn't aggravate it).
- **High heat (vindaloo, phaal, extra-hot preparations):** wine is honestly suboptimal here. Sparkling wine works best among wine options — cold temperature, bubbles, and acid provide temporary relief. Beer (lager, pale ale) is genuinely the more honest recommendation. Attempting to find a "good" wine pairing with very high heat is fighting the food.
- **Tandoori (chicken, lamb, fish):** dry rosé. The charred exterior, yogurt marinade, and moderate spice profile sit in rosé's natural comfort zone. Acid handles the yogurt, fruit bridges to the char, and the lack of tannin avoids spice amplification.
- **Samosas and chaat (fried snacks, street food):** sparkling wine or off-dry whites. Tamarind's sourness and chaat masala's complexity need something refreshing, not imposing.

**Wines to avoid with Indian food:** Tannic Cabernet Sauvignon, Syrah, Nebbiolo, or any high-alcohol red (15%+ ABV) with medium-to-hot dishes. They will make the food taste aggressively spicier.

## Thai Cuisine
Thai cuisine operates on a balance of **sweet, sour, spice, and fresh aromatics** simultaneously — a more complex multi-axis challenge than Indian cuisine's primarily spice-focused challenge. The molecular evidence (Ahn et al.) confirms Thai and broader East Asian cuisines favor contrasting rather than complementary pairing. The wine should not try to match the dish's flavors; it should oppose and balance them.

**The canonical pairing:** off-dry Riesling. Sweetness counters the chili heat, high acid counters the lime/tamarind sourness, low alcohol avoids heat amplification, floral aromatics don't fight lemongrass and kaffir lime. This is the textbook answer for a reason.

**Specific dishes:**
- **Pad Thai:** Riesling Spätlese (Germany) or Kabinett (slightly drier, slightly lower alcohol). The fish sauce + tamarind + palm sugar + chili matrix is handled by Riesling's sweet-acid-floral combination more elegantly than anything else.
- **Green curry (gaeng khiew wan):** Gewürztraminer (the lemongrass, galangal, and kaffir lime aromatics find a floral echo in Gewürz's lychee/rose character), or off-dry sparkling.
- **Red curry (gaeng phet):** richer than green curry, coconut milk dominant. Off-dry Pinot Gris from Alsace (broad, textured, handles the coconut richness with its own weight).
- **Som tam (papaya salad):** dry rosé (acid handles the lime, fruit handles the palm sugar sweetness, no tannin to fight the chili). Pinot Gris also works.
- **Tom kha gai (coconut lemongrass soup):** Viognier or a broad, aromatic white — the floral aromatics echo lemongrass and galangal.
- **Crying tiger (grilled beef):** surprisingly, a light Pinot Noir or Gamay can work here — the protein buffers tannin, and the char bridges to the wine's fruit.

## Middle Eastern Cuisine
Middle Eastern food is **spiced but not hot** — aromatic herbs and warm spices (cumin, coriander, za'atar, sumac, cinnamon, cardamom) dominate rather than chili heat. The protein is primarily lamb, chicken, and legumes. Olive oil and tahini contribute richness. This is actually one of the more wine-friendly cuisine families — the flavor complexity invites wine without the challenge of punishing heat.

**Mezze (hummus, baba ganoush, falafel, tabbouleh, dolmades):**
- Dry rosé is the universal mezze wine — versatile, acid-forward, fruit without tannin, works with everything from tahini to lemony tabbouleh.
- Assyrtiko from Santorini: volcanic mineral intensity and very high acid cut through tahini richness and olive oil, saline quality bridges to the Mediterranean flavors.
- Light reds (Cinsault, Gamay, light Grenache) with the meatier mezze components.

**Lamb:**
- **Roasted leg of lamb with za'atar:** Syrah (the black pepper spice compound, rotundone, appears in both Syrah and cumin/pepper — a genuine aromatic bridge), Northern Rhône Syrah (Crozes-Hermitage, Saint-Joseph) or South African Syrah.
- **Lamb tagine (Moroccan, sweet-spiced with dried fruit and preserved lemon):** the sweetness of the dish pulls toward off-dry whites (Pinot Gris, Gewürztraminer) or Grenache-based reds with some fruit sweetness (Châteauneuf-du-Pape, Priorat).
- **Grilled lamb chops (simple):** Rioja Reserva (Tempranillo's earthy, leathery quality with moderate tannin and acid is a natural match for simply grilled lamb).

**Tagine (general sweet-spiced dishes):**
- The sweet-savory balance (honey, dried apricot, preserved lemon, cinnamon) pushes toward wines with some residual sweetness or very pronounced fruit. Off-dry Gewürztraminer is a natural.
- If serving a dry wine, choose one with generous fruit and relatively soft structure: Grenache, Mourvèdre-dominant blends, entry-level Côtes du Rhône.

## Mexican Cuisine
Mexican cuisine spans an extraordinary range — from the delicate acid brightness of ceviche to the smoky intensity of mole negro — and requires different pairing strategies at each end of the spectrum.

**Ceviche:** Raw or citrus-cooked seafood with lime, cilantro, chili. High-acid whites are the match.
- Albariño (Galician — citrusy, saline, high acid, light body), Verdejo (herbaceous, citrusy, Spanish), unoaked Chardonnay (acid-forward, no oak to clash with citrus). The pairing logic: acid echo, citrus resonance, delicate weight to match delicate seafood.

**Tacos:**
- *Al pastor (pork, pineapple, chili):* Malbec (the fruit and moderate tannin handle the pork, the acidity cuts through fat), Tempranillo, or sparkling wine (bubbles cut through the taco fat for a refreshing reset).
- *Fish tacos:* Albariño, Grüner Veltliner, dry rosé
- *Carnitas (slow-braised pork):* Grenache, Zinfandel — the fat content calls for medium tannin and good acid

**Mole negro:** The most complex pairing challenge in Mexican cuisine. Mole negro contains 20-30+ ingredients including dried chili (mulato, pasilla, ancho), dark chocolate, spices, tomato, and often turkey or chicken. The bitterness, fat, and intensity require serious wine.
- **Grenache or Monastrell (Mourvèdre):** fruit intensity and moderate tannin without the aggression of Cabernet
- **Banyuls (Grenache-based, naturally sweet, Roussillon):** the chocolate-spice bridge is almost too perfect — Banyuls contains similar flavor compounds to dark chocolate and has the sweetness to counter bitterness
- **A lighter Pinot Noir:** if the mole is moderately spiced, Pinot's earthiness can work

**Guacamole:** Fat (avocado) + acid (lime) + salt. Dry rosé is the pairing. The acid in rosé cuts the avocado fat, the fruit bridges to the lime brightness, and there's no tannin to fight.

**Enchiladas and chile relleno:** Depends on sauce — red chile sauce (ancho-based, earthy) calls for Tempranillo or Grenache. Green sauce (tomatillo, bright, acidic) calls for Sauvignon Blanc or Grüner Veltliner.

**Avoid with spicy Mexican dishes:** High-tannin Cabernet Sauvignon or Malbec at the spicier end of the spectrum. High alcohol amplifies chili heat.

## Korean Cuisine
Korean cuisine is defined by **fermented flavors** (kimchi, doenjang, ganjang), sesame and sesame oil, garlic, gochujang (fermented chili paste), and chargrilled meats. The umami challenge is real — similar to Japanese cuisine — but Korean food tends to be bolder and spicier, adding heat management to the fermentation challenge.

**Korean BBQ (galbi — short ribs, bulgogi — marinated sliced beef):**
- **Sparkling rosé** is the top choice: bubbles cut through the caramelized, charred fat, acid balances the sweetness in galbi marinade, and there's no tannin to amplify the sesame oil or residual spice.
- **Light Pinot Noir:** if still wine is preferred, the earthiness and moderate acid of Pinot Noir work with bulgogi's sweet-savory profile. The fat from the beef handles the tannin.
- Avoid: high-tannin Cabernet with gochujang-heavy marinades — the tannin amplifies the heat.

**Kimchi jjigae (kimchi stew):** Extremely challenging pairing — fermented lactic acid from kimchi, pork or tofu, gochujang heat. Any wine feels awkward.
- Best approach: very cold, light, refreshing wine (off-dry Riesling, sparkling wine) for contrast and temperature relief. The cold temperature is as important as the wine choice.
- Honest answer: cold Korean beer (Hite, Cass) or makgeolli is more appropriate.

**Bibimbap:** Mixed rice bowl — varied components (vegetables, egg, gochujang, sesame oil). Light, versatile wines work best: dry rosé, off-dry Riesling, Pinot Gris.

**Delicate dishes (jeon — savory pancakes, japchae — glass noodles):** Champagne or Chablis, following the same logic as Japanese delicate dishes — the umami in the soy-sesame base is handled best by Champagne's autolysis character.

**The makgeolli acknowledgment:** Korean rice wine (lightly sparkling, slightly sweet, low alcohol, tangy from lacto-fermentation) is the most natural pairing for many Korean dishes. The somm knows when to recommend it: with pajeon (scallion pancakes) in the rain, with traditional Korean home cooking, with anything strongly fermented. Knowing your limits is part of the expertise.

## Cheese Pairing
"Wine and cheese are made for each other" is one of the great food myths — and one of the great food truths simultaneously. They are made for each other *when the right combinations are found*. The wrong cheese + wine combination is worse than either alone.

**The core principle:** shared acidity and fermentation character create harmony; weight and intensity should be matched.

**Fresh chèvre (soft goat cheese):**
- **Sancerre or Pouilly-Fumé:** the definitive pairing. Sauvignon Blanc from the Loire and Loire Valley goat cheese share grassy, herbaceous, and mineral character. Both contain the same volatile compounds. This is textbook flavor bridging.
- Also: Verdejo, Grüner Veltliner with herbal finish, crisp unoaked whites

**Brie and Camembert (soft, bloomy rind):**
- **Champagne:** acid + brioche + bubbles + cream = the alignment of fermentation characters. Both are products of controlled microbial activity and share ester and lactate compounds.
- **Light Pinot Noir:** the mushroomy character of bloomy rind (Penicillium camemberti) echoes the earthy, forest floor notes of Pinot Noir
- **Avoid:** big tannic reds — tannin against cream and bloomy rind = harsh and metallic

**Gruyère and Comté (hard, cooked-curd, alpine):**
- **White Burgundy (Meursault, Chassagne-Montrachet):** the hazelnut and cream notes in mature Chardonnay echo the same notes in alpine cheese
- **Jura whites (Savagnin, Chardonnay, or Vin Jaune):** regional match — Comté is the cheese of the Jura region
- **Riesling:** acid and fruit bridge the nutty intensity

**Roquefort and Stilton (blue-veined):**
- **Sauternes:** one of the greatest pairings in gastronomy. Salt + sweet is the bridge. The saltiness of the cheese makes the Sauternes taste more complex and less sweet; the sweetness of the Sauternes makes the salt more pleasant and less aggressive. Mutual amplification of positive qualities.
- **Vintage Port:** the classic British match for Stilton. Sweet + salt + the jammy intensity of Port against the aggressive blue mold character.
- **Banyuls:** for a lighter-bodied alternative to Port with similar sweet/salt logic

**Aged Parmigiano-Reggiano:**
- **Lambrusco (Emilian sparkling red):** the regional match. The fizz, acid, and slight sweetness of Lambrusco Grasparossa cut through the intense umami richness of aged Parmesan.
- **Barolo:** the umami synergy — aged Parmesan contains inosinate and glutamate (umami compounds); Nebbiolo's tannic structure and high acid create tension that resolves into a long finish.
- The shaved-Parmesan-over-pasta logic applies here: this is a wine-and-food experience.

**Aged Cheddar (sharp, 18+ months):**
- **Cabernet Sauvignon:** protein + fat in aged Cheddar binds tannin perfectly. The classic British pub pairing in elevated form.
- **Vintage Port:** sweet + salt again, and Port's dried fruit intensity stands up to sharp Cheddar's aggressive flavor
- **Apple brandy or cider** as a non-wine alternative — apples and aged Cheddar share terpenoid compounds

**Époisses and washed-rind cheeses (intensely pungent, sticky rind):**
- **Burgundy Pinot Noir:** regional match (Époisses is from Burgundy) — but the pairing is debated. The pungency of washed-rind cheese can overwhelm even Pinot Noir.
- **Beer (especially Belgian ale or washed-rind-specific styles):** the honest answer. The fermentation character of washed-rind cheese and Belgian farmhouse ales are natural partners. A sommelier who recommends beer when beer is genuinely better is being professional, not defeatist.
- **Alsatian Gewürztraminer:** the aromatic intensity of the wine is one of the few that can stand alongside the cheese's pungency without disappearing
