# Deductive Grid — Quick Reference Schema

A structured decision reference for the CMS Deductive Tasting Grid. Use this for rapid lookup during blind tasting practice, exam preparation, or when working through the SKILL.md requires faster reference access.

---

## Grid Nodes — Complete Enumerated Options

### SIGHT

**Clarity**
- Clear
- Hazy

**Brightness**
- Dull
- Bright
- Day-Bright
- Brilliant

**Color — White**
- Water-white → Very young, neutral variety, reductive winemaking
- Straw → Young, fresh, unoaked
- Yellow → Some age or ripeness, possible light oak
- Gold → Oak aging, late harvest, botrytis, or 3–8+ years age
- Amber → Oxidative aging, orange wine, or significant age
- Brown → Deep age, PX/Tawny style, or oxidation fault

**Color — Red**
- Purple → Very young (< 3 years), high anthocyanin (Syrah, Malbec, Zinfandel)
- Ruby → Youthful to mid-aged; broad category
- Garnet → Some development, translucency increasing
- Tawny → Significant age or oxidative aging (Nebbiolo, Port)
- Brown → Advanced age, heat damage, or heavy oxidation

**Rim Variation**
- No variation → Young
- Slight rim paling → Mid-age; 3–6 years depending on variety
- Orange/brick rim → Mature; 6–12+ years
- Wide tawny rim → Significantly aged or oxidative

**Viscosity / Legs**
- Thick, slow legs → Higher alcohol and/or RS
- Thin, fast legs → Lower alcohol
- NOTE: Legs do NOT indicate quality. Never use as a quality assessment.

**Other**
- Persistent fine bubbles → Traditional method sparkling
- Large/irregular bubbles → Tank method or carbonation
- Bubbles in still wine → Possible refermentation or intentional pétillance
- Sediment (powdery/tartrate) → Cold instability, not a fault
- Sediment (dark/chunky) → Age-derived tannin polymers in red wine; expected in aged wines
- Staining above wine line → High extract, concentrated wine

---

### NOSE

**Condition**
- Clean → Proceed to intensity
- Flawed → STOP. Route to fault-diagnosis. Identify descriptor before continuing.
  - Wet cardboard / damp basement → TCA (cork taint)
  - Vinegar / nail polish → Volatile acidity / ethyl acetate
  - Rotten eggs / garlic / struck match → Reduction (H2S)
  - Bandaid / barnyard / horse stable → Brettanomyces
  - Cooked / jammy / brick color → Heat damage
  - Excess sulphur / matchstick → SO2
  - Geranium leaf → Geranium taint (sorbate + LAB)
  - Musty / mouse cage → Mousiness (THP compounds)

**Intensity**
- Delicate → Cool climate, neutral variety, or aged (aromas fading)
- Moderate → Most mid-range wines; standard presentation
- Powerful → Warm climate, concentrated variety, or great terroir

**Development**
- Youthful → Primary aromatics dominant; fresh
- Vinous → "Winey" rather than specifically fruity; transition
- Developing → Secondary/tertiary emerging alongside primary
- Complex → All three tiers present; full evolution
- Tired → Aromatics faded, oxidized, or flat; past window

**Fruit Condition**
- Tart → Cool climate, early harvest, high acid
- Ripe → Optimal ripeness, moderate climate
- Overripe → Warm climate, late harvest
- Jammy → Very warm or very ripe; cooked berry
- Candied → RS influence or very ripe
- Baked → Heat stress or storage damage
- Dried → Age or dried-grape winemaking (Amarone, Passito)
- Bruised → Oxidative or brett-compromised fruit

---

### PALATE

**Sweetness**
- Bone Dry: < 1 g/L RS
- Dry: 1–4 g/L RS
- Off-Dry: 4–12 g/L RS
- Medium-Dry: 12–45 g/L RS
- Medium-Sweet: 45–120 g/L RS
- Sweet: 120–220 g/L RS
- Dessert: 220+ g/L RS

**Tannin (red wines)**
- Low | Med- | Medium | Med+ | High
- Nature: fine-grained / silky / chalky / grippy / drying / green / ripe / coarse

**Acidity**
- Low | Med- | Medium | Med+ | High
- Assess via salivation under tongue after swallowing

**Alcohol**
- Low: < 11% ABV
- Med-: 11–12.5%
- Medium: 12.5–13.5%
- Med+: 13.5–14.5%
- High: 14.5%+
- Assess via heat on throat finish

**Body**
- Light / Medium- / Medium / Medium+ / Full
- Reference: Skim milk → whole milk → heavy cream

**Texture**
- Creamy | Silky | Grippy | Oily/waxy | Tingly | Astringent

**Finish Length**
- Short: < 30 seconds
- Medium: 30–45 seconds
- Long: > 45 seconds
- Very Long: > 60 seconds

## Diagnostic Implications — Key Observations

**"Garnet color with orange/brick rim"**
→ Wine has significant age. For Nebbiolo/Barolo: likely 8–15 years. For Pinot Noir: likely 6–10 years. For Cabernet: likely 8–12+ years. Expect developing to complex nose; softened tannins; possible tertiary aromas.

**"Purple color, opaque core, no rim variation"**
→ Very young wine, probably < 3 years. Expect primary fruit dominant. Varieties: likely Syrah, Malbec, Zinfandel, or young Cabernet. Do not expect complexity; structural markers will be aggressive (grippy tannin, high acid unintegrated).

**"High tannin (grippy) + purple color + dark fruit"**
→ Young tannic red. Narrow to: Cabernet Sauvignon, Syrah, Malbec, Aglianico, or Sagrantino. Structural evidence outweighs color alone. Check nose: cassis = Cabernet; dark berry + pepper = Syrah; blueberry/plum = Malbec.

**"High tannin (chalky) + garnet-tawny + dried rose + tar"**
→ Classic Nebbiolo. Barolo or Barbaresco. Assess age from color development and nose (dried rose + tar + truffle = mature). Chalky tannin is the key differentiator from other high-tannin reds.

**"High acid + no tannin + petrol/lime + off-dry to dry"**
→ Riesling. Climate from acidity level and alcohol: high acid + low alcohol = Mosel/Germany; high acid + lime + mineral = Clare Valley or Alsace; petrol intensity increases with age. Off-dry = Spätlese; bone dry = Trocken.

**"Pale garnet, translucent, light body, earthy, forest floor, red fruit"**
→ Pinot Noir in maturity. Old World (earthy, foresty) vs New World (red fruit forward). Check tannin: silky and fine = Burgundy or aged; slightly grippy and fruit-forward = Oregon, New Zealand, or younger Burgundy.

**"Vanilla + coconut + dark fruit + Med+ tannin"**
→ American oak influence. New World red or Spanish Rioja/Ribera in new American oak. Cabernet or Tempranillo most likely. Coconut is the diagnostic oak marker for new American oak (oak lactone).

**"High acid + straw color + green apple + flinty/mineral + lean body"**
→ Unoaked Chardonnay from cool climate. Chablis is the prime candidate (Kimmeridgian chalk → flinty note). Also possible: Mâcon, Burgundy Blanc, or cool-climate New World unoaked Chardonnay.

**"Brioche + cream + fine persistent bubbles + yeasty"**
→ Traditional method sparkling wine; lees/autolysis contact. Champagne (brioche = extended autolysis); also Crémant, Cava, Franciacorta, English sparkling. Quality of bubbles (fine, persistent) confirms traditional method.

**"Bandaid + barnyard at low level + fruit intact + complexity"**
→ Low-level Brett. This is a flaw, not a fault at this level. Many classic Old World wines (Châteauneuf, some Bordeaux, some Barolo) show low Brett as a complexity element. Assess: does it add or detract? Route to fault-diagnosis for threshold determination.

## The Conclusion Decision Tree

Work through each branch in order. Each confirmed answer narrows the field.

```
START: Assess Color
│
├─ White/Rosé → White wine pathway
│   ├─ Acid high + floral white + low alcohol → RIESLING
│   │   └─ Add petrol + mineral → Confirm. Climate from acid/alcohol balance.
│   ├─ Acid high + grassy + grapefruit/passion fruit → SAUVIGNON BLANC
│   │   └─ Pyrazine present → Cool climate. Absent → Warm climate or oaked.
│   ├─ Stone fruit + white flower + full body + low acid → VIOGNIER
│   ├─ Lemon/apple + varying acid/oak + versatile → CHARDONNAY
│   │   ├─ No oak + high acid + mineral → Chablis
│   │   ├─ Butter + toast + full → Oaked New World or Meursault
│   ├─ Rose water + lychee + spice + low acid → GEWÜRZTRAMINER
│   └─ Mineral + waxy + high acid + quince → CHENIN BLANC or GRÜNER VELTLINER
│       └─ White pepper signature → GRÜNER VELTLINER confirmed
│
└─ Red wine pathway
    ├─ Light body + high acid + silky tannin + red fruit + earthy
    │   └─ PINOT NOIR → Old World = earthy; New World = fruit-forward
    ├─ Light body + low tannin + red fruit + floral + juicy
    │   └─ GAMAY (Beaujolais)
    ├─ Med-high body + grippy tannin (Med+) + cassis + cedar/tobacco
    │   └─ CABERNET SAUVIGNON
    │       ├─ Cedar + restrained → Left Bank Bordeaux
    │       ├─ Ripe cassis + eucalyptus → Napa or Coonawarra
    ├─ High tannin + chalky + dried rose + tar + garnet-tawny
    │   └─ NEBBIOLO → Barolo (more power) / Barbaresco (more elegance)
    ├─ High acid + grippy tannin + cherry + earthy + iron
    │   └─ SANGIOVESE → Chianti (lighter) / Brunello (fuller) / Montalcino
    ├─ Dark fruit + white/black pepper + full body + spice
    │   └─ SYRAH/SHIRAZ
    │       ├─ White pepper + olive + earthy → Northern Rhône (Syrah)
    │       ├─ Blackberry + chocolate + warm → Barossa (Shiraz)
    ├─ Blueberry + plum + low acid + full body
    │   └─ MALBEC (Mendoza)
    ├─ Red fruit + garrigue + low tannin + spice + warm finish
    │   └─ GRENACHE → Southern Rhône, Priorat, Sardinia
    └─ Jam + high alcohol + pepper + blackberry
        └─ ZINFANDEL / PRIMITIVO
```

## Common Misreads and How to Avoid Them

**Misread 1: "The wine has great legs, so it must be high quality."**
Correction: Legs/tears indicate alcohol and sugar content via the Marangoni effect. They are not a quality marker. A cheap high-alcohol wine will have pronounced legs. Never use this as a compliment or evidence of quality.

**Misread 2: "This wine is tannic, so it must be Cabernet."**
Correction: Many grapes produce high tannin — Nebbiolo, Aglianico, Sagrantino, Malbec, Syrah, and young Tempranillo can all present high tannin. Always assess tannin nature alongside level. Chalky = Nebbiolo/Sangiovese; Grippy/firm = Cabernet; Powdery = limestone-grown varieties.

**Misread 3: "High acid means cool climate."**
Correction: High acid is a strong cool-climate indicator, but acid can be added in winemaking (acidification), and some warm-climate varieties (Barbera, Montepulciano) retain high acid despite warm origins. Always triangulate with alcohol and fruit character. High acid + low alcohol + lean body + herbal = cool climate. High acid + high alcohol + jammy fruit = likely acidified.

**Misread 4: "The color is deep purple, so this is a powerful wine."**
Correction: Color depth reflects anthocyanin concentration, which is grape-variety dependent as much as ripeness. Malbec, Syrah, and Sagrantino are deeply colored varieties even at moderate ripeness. Nebbiolo is lightly colored even in a powerful wine. Never use color alone to infer power or quality.

**Misread 5: "That earthy note means the wine is flawed or 'dirty'."**
Correction: Earthiness (forest floor, leaf litter, iron, chalk) is a legitimate and often prized terroir-derived quality in Old World wines. It is a primary marker of Burgundy Pinot Noir, aged Bordeaux, Barolo, and Rioja. Only flag as a fault if the earthiness is combined with TCA, geosmin, or brett at fault-level concentrations.

**Misread 6: "The wine smells buttery, so it's a New World Chardonnay."**
Correction: Butter/cream is a diacetyl marker from malolactic fermentation — this occurs in both Old and New World wines. White Burgundy (Meursault, Puligny-Montrachet) undergoes full MLF and shows butter/cream notes. The distinction is integration: Old World butter is woven into a mineral, complex framework; New World butter may be more isolated and dominant.

**Misread 7: "This wine is complex, so it must be high quality."**
Correction: Complexity is a quality indicator but not a sufficient one. A wine can be complex and unbalanced (e.g., tertiary development present but alcohol is hot and finish is harsh). Apply the full BLIC framework (Balance, Length, Intensity, Complexity) before assigning quality. Balance is the primary constraint — complexity without balance is not outstanding quality.

## Tim Gaiser MS — The 5 Diagnostic "Tells"

Master Sommelier Tim Gaiser, who helped design the CMS deductive framework, articulates that experienced tasters use five diagnostic signals that — when correctly identified — narrow a wine faster than any other approach:

**Tell 1: Acid Level and Type**
Acid is the single most diagnostic structural element. It determines climate, style, and narrows grape candidates dramatically. Before anything else: is this a high-acid or low-acid wine? High acid = cool climate or cool-climate variety; low acid = warm climate. Then: is it "electric" (piercing, almost sharp = Riesling, Grüner, Champagne) or "soft and round" (Viognier, warm-climate reds)?

**Tell 2: The Tannin–Fruit Interaction**
In red wines, how do the tannins relate to the fruit? If the tannins are more prominent than the fruit (they dominate the mid-palate and finish), the wine is likely: young, or from a variety where tannin is the structural engine (Nebbiolo, Cabernet). If the fruit and tannin are in balance, the wine is in its window. If the fruit has outlasted the tannins (silky texture, dried fruit character), it is mature or past peak.

**Tell 3: Color + Development Alignment**
The color should match the development stage. Purple + complex tertiary nose = something is wrong — either the development assessment or the color assessment. Garnet-tawny + youthful primary fruit = unusual — check for heat damage (which can mimic age in color but not in aromatics). When color and development align, you have reliable evidence. When they diverge, reason through the anomaly.

**Tell 4: The Signature Aroma**
Almost every great grape variety has one aroma that functions as a fingerprint:
- Petrol/kerosene → Riesling (TDN)
- Cassis/blackcurrant leaf → Cabernet Sauvignon
- Tar + dried rose → Nebbiolo
- Violet → Malbec or Cabernet Franc
- White pepper → Grüner Veltliner or Northern Rhône Syrah
- Lychee + rose water → Gewürztraminer
- Struck match / gunflint → Muscadet or Sancerre (terroir-derived); also reduction
- Brioche + autolytic yeast → Traditional method sparkling

When you detect the signature aroma, work backward to confirm with structure. Do not call the grape from the signature alone — confirm with the grid.

**Tell 5: The Finish Quality**
The finish reveals more about quality, terroir, and vintage than almost any other single data point. A long, clean, evolving finish that shifts from fruit to mineral to savory and then to dry earth or spice is the mark of great wine and great terroir. A finish that cuts off abruptly, turns bitter, or is dominated by alcohol or tannin is a quality flag regardless of how impressive the attack was. Always assess: how long, how clean, what does it finish on, and does it evolve or stay static?
