# Greece and Georgia — Regional Reference

### Greece

Greece is ancient wine country — the vine may have been cultivated in Greece continuously for 4,000 years. But for most of the 20th century, Greek wine was either consumed locally or exported as cheap bulk. The quality revolution began in the late 1980s, driven by a generation of winemakers trained in France and returning with ambitions to produce world-class wine from indigenous varieties.

**Assyrtiko (Santorini PDO):** The most internationally acclaimed Greek variety. Grown on the volcanic island of Santorini (Thira), where the soils are pure pumice and ash over basalt — genuinely porous, impossibly difficult farming, no phylloxera (the louse cannot move through the fine pumice). Vines are trained in a basket shape (*kouloura*) low to the ground, the baskets touching the soil to protect against the fierce Meltemi winds. No irrigation is possible; the vines absorb moisture from sea fog and morning dew through the vine and soil.

**Character:** Electrically high acidity, citrus (lemon, lime), saline mineral, volcanic smoke, and with age (10–20 years) extraordinary complexity — waxy, honeyed, lanolin notes develop while the acid remains vital. The *nykteri* style (night-harvested for extra freshness, barrel-fermented) adds textural richness. One of the world's most distinctive white wines. Sigalas, Hadzidakis, Gaia.

**Xinomavro (Naoussa PDO, Rapsani PDO, Amyndeon PDO):** Greece's most important red variety. From Macedonia in northern Greece. Named for its characteristics: *xino* (sour/acid) + *mavro* (black). High tannin, high acid, pale color — frequently compared to Nebbiolo in structure and ageability, though the flavor profile (tomato, olive, spice, dried fruit) is uniquely Greek. Needs significant aging; great Naoussa from 10+ years is revelatory. Kir-Yianni, Thymiopoulos, Boutari.

**Other significant Greek varieties:** Moschofilero (pink-skinned, rose-scented, for Mantinia PDO), Muscat of Patras and Samos (sweet, one of the world's great dessert wine traditions), Agiorgitiko (Nemea PDO — "the Blood of Hercules"; rich, velvety Peloponnesian red).

### Georgia

Georgia's claim to be the birthplace of wine is among the most credible in the archaeology of fermentation — evidence of grape cultivation and winemaking dates to approximately 6,000–8,000 BCE, making the South Caucasus region the oldest known wine-producing culture. The country has over 500 indigenous grape varieties registered.

**Qvevri winemaking (UNESCO Intangible Heritage 2013):** The traditional Georgian method uses *qvevri* — large clay amphorae (50–3,500 liters) buried in the earth up to their necks. The grapes — white or red — are fermented in the *qvevri* with skins and stems for extended maceration (weeks to months). The buried clay maintains a constant cool temperature during fermentation; after maceration, the *qvevri* is sealed with beeswax and the wine ages underground for months to years. The extended skin contact on white wines produces the "amber wine" or "orange wine" — white wine with tannin, bronze-amber color, and extraordinary textural complexity, quite unlike any other wine category.

**Key varieties:**
- *Rkatsiteli:* The most widely planted white variety in Georgia. Dry, high-acid, herbal and citrus character when made conventionally; in qvevri skin-contact style, develops dried apricot, tea, walnut, oxidative complexity.
- *Saperavi:* Georgia's flagship red. Deep, almost inky purple-black color; intense dark fruit, tobacco, plum, with firm tannin and high acid. Exceptional age-worthiness. Used for both conventional dry table wine and the sweet *Kindzmarauli* style (named for a famous *microzone* in Kakheti).
- *Mtsvane:* Aromatic white variety often blended with Rkatsiteli.

**Key regions:**
- *Kakheti:* The dominant wine region in eastern Georgia, responsible for 70%+ of production. Warm, continental, protected by the Greater Caucasus mountains.
- *Kartli:* Central Georgia, cooler, lighter wines.
- *Racha-Lechkhumi:* Northwest Georgia, known for naturally semi-sweet wines including Khvanchkara.
- *Imereti:* Western Georgia, where partial qvevri skin contact (less extreme than Kakheti) is traditional.

**The natural wine movement's rediscovery:** International natural wine interest in the early 2010s brought significant attention to traditional Georgian qvevri wines, with producers like Pheasant's Tears (John Wurdeman), Iago's Wine (Iago Bitarishvili), and COS (Sicilian producers making qvevri wines in Italy from Sicilian varieties) bringing the style to global wine markets.
