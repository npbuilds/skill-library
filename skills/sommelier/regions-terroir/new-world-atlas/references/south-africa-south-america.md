# South Africa and South America — Regional Reference

## South Africa

South Africa's wine culture is layered with history, complexity, and ongoing transformation. The industry had significant international participation until 1994 — the apartheid era's international isolation stunted export development — but Cape wine has a four-century history, with Constantia's sweet wines the most famous luxury product of the 18th-century world. Since 1994, Cape wine has been reintegrating into global markets with some extraordinary results.

### Stellenbosch WO (Wine of Origin)

The epicenter of South African fine wine production. A diverse mosaic of soils and aspects within 50km of Cape Town: decomposed granite on the hillsides, lower-fertility Hutton red soils on the benchlands, sandy coastal soils toward False Bay. The combination of Atlantic Ocean cooling, altitude, and diverse geology produces wines of genuine complexity.

**Cabernet Sauvignon:** The historical flagship variety, and still dominant in blends. Stellenbosch Cab has a distinctive character: red and black fruit, a dusty/earthy undertone (sometimes called "Cape dust"), well-integrated oak, firm structure. Not as ripe and opulent as Napa; not as austere as young Bordeaux.

**Chenin Blanc:** The most planted white variety in South Africa (planted there as "Steen" in the 17th century). Historically used for bulk wine and distillation; now increasingly recognized as South Africa's distinctive white grape expression. In Stellenbosch and Franschhoek, old-vine Chenin on decomposed granite can produce serious, textured, age-worthy whites with quince, beeswax, and hay character.

**Key producers:** Kanonkop (the benchmark for Pinotage — South Africa's own crossing of Pinot Noir and Cinsault, developed at Stellenbosch University in 1925; Kanonkop Paul Sauer is also a brilliant Bordeaux-blend), Meerlust (the founding estate for serious Cape Bordeaux blends; Rubicon has been produced since 1980), Vergelegen, Warwick Estate, Rustenberg.

**The Eben Sadie influence:** Eben Sadie's Sadie Family Wines (based in Swartland but with major Stellenbosch sourcing) has been enormously influential in raising standards and changing the conversation toward site-specificity, old vines, and minimal intervention.

### Swartland WO

The revolution. Northwest of Stellenbosch, the Swartland ("Black Land" — named for the dark Renosterbos shrub that once covered the plains) is wheat country and, since the 2000s, ground zero for South Africa's most exciting wine development. The key figure: Eben Sadie, who began farming old-vine Chenin Blanc and Cinsault in the Swartland's granite hills in the late 1990s when the region was ignored.

**Why Swartland?** Old vines (Chenin Blanc, Cinsault, Grenache, Mourvèdre, some Palomino) on decomposed granite, planted without irrigation — "dryland farming" — forced by low annual rainfall and no available water. These stressed, unirrigated vines produce tiny crops (1–3 tonnes/ha) of extraordinary concentration. The Rhône varieties find in Swartland's warm, dry climate a close analog to the Southern Rhône. The philosophy: minimal intervention, no irrigation, whole-bunch or semi-carbonic fermentation, old neutral oak or concrete.

**Eben Sadie:** His two flagship wines — Columella (Syrah/Mourvèdre/Grenache Noir/Cinsault red blend) and Palladius (Chenin Blanc/Clairette/Viognier white blend from high-altitude granite) — established the idiom and the price benchmark. Now commands prices competitive with Burgundy Village level and above.

**A.A. Badenhorst Family Wines:** Adi Badenhorst and cousin Hein's estate on the Paardebosch farm in Perdeberg; Secateurs Chenin Blanc (the entry level) and the flagship red Badenhorst Blend (Shiraz, Grenache, Cinsault, Tinta Barocca) define the Swartland character at multiple price points.

**The Swartland Revolution:** An annual gathering (now suspended) of small Swartland producers that served as a manifesto and launching pad for a new generation: Chris and Andrea Mullineux (Mullineux & Leeu Family Wines), David Sadie, Callie Louw (Porseleinberg), Marc Kent (Boekenhoutskloof), Donovan Rall.

### Constantia WO

The oldest wine district in South Africa, directly below Table Mountain on the Cape Peninsula. Cool maritime climate (closest to the Atlantic). The Constantia Estate produced Vin de Constance — a sweet Muscat wine that was among the most famous luxury goods of the 18th century, praised by Dickens, Jane Austen, and Napoleon (who reportedly ordered it on St. Helena). Klein Constantia revived the original style in 1986 as Vin de Constance, from Muscat de Frontignan; the wine remains South Africa's most historically resonant sweet wine.

Contemporary Constantia dry whites (Sauvignon Blanc/Sémillon blends, Chardonnay) are fresh, precise, and mineral — among South Africa's finest.

## South America

### Argentina — Mendoza Province

Argentina is wine: 75% of the country's production comes from Mendoza Province at the eastern foot of the Andes. The combination of altitude (the defining factor), irrigation from snowmelt rivers, alluvial soils, and the unique climate of the high Andes has created a wine identity anchored on one grape variety — Malbec — while hiding extraordinary diversity in other varieties and sub-regions.

**Why altitude matters so much here:** The Mendoza wine regions range from 700m (Maipú, lower Luján de Cuyo) to 1,500m (Uco Valley) to 3,000m+ (Salta's Calchaquí Valley). At these elevations, the Winkler heat summation during summer is moderated by intense UV radiation and cold nights — the diurnal range of 15–25°C is among the widest in the world's wine regions. The result: grapes accumulate sugar (from warm days) while retaining extraordinary acid (from cold nights), producing wines with high alcohol potential but surprising freshness.

#### Malbec — An Argentine Redemption Story

Malbec (*Côt* in France, the dominant variety of Cahors) was a minor Bordeaux blending variety, famously difficult to grow in France (coulure susceptibility in cold springs, disease sensitivity). It was brought to Argentina in 1852 by Michel Pouget, a French agronomist invited by the Argentine government. In Mendoza's high-altitude, dry, sunny climate, Malbec found conditions it was never given in France: warmth to ripen fully, low disease pressure (low rainfall + irrigation control), and thin, well-drained soils on alluvial river cones.

The result: Argentina's Malbec has a character entirely different from French Côt. Richer, more opulent, with dark plum, violet, and chocolate rather than Cahors' more rustic iron and ink character.

**Luján de Cuyo:** The historical heartland of premium Mendoza Malbec. River cone alluvial soils (torrontés and limestone-clay mixes) at 900–1,100m. The "classic" style: full body, violet aromatics, plum and black cherry, integrated tannin, moderate acidity. Achaval Ferrer (Finca Altamira and Finca Bella Vista single-vineyard Malbecs are benchmarks), Catena Zapata (Adriano Catena was the pioneer of single-vineyard old-vine Malbec premiumization in the 1990s; Adrianna Vineyard at 1,500m in the Uco Valley is his crown jewel), Norton, Rutini.

**Uco Valley (Valle de Uco):** The future of Argentine fine wine. A large high-altitude valley south of Mendoza city, at 1,000–1,500m, centered on the towns of Tupungato, Tunuyán, and San Carlos. Soils here are different from Luján — more limestone, alluvial deposits from the Rio Tunuyán, volcanic ash in places. The Uco Valley's altitude produces Malbec and Cabernet Franc with a freshness and mineral precision that the lower Luján sites cannot match in warm years.

*Cheval des Andes:* A joint venture between Château Cheval Blanc (the Saint-Émilion legend) and Terrazas de Los Andes. Malbec and Cabernet Franc blend from the Uco Valley at altitude. The price and quality signal that Bordeaux is taking Argentine terroir seriously.

*Clos de los Siete:* Michel Rolland's collective project in the Uco Valley — seven producers sharing infrastructure; individual estates produce separate wines under the umbrella.

*Zuccardi Valle de Uco:* The most influential family winery in the current Uco Valley, with an extraordinary commitment to site-specificity and minimal intervention. Their Concreto Malbec (concrete-fermented, no new oak) and single-parcel Aluvional, Calcáreo, and Granito wines demonstrate the Uco Valley's soil diversity.

**Salta — High-Altitude Extremes:** The northwestern province of Salta contains the world's highest commercially cultivated wine vineyards, particularly in the Calchaquí Valley around the town of Cafayate (1,700m) and at even higher elevations (up to 3,111m at Finca El Fin del Mundo in Luracatao). The local white variety Torrontés (unique to Argentina, a crossing of Muscat Blanc à Petits Grains with Criolla Chica) produces intensely aromatic, floral, peach-scented dry whites that are Argentina's most distinctive white wine contribution. Colomé (one of the world's highest wineries, owned by the Swiss collector Donald Hess), El Esteco, Clos de Chacras.

### Chile

Chile's viticultural geography is one of the world's most extreme. A ribbon of land never wider than 200km, squeezed between the Pacific Ocean, the Andes, the Atacama Desert in the north, and Antarctica to the south. The combination of geographic isolation (the Atacama, Andes, Southern Ocean, and Pacific form near-perfect natural quarantine) has given Chile one of the world's most disease-free viticultural environments: no phylloxera (vines are ungrafted), minimal fungal disease (Pacific cold currents and coastal winds are dry), limited need for pesticide intervention.

**Carménère — Chile's Misidentified Grape:** In 1994, French ampelographer Jean-Michel Boursiquot discovered that much of the "Merlot" planted in Chilean vineyards was in fact Carménère — a Bordeaux variety believed extinct in France after phylloxera, but surviving in Chile under the wrong name since the mid-19th century. Carménère (deeper color, more herbaceous, higher pyrazine content, red spice character) was then identified as Chile's unique indigenous variety, differentiated from Merlot in ampelography and subsequently developed as Chile's signature red. Colchagua Valley (warm, dry) produces the finest Carménère expressions.

#### Central Valley Zones (North to South)

*Maipo Valley:* Chile's most prestigious appellation; immediately east of Santiago; foothills of the Andes. The Alto Maipo sub-zone, in the Andean foothills above Santiago at 700–900m, has a distinctive rocky-alluvial character ideal for Cabernet Sauvignon. Thin soils on decomposed granite force vine stress; the altitude moderates heat.

*Don Melchor (Concha y Toro):* Chile's most internationally acclaimed Cabernet Sauvignon; from a single vineyard in Puente Alto, Alto Maipo. Since 1987, produced as a single-estate prestige bottling. Now competes seriously with mid-level Napa and Bordeaux classified growths.

*Almaviva:* Joint venture between Concha y Toro and Mouton Rothschild (Baron Philippe de Rothschild S.A.) — the most explicit Bordeaux/Chile cross-pollination. Cabernet Sauvignon/Carménère/Cabernet Franc blend from Puente Alto.

*Casablanca Valley and San Antonio Valley (Leyda):* Cool-climate Pacific coastal zones discovered in the 1990s when irrigation allowed planting in this previously dry area. Cold Humboldt Current upwelling keeps temperatures low; morning fog delays ripening. Chile's answer to Sonoma Coast or Santa Barbara: Sauvignon Blanc, Chardonnay, and Pinot Noir with genuine aromatic freshness and acid. Veramonte, Viña Casas del Bosque (Casablanca), Viña Leyda and Casa Marin (San Antonio/Leyda — the coldest, most fog-affected).

*Colchagua Valley:* Chile's premium red-wine destination; warm, dry Andean foothill valley; diverse soils (clay, gravel, sandy loam). Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot, Carménère, Syrah. Lapostolle (Casa Apalta — the flagship single-estate blend, primarily Carménère; conceived by Michel Rolland), Montes (Montes Alpha M — the prestige Cabernet), Viu Manent.

*País revival:* The País grape (Listán Prieto — the first grape planted throughout the Spanish Americas in the 16th century) survived in Chile in extreme old-vine condition (some vines 200+ years old), farmed by smallholders in the Bío-Bío, Itata, and Maule regions as an everyday wine grape. The natural wine movement discovered it: low-intervention, carbonic or partial-whole-bunch fermented, light-bodied, high-acid *pipeño* (the Chilean term for fresh, unaged País wines). Garage Wine Co., De Martino (Viejas Tinajas range — clay amphora-aged Cinsault, País, Muscat), Louis-Antoine Luyt. This is arguably Chile's most culturally authentic wine tradition and represents the country's most original contribution to the natural wine conversation.

*Itata and Bío-Bío Valleys:* Further south; cooler and wetter; traditional País and Moscatel planting. The frontier of Chilean fine wine discovery. Unfashionable soils (volcanic, granitic), old vines, forgotten grapes — exactly the profile that attracted attention in Swartland and the Douro a generation ago.
