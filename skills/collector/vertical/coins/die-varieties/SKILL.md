# Die Varieties — When the Variety Beats the Date

> **Type:** Knowledge
> **Suite:** The Collector
> **Axis:** Vertical (coins)
> **Parent:** coins

## What Die Varieties Are

A die variety is a specific characteristic on a coin that distinguishes it from other coins of the same date and mintmark, caused by:

- **Doubled die** — die was inscribed twice during production, creating doubled imagery
- **Repunched mint mark (RPM)** — mintmark was struck multiple times in different positions
- **Overdate** — old date partially visible under new date
- **Engraved variety** — minor design differences within a year
- **Strike variety** — known dies that produce specific consistent characteristics

Variety identification is its own discipline. Variety attribution comes from named specialists who catalog and assign numbers (FS-numbers for Fivaz-Stanton attributions; CONECA numbers for the Combined Organizations of Numismatic Error Collectors of America; VAM-numbers for Van Allen-Mallis Morgan dollar attributions).

## The Canonical Doubled Dies

### 1955 Doubled Die Obverse Lincoln Cent

The most-collected modern die variety. The obverse die was inscribed twice with a slight rotation, producing visibly doubled text and date. The variety:

- **Strong on date and "LIBERTY"** — visible doubling without magnification
- **Color matters** — RD examples are trophy; RB and BN are still major value
- **Common dates of the year** trade $1; the DDO trades $1,000-$50,000+ depending on grade

The 1955 DDO is the entry-level coin for die-variety collecting and the most-recognized example.

### 1969-S Doubled Die Obverse Lincoln Cent

Similar to 1955 but rarer. The 1969-S DDO is so rare that the Secret Service initially confiscated examples as counterfeit (until they were verified as genuine errors). Trades $25K-$200K+ depending on grade.

### 1972 Doubled Die Obverse Lincoln Cent

Strong doubling on "LIBERTY" and the date. Less rare than 1955 or 1969-S but still significantly more valuable than common 1972 cents.

### 1995 Doubled Die Obverse Lincoln Cent

Modern doubling visible without magnification. Common in collector hands; trades modestly above face value in lower grades.

## Repunched Mint Marks (RPM)

The mintmark was struck multiple times in slightly different positions, leaving visible "shadow" mintmarks. Major examples:

- **1909-S/S Indian Head Cent** — repunched S mintmark
- **1942/1 Mercury dime** — overdate (also could be classed as overdate variety)
- **1944-D/S Lincoln Cent** — D over S mintmark variety
- **1946-S/D Walking Liberty Half Dollar** — S over D mintmark

## Overdates

A new date was punched over an old date during die preparation. Major overdates:

- **1942/1 Mercury Dime** — the canonical 20th-century overdate
- **1918/7-D Buffalo Nickel** — major key variety
- **1942/1-D Mercury Dime** — companion to 1942/1
- **1909/8 Indian Head Cent** — overdate

Overdates are visible under magnification and sometimes without. They trade at significant premiums to the standard date.

## Morgan Dollar VAMs

Van Allen and Mallis catalog Morgan dollar die varieties exhaustively. Major Morgan VAMs:

- **VAM-3 1878 8 Tail Feathers** — first year Morgan with 8 tail feathers (changed to 7 mid-year)
- **VAM-1 1878 8TF/7TF** — overstrike of 7 tail feathers on 8 tail feathers
- **VAM-44 1888-O "Hot Lips"** — major doubled die obverse Morgan
- **VAM-1A 1899-O Micro O** — small mintmark variety

Morgan dollar specialists trade VAMs as their own market — the VAM number is the primary identifier, not just the date.

## Buffalo Nickel 3-Legged

**1937-D 3-Legged Buffalo Nickel** — a specific die was over-polished, removing the front leg of the buffalo. The result is a recognized error variety; trades multi-thousand dollars in any grade.

## Authentication

Die variety attribution requires:

1. **Visual confirmation** — under magnification, the variety should match published photographs (CONECA, ANACS, PCGS attribution photos)
2. **Service attribution** — PCGS, NGC, ANACS will note the variety on the slab label (e.g., "FS-101 DDO")
3. **Specialist consultation** for ambiguous cases — die-variety specialists at Heritage Auctions, Stack's Bowers, and dedicated variety dealers (Bowers' books; "100 Greatest U.S. Modern Coins" reference)

## Why Varieties Matter

The variety market is parallel to the date/mintmark market. A common-date coin with a rare variety can be more valuable than a key-date coin without the variety. For collectors with patience and pattern-recognition discipline:

- **Variety hunting in lots** of unattributed common coins is genuine treasure-hunting
- **Pop reports for varieties** are even thinner than for date/mintmark sets
- **Trophy varieties** (1955 DDO MS-65 RD, 1969-S DDO MS-65 RD, 1937-D 3-Legged Buffalo MS-65) carry premiums that compound with date/grade premiums

Connoisseur ─── The Variety Hunter Looks Where Others Don't

The collector who specializes in die varieties operates in a niche that rewards pattern recognition over capital. A common 1955 Lincoln cent in MS-63 RD is $5; the same date with DDO is $5,000. The variety hunter spending hours examining BU rolls of common cents may find one over many years — the discovery is the asset. This is the closest thing to picking that still exists in mature numismatic categories.

Allocator ─── Variety Premiums Are Multiplicative on Date/Mint Premiums

A common date Lincoln cent in MS-65 RD: $5. A 1955 Lincoln cent in MS-65 RD: $5 (still common). A 1955 DDO in MS-65 RD: $25K+. The variety can multiply value by 5,000×. The premium is concentrated in confirmed-variety, high-grade, original-color examples. The trophy variety tier of US numismatics is small but established, with mature comp data and stable demand from variety specialists.
