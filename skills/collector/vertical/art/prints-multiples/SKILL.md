# Prints and Multiples — Edition Fractions and Technique

> **Type:** Knowledge
> **Suite:** The Collector
> **Axis:** Vertical (art)
> **Parent:** art

## The Edition Vocabulary

A limited-edition print is signed and numbered. The fraction in the lower-left or lower-right typically reads as "24/75" — meaning the 24th impression of an edition of 75.

### The Special-Fraction Designations

| Designation | Meaning | Typical Premium |
|---|---|---|
| **Regular numbered** (e.g., 24/75) | Standard edition impression | Baseline price |
| **AP** (Artist's Proof) | Reserved for the artist's use; typically 10-15% of edition size | +25-50% over numbered |
| **EA** (Épreuve d'Artiste) | French equivalent of AP | +25-50% over numbered |
| **HC** (Hors Commerce) | "Outside of commerce"; gallery / promotional samples | Variable; sometimes premium, sometimes lower |
| **PP** (Printer's Proof) | Reserved for the printer/printer's collaborators | +15-30% over numbered |
| **BAT** (Bon à Tirer) | "Good to pull"; the master proof approved by the artist as the standard for the edition; usually 1 of 1 | Trophy premium; often 2-3× numbered |
| **TP** (Trial Proof) | Proof from a developmental stage; before the final edition was decided | Variable; can be premium if a published edition followed |
| **WP** (Working Proof) | Annotated proof showing artist's revisions; usually 1 of 1 | Trophy premium |
| **PP** (Press Proof) | Sometimes "Press Proof" in other contexts | Variable |

The Bon à Tirer is the trophy — it is the literal master proof against which the printer was instructed to match the edition. Often 1 of 1 by definition.

## Edition Size Economics

The market values prints inversely to edition size, but not linearly:

- **Editions of 1-25** → trophy territory; trade at substantial premiums; often gallery-exclusive at primary release
- **Editions of 26-100** → standard limited edition; mid-market; broad collector pool
- **Editions of 101-500** → larger edition; lower per-impression price; broader market
- **Editions over 500** → mass-market; pricing low; positioning closer to poster than print
- **Open editions** → unlimited; effectively poster pricing

For example, a Picasso linocut from an edition of 50 trades fundamentally differently than a Murakami print from an edition of 300. The same artist, the same medium, but the scarcity is one to two orders of magnitude apart.

## Major Print Techniques

### Lithograph

- Drawing on stone or metal plate with greasy crayon; image is offset to paper via grease-and-water chemistry
- Allows wide range of marks; standard for fine-art prints since 19th century
- Major modern practitioners: Picasso, Miró, Calder, Lichtenstein

### Etching

- Image is drawn on a wax-coated copper plate; acid bites the exposed lines
- Allows precise linear marks; rich tonal possibilities with aquatint
- Major practitioners: Picasso (Vollard Suite, Suite 347), Hopper, Rembrandt (historical), Whistler

### Silkscreen (Screenprint)

- Image is created by forcing ink through a stenciled screen
- Strong solid colors; high impact; the canonical Pop Art technique
- Major practitioners: Warhol, Rauschenberg, Indiana

### Woodcut / Wood Engraving

- Image is cut into wood block; ink is applied to raised surface
- Bold marks; historical Japanese ukiyo-e tradition; Western expressionism
- Major practitioners: Hokusai, Hiroshige (historical); Munch, Kirchner

### Photogravure

- Photographic image transferred to a copper plate via gelatin and acid
- Subtle tonal range; canonical 19th-20th century process for photographic reproduction
- Major practitioners: Stieglitz, Steichen, Adams

### Digital / Inkjet Pigment Print

- Modern technique; high-resolution digital file to archival pigment ink
- Used by contemporary artists for limited-edition photographic prints
- Disputed status historically; now widely accepted

## Signature Conventions

### Standard Convention (Most Western Artists)

- **Edition fraction** in lower-left
- **Signature** in lower-right (sometimes accompanied by date)
- **Pencil signature** — the standard; signed after printing on the paper itself
- **Plate signature** — printed signature within the image; lower value tier than pencil-signed

### Variations

- **Inscribed** — additional text beyond the signature (dedication, location, year)
- **Embossed signature** — relief mark of the artist's stamp; common in some etching traditions
- **Estate-stamped** — signed by the artist's estate after the artist's death; lower tier than artist-signed
- **Counter-signed by the printer** — common in collaborative prints; doesn't affect value but signals provenance

## Authentication

Prints have their own forgery patterns:

- **Plate-signed reproductions** sold as pencil-signed originals — major fraud category
- **Unauthorized reprints** after the plate was sent to the artist's estate — sometimes done by family or estate without proper documentation
- **Forged signatures** added to legitimate but unsigned prints — common
- **Posthumous editions** sold as lifetime editions — for some artists (Picasso, especially), there are large posthumous editions issued by the estate; these trade at significant discounts to lifetime editions

For major artists, the **catalogue raisonné of prints** is the authentication standard. Picasso prints are catalogued in Brigitte Baer's *Picasso peintre-graveur*. Warhol prints are catalogued in *Andy Warhol Prints: A Catalogue Raisonné*.

## Condition Concerns

Prints have specific condition vulnerabilities:

- **Foxing** — small brown spots from oxidation; common on works on paper, especially pre-1950
- **Mat burn** — discoloration along the mat opening edge; from acidic matting materials
- **Fading** — light damage; permanent
- **Toning** — overall yellowing of paper from acidic environment
- **Creasing** — folds in the paper, irreversible
- **Tears** — major condition penalty even when conservatively mended
- **Tape stains** — from prior framing with non-archival tape

Conservation is possible (deacidification, bleaching, mending) but each intervention should be documented. Prints with disclosed conservation trade at a discount to pristine; prints with undisclosed conservation discovered post-purchase are a major buyer's market problem.

## Common Beginner Mistakes

1. **Confusing plate-signed with pencil-signed** — plate-signed (signature printed into the image) is fundamentally different and lower-tier from pencil-signed
2. **Buying late editions believing they are lifetime** — posthumous Picasso editions exist in significant volume; verify against the raisonné
3. **Ignoring edition size** — a 300/300 modern print is not the same scarcity as a 50/75 mid-century print
4. **Misunderstanding AP / EA designations** — these are real premiums but specific; verify the actual edition structure (some artists have 100 AP in an edition of 50, which is unusual)
5. **Buying restored prints as unrestored** — disclose disclose disclose

Connoisseur ─── The BAT Is the Print That Was the Standard

In any major print edition, one impression — the Bon à Tirer — was the artist's approved standard. Every numbered impression should match it. Holding a BAT means holding the literal reference point against which the edition was struck. It is the rarest and most documented impression by definition. For collectors who want depth in a single artist's print work, owning the BAT (when available) is the trophy tier of trophy tiers.

Allocator ─── Edition Size Drives Price Inversely; Lifetime Status Drives Premium

A Picasso lithograph from an edition of 50 trades at 5-10× a Picasso lithograph from an edition of 500, all else equal. A lifetime impression (printed and signed by the artist during their lifetime) trades at 3-5× a posthumous impression. The trophy combination: small lifetime edition, signed, in fine condition, with raisonné inclusion. The math: a 50-impression lifetime signed Picasso etching from the Vollard Suite trades $30-150K depending on subject; a 300-impression posthumous print from the same plate trades $3-15K. Same artist, same image — different asset class entirely.
