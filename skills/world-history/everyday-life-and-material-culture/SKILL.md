# Everyday Life and Material Culture — What Life Was Actually Like

Most people who ever lived never appeared in a political chronicle, a military dispatch, or an economic treatise. Their lives — what they ate, how they worked, what they believed, how they raised children, how they experienced illness and death — constitute the vast majority of human experience. Everyday life history recovers this experience using the methods pioneered by the French Annales school: long-term structural analysis, material culture, and the study of *mentalites* (collective attitudes and assumptions).

## When This Applies

- User asks "what was life like in [time period]?"
- User asks about food, clothing, housing, health, or daily routines in history
- User asks about the Annales school or Braudel
- User asks about microhistory (Carlo Ginzburg, Natalie Zemon Davis)
- User asks about how ordinary people experienced major historical events
- User wants to understand a period beyond its political narrative

## Braudel's Three Temporal Registers

Fernand Braudel's revolutionary insight: history operates at three different speeds simultaneously, and most historians only study the fastest one.

### 1. Longue Duree (Centuries to Millennia)

The slow-moving structures that shape human life without most people noticing:
- **Geography**: Mountain ranges, river systems, coastlines determine settlement, trade, and warfare patterns
- **Climate**: Agricultural calendars, disease environments, seasonal rhythms
- **Biology**: Human nutritional needs, disease susceptibility, reproductive patterns
- **Material culture**: Techniques of farming, building, cooking, healing that persist for centuries

**Example**: Mediterranean agriculture (wheat, olives, grapes) persisted from antiquity through the 20th century with remarkably little change. The fundamental rhythms of planting, harvesting, and processing structured life for 3,000 years.

### 2. Conjonctures (Decades to Centuries)

Medium-term cycles that shape generations:
- **Economic cycles**: Price revolutions, trade booms and busts, demographic swings
- **Social structures**: Class systems, guild organizations, family structures
- **Mentalites**: Collective attitudes toward death, childhood, authority, nature

**Example**: The "long 16th century" price revolution (1450-1640) doubled or tripled prices across Europe, reshaping class relations, encouraging enclosure of common lands, and contributing to social unrest.

### 3. Evenements (Days to Years)

The fast-moving surface of events — what traditional history covers:
- **Political events**: Wars, treaties, elections, coronations
- **Individual actions**: Assassinations, discoveries, decisions
- **Crises**: Natural disasters, epidemics, revolutions

**Braudel's critique**: Traditional history is obsessed with *evenements* — "the froth on the waves of history." The deep forces that shape human life operate at the *longue duree* and *conjoncture* levels.

## Microhistory

The Italian school (Carlo Ginzburg, Giovanni Levi) pioneered a different approach to everyday life: study a single individual, village, or event in extraordinary detail and use it as a window onto the larger world.

**Canonical works:**
- **Ginzburg, "The Cheese and the Worms" (1976)**: A 16th-century Italian miller named Menocchio developed his own cosmology by reading widely and thinking independently. His Inquisition trial records reveal a world of popular intellectual life invisible to conventional history.
- **Davis, "The Return of Martin Guerre" (1983)**: A 16th-century French peasant disappeared; an impostor took his place and lived as him for years. The case reveals peasant marriage, identity, community, and law.
- **Darnton, "The Great Cat Massacre" (1984)**: Parisian printing apprentices staged a mock trial and execution of cats. What seems bizarre reveals the symbolic world of 18th-century urban labor.

**The method**: Microhistory uses the exceptional case to illuminate the normal — the trial record, the Inquisition file, the court case that documents what ordinary life left unrecorded.

## Material Culture Categories

| Category | What to Examine | What It Reveals |
|---|---|---|
| **Food** | Diet, preparation, meal customs, famine frequency | Nutrition, class structure, trade networks, agricultural systems |
| **Clothing** | Fabric, style, sumptuary laws, production | Social hierarchy, trade, technology, gender norms |
| **Housing** | Structure, materials, layout, urban/rural | Family structure, class, technology, privacy norms |
| **Health** | Disease environment, medicine, life expectancy, childbirth | Knowledge systems, social investment, vulnerability |
| **Work** | Daily routine, seasonal cycles, tools, skills | Economic system, gender division of labor, technology |
| **Death** | Burial practices, mourning customs, life expectancy | Beliefs, social bonds, disease environment, inequality |
| **Leisure** | Festivals, sports, music, storytelling, religion | Values, social bonds, resistance, identity |

## The History of Experience

Recent historiography (history of the senses, history of emotions, history of the body) pushes even further into subjective experience:

- **What did the past smell like?** (Pre-modern cities were overwhelmingly odorous by modern standards; smell marked social boundaries)
- **What were people afraid of?** (Demons, plague, famine, damnation — fears varied enormously by time and place)
- **How did people experience time?** (Clock time is modern; pre-industrial time was seasonal, liturgical, task-oriented)
- **How did people experience pain?** (Pain management changed radically with anesthesia in the 1840s; before that, surgery was conscious)

## Diagnostic Framework

When analyzing everyday life in a historical period:

1. **What did people eat?** (And what does that tell us about agriculture, trade, and class?)
2. **What was the disease environment?** (Life expectancy, endemic diseases, epidemic risk)
3. **What was the work rhythm?** (Seasonal? Continuous? How gendered?)
4. **What did people believe?** (Not official doctrine — lived belief, superstition, folk practice)
5. **What Braudel register is this question operating at?** (Longue duree, conjoncture, or evenement?)
6. **Whose everyday life?** (Elite, middling, poor — and how different were they?)
