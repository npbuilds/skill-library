# Historical Thinking — The Cognitive Operating System

Historical thinking is genuinely unnatural. Sam Wineburg's research demonstrates that even intelligent adults default to reading historical sources the way they read a newspaper — absorbing claims at face value. Trained historians do something radically different: they interrogate the source *before* processing the content. This skill teaches the cognitive habits that separate historical thinking from ordinary reading.

This is not historiography (the study of how historians write). It is the prerequisite operating system that makes all other historical learning effective. Load this skill before content skills.

## When This Applies

- User asks "how should I approach" a historical question
- User reads a historical claim without questioning the source
- User treats a single source as definitive
- User imports modern assumptions into a pre-modern context
- User asks to "learn history" without a specific topic (start here)
- Any context where the user needs the *process* of historical thinking, not a specific *product*

## The Three Heuristics (Wineburg)

Wineburg identified three cognitive strategies that expert historians use automatically but novices must learn deliberately. These are not steps in a sequence — they are habits that operate simultaneously.

### 1. Sourcing

**Before reading content, ask: Who created this? When? Why? For whom?**

The sourcing heuristic is the single most powerful distinction between expert and novice historical readers. In Wineburg's studies, historians spent more time examining the source attribution of a document than reading the document itself. Students did the opposite.

**Application protocol:**
- Identify the author and their position (social, political, institutional)
- Identify the date of creation and its distance from the events described
- Identify the intended audience — who was this written for?
- Identify the purpose — to inform, persuade, justify, record, entertain?
- Only then read the content, with the above as your interpretive frame

**Diagnostic signal:** If you find yourself accepting a historical claim without knowing who made it, when, and why — the sourcing heuristic has not engaged.

### 2. Contextualization

**Place the source in its world. What was happening when this was created?**

Contextualization means reconstructing the conditions under which a source was produced. A letter written during a siege means something different from the same words written in peacetime. A political speech before an election means something different from a private diary entry.

**Application protocol:**
- What was the political situation at the time of creation?
- What were the prevailing beliefs, values, and assumptions?
- What did the author know — and what could they not yet know?
- What events had recently occurred that might shape the author's perspective?

**The anachronism trap:** The most common failure of contextualization is projecting modern values onto the past. Judging a 15th-century figure by 21st-century standards produces moral satisfaction but zero historical understanding. Contextualization does not mean moral endorsement — it means understanding *why people thought and acted as they did within their own frame of reference.*

### 3. Corroboration

**Check this source against others. Do independent sources confirm, contradict, or complicate?**

No single source is sufficient. Corroboration means triangulating: comparing what multiple sources say about the same event, noting agreements, disagreements, and silences.

**Application protocol:**
- What do other contemporary sources say about the same event?
- Where do sources agree? (Convergence suggests reliability)
- Where do they disagree? (Divergence reveals bias, perspective, or contested facts)
- What is *absent*? (Silence can be as revealing as testimony)
- Are the confirming sources truly independent, or do they share a common origin?

## The Six Historical Thinking Concepts (Seixas)

Peter Seixas's framework identifies six interconnected concepts that constitute historical thinking competency. Where Wineburg's heuristics are *reading strategies*, Seixas's concepts are *thinking dispositions*.

### 1. Historical Significance

**Not everything that happened matters equally. How do we decide what is historically significant?**

Significance is not inherent — it is assigned by historians based on criteria: How many people were affected? How deep were the changes? How long did the effects last? Does this event illuminate broader patterns? Significance can change as the present changes — events once considered marginal (women's history, environmental history) gain significance when new questions are asked.

### 2. Primary Source Evidence

**What can this source tell us? What can it not tell us?**

Sources are not windows onto the past — they are fragments that require interpretation. Every source has a perspective, a purpose, and limits. The skill is distinguishing what a source *can* tell us (the author's perspective, the conditions of creation, specific details) from what it *cannot* (what the author didn't witness, didn't know, or chose not to record).

### 3. Continuity and Change

**What stayed the same? What changed? How fast? For whom?**

History is not a sequence of events but a pattern of continuities and changes operating at different speeds. Braudel's three temporal registers are useful: the *longue durée* (geographic, climatic, structural forces changing over centuries), *conjonctures* (economic and social cycles spanning decades), and *événements* (the fast-moving surface of political events). Real historical thinking identifies which register a change operates in.

### 4. Cause and Consequence

**Why did this happen? What followed from it?**

Historical causation is multi-layered. Distinguish:
- **Immediate triggers** (the spark): assassination, financial panic, declaration
- **Underlying conditions** (the kindling): structural tensions, economic strain, social grievances
- **Deep structural forces** (the climate): demographic pressure, technological change, environmental shifts

Consequences are similarly layered: intended vs. unintended, short-term vs. long-term, visible vs. latent. The Industrial Revolution's intended consequence was increased production; its unintended consequences included urbanization, pollution, class formation, and eventually global climate change.

### 5. Historical Perspective-Taking

**How did people in the past see their own world?**

This is not "empathy" in the modern therapeutic sense. It is the disciplined reconstruction of how historical actors understood their own situation, given what they knew, believed, and valued. A medieval peasant did not experience feudalism as "oppression" in the modern sense — they experienced it within a cosmological framework where social hierarchy reflected divine order. Understanding this does not endorse it; it explains behavior that would otherwise seem inexplicable.

### 6. The Ethical Dimension

**How do we make fair judgments about people in the past?**

Historical thinking requires navigating the tension between understanding (contextualization) and judgment (ethics). We can understand *why* slaveholders defended slavery while still judging slavery as morally indefensible. The skill is holding both simultaneously — contextualizing without excusing, judging without anachronism.

## The C3 Framework (Four Dimensions)

The College, Career, and Civic Life (C3) Framework organizes historical inquiry into four dimensions that form a cycle:

1. **Developing questions and planning inquiries** — Formulating compelling and supporting questions
2. **Applying disciplinary concepts and tools** — Using the concepts above to investigate
3. **Evaluating sources and using evidence** — Source criticism and evidence-based argumentation
4. **Communicating conclusions and taking informed action** — Constructing arguments and acting on findings

The cycle is iterative: conclusions generate new questions. This framework is most useful when structuring a sustained historical inquiry rather than answering a single question.

## Integration Protocol

When loaded alongside a content skill (e.g., empires-and-states), historical-thinking provides the *process layer*:

1. Apply **sourcing** to any claim or narrative the content skill presents
2. Apply **contextualization** before analyzing causes or significance
3. Apply **corroboration** when evidence is contested
4. Identify which of Seixas's six concepts the question primarily engages
5. Structure the response to model these habits for the user
