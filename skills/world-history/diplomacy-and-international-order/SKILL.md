# Diplomacy and International Order — How States Relate

International order is not natural — it is built, maintained, and eventually collapses. Every era has its architecture: the tribute system of the ancient Near East, the Sinocentric order, the Westphalian state system, the Concert of Europe, the bipolar Cold War, and whatever is emerging now. Understanding these architectures — how they function, what holds them together, and why they fail — is essential to understanding political history at any scale larger than a single state.

## International Order Frameworks

### The Three Schools of International Relations

| School | Core Claim | Key Thinkers | Explains Well | Explains Poorly |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Realism** | States pursue power in an anarchic system; cooperation is temporary | Thucydides, Machiavelli, Morgenthau, Mearsheimer | Arms races, security dilemmas, great power competition | International institutions, norms, cooperation without coercion |
| **Liberalism** | Institutions, trade, and democracy reduce conflict | Kant, Keohane, Nye | EU integration, trade interdependence, democratic peace | Why democracies still fight, institutional failure |
| **Constructivism** | Ideas, norms, and identity shape state behavior | Wendt, Finnemore | Norm diffusion (human rights, sovereignty), identity-driven conflict | Material power dynamics, rapid change |

**The key insight**: No single school explains everything. Realism explains great power behavior under high threat. Liberalism explains cooperative regimes in low-threat environments. Constructivism explains why states care about things that have no material value (prestige, recognition, legitimacy). Use all three as complementary lenses.

## The Evolution of International Order

### Pre-Westphalian Systems

- **Ancient Near Eastern tribute**: Asymmetric relations — great kings and vassals, with diplomatic correspondence (Amarna Letters, ~1350 BCE)
- **Greek interstate system**: The first recognizable "balance of power" — poleis forming leagues, shifting alliances, hegemonic competition (Delian League vs. Peloponnesian League)
- **Sinocentric order**: The tributary system — China as the center, surrounding states acknowledging Chinese superiority in exchange for trade and non-interference. Lasted ~2,000 years.
- **Islamic international law**: Dar al-Islam and dar al-harb; rules governing warfare, treaties, and the treatment of non-Muslim communities

### The Westphalian System (1648 – present)

The Treaties of Westphalia (1648) established principles that still structure international relations:
- **Sovereignty**: States are the primary actors; no authority above them
- **Non-interference**: Internal affairs are off-limits (in theory)
- **Legal equality**: All sovereign states are formally equal (regardless of power)
- **Territorial integrity**: Borders are sacred (in principle)

**The balance of power** became the operating mechanism: no single state should dominate, and coalitions form to check aspiring hegemons (Habsburg Spain, Louis XIV's France, Napoleonic France, Wilhelmine Germany, Nazi Germany, the Soviet Union).

### Key International Orders

| Order | Period | Mechanism | Why It Ended |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Concert of Europe** | 1815 – 1854 | Great power consensus, legitimist ideology | Crimean War broke the consensus |
| **Bismarckian system** | 1871 – 1890 | Complex alliance management by one statesman | Bismarck's dismissal; alliance rigidity |
| **Versailles order** | 1919 – 1939 | Collective security via League of Nations | US non-participation, failure to enforce |
| **Bipolar Cold War** | 1947 – 1991 | Nuclear deterrence, ideological competition | Soviet collapse |
| **Unipolar moment** | 1991 – ~2008 | US hegemony, liberal institutionalism | Iraq War overreach, financial crisis, rise of China |
| **Current transition** | 2008 – present | Contested; multipolar? bipolar? fragmented? | Ongoing |

## Core Concepts

### The Security Dilemma
When one state increases its security (building arms, forming alliances), it decreases the security of others — who respond in kind. The result is an arms race neither side wanted. This mechanism explains much of the escalation before WWI.

### Hegemonic Stability
A dominant power provides public goods (open trade routes, security, monetary stability) that benefit the system. When the hegemon declines, these goods erode. Britain's navy kept sea lanes open 1815–1914; the US did the same after 1945.

### The Democratic Peace
Democracies rarely fight each other (though they fight non-democracies freely). Whether this is because of shared norms, institutional constraints, or trade interdependence is debated. The finding is robust; the mechanism is contested.

## Diagnostic Framework

When analyzing an international order or diplomatic situation:

1. **What is the distribution of power?** (Unipolar, bipolar, multipolar?)
2. **What institutions exist?** (And do they have enforcement capacity?)
3. **What norms govern behavior?** (Sovereignty, human rights, non-proliferation?)
4. **Where are the security dilemmas?** (Which states feel threatened by others' security?)
5. **Is the hegemon rising, stable, or declining?** (And does the system have a hegemon at all?)
6. **What would the realist, liberal, and constructivist each emphasize?**
