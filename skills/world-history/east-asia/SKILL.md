# East Asia — The Other Great Tradition

East Asian history provides the essential counterpoint to Western-centered narratives. For most of the last 2,000 years, China was the world's largest economy, most sophisticated bureaucracy, and most influential cultural center. Understanding East Asia is not "adding diversity" — it is correcting a fundamental distortion in how most Westerners learn history.

## When This Applies

- User asks about Chinese history (any dynasty or period)
- User asks about Japan, Korea, or the steppe empires (Mongols, Turks)
- User asks about the Sinocentric order or tributary system
- User asks about the examination system, Confucianism as governance, or Chinese invention
- User asks about East Asian modernization (Meiji, Chinese revolution, Korean development)

## Regional Distinctives

**What makes East Asian history distinctive:**

- **Bureaucratic continuity**: China maintained bureaucratic governance for 2,000+ years; the examination system (605-1905) selected officials by merit for 1,300 years — the longest-running meritocratic institution in history
- **The Sinocentric order**: Not a European-style balance of power but a hierarchical system with China at the center and surrounding states acknowledging Chinese cultural supremacy in exchange for trade and non-interference
- **Confucian civilization**: A shared cultural framework (Confucian ethics, Chinese writing system, Buddhist-Confucian synthesis) that connected China, Korea, Japan, and Vietnam despite political independence
- **The dynastic cycle**: Chinese political history follows a recognizable pattern — dynastic founding → consolidation → prosperity → decline → rebellion → new dynasty. Ibn Khaldun's asabiyyah concept maps onto this pattern
- **Early technological leadership**: Papermaking, printing, gunpowder, the compass, cast iron, the blast furnace, paper money — all Chinese inventions that reached Europe centuries later

## The Great Divergence in East Asian Context

Kenneth Pomeranz's central argument: China's Yangtze Delta was as economically advanced as Britain's midlands in 1750. The divergence came from Britain's access to coal and New World resources, not from cultural or institutional superiority. This reframes the Industrial Revolution as a *geographic accident* rather than European exceptionalism.

**Counter-arguments**: Mokyr argues Europe's competitive political landscape and culture of open inquiry mattered more than coal. Acemoglu argues China's centralized state could suppress disruptive innovation in ways Europe's fragmented states could not.

## Connected Histories

- **To Central/Inner Asia**: Steppe-sedentary dynamics (Mongols, Turks, Manchus); Silk Road
- **To Southeast Asia**: Maritime trade, Chinese diaspora, Sinicization vs. Indianization
- **To the West**: Silk Road; Jesuit mission; Opium Wars; modern great power competition
- **To the Pacific**: Japanese maritime expansion; Polynesian navigation; Pacific Rim economics

## Diagnostic Framework

When analyzing East Asian history:

1. **Which dynasty/period?** (And where in the dynastic cycle?)
2. **What is the steppe-sedentary dynamic?** (Central Asian peoples are always in the picture)
3. **How does the Sinocentric order function here?** (Tributary, hostile, or outside the system?)
4. **What is the relationship between state and society?** (Confucian bureaucracy vs. local autonomy)
5. **How does this connect to global patterns?** (Avoid treating East Asia as separate from world history)
