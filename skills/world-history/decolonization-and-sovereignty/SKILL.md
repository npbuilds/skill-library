# Decolonization and Sovereignty — The Unfinished Revolution

Between 1945 and 1975, the European colonial empires dissolved and roughly 80 new states entered the international system. This was the most rapid transformation of the global political map in history. But decolonization was not a single event — it was a process shaped by different imperial traditions, different liberation strategies, different Cold War dynamics, and different post-colonial outcomes. Understanding decolonization requires holding all of these together.

## When This Applies

- User asks about the end of specific colonial empires (British, French, Dutch, Portuguese, Belgian)
- User asks about independence movements or national liberation
- User asks about post-colonial state building, failure, or development
- User asks about neo-colonialism, dependency, or the "Global South"
- User asks about Cold War dynamics in the Third World
- User asks about the legacies of colonialism in contemporary politics

## The Colonial Systems

Not all colonialisms were the same. The administrative model shaped the decolonization process:

| Imperial Model | Characteristics | Decolonization Pattern | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **British indirect rule** | Ruled through local elites; some institutional development | Negotiated transitions; inherited institutions (sometimes functional) | India, Nigeria, Ghana, Kenya |
| **French assimilation** | Centralized; colonies treated as extensions of France | Violent rupture (Algeria, Vietnam) or negotiated transition with continued dependency (Françafrique) | Algeria, Senegal, Vietnam |
| **Belgian extraction** | Minimal education, no indigenous elite preparation | Catastrophic collapse on departure | Congo |
| **Portuguese Estado Novo** | Held longest; refused to decolonize until revolution at home | Protracted liberation wars; abrupt departure | Angola, Mozambique, Guinea-Bissau |
| **Japanese** | Short but intense; displaced European colonizers | Created power vacuums that enabled independence movements | Indonesia, Vietnam, Korea |

## Phases of Decolonization

### Phase 1 — Asian Independence (1945-1954)
India/Pakistan (1947), Indonesia (1949), Vietnam's war against France (1946-1954). The first wave was driven by mature nationalist movements that predated WWII, Japanese occupation that shattered the myth of European invincibility, and war-exhausted European powers unable to reimpose control.

### Phase 2 — African Independence (1956-1968)
Ghana (1957) opened the floodgate. By 1968, most of sub-Saharan Africa was independent. Driven by pan-Africanism (Nkrumah, Nyerere), Cold War competition for allies, and the realization by colonial powers that holding colonies was becoming costlier than releasing them.

### Phase 3 — Settler Colonies and Portuguese Empire (1960-1980)
Algeria (1962), Portuguese colonies (1974-75), Zimbabwe (1980). Where large settler populations existed, decolonization was violent and prolonged. The Portuguese colonies required a revolution in Portugal itself (the Carnation Revolution, 1974) to trigger decolonization.

### Phase 4 — Late and Unfinished (1990-present)
Namibia (1990), apartheid South Africa (1994), East Timor (2002). Some argue decolonization is ongoing — in Palestine, Western Sahara, and in the neo-colonial economic relationships that persist.

## The Post-Colonial State Problem

Most post-colonial states inherited borders drawn by European diplomats with no regard for ethnic, linguistic, or ecological boundaries. The result:

- **Artificial states**: Borders that lump hostile groups together and split cohesive ones apart (Nigeria, Iraq, many African states)
- **Weak institutions**: Colonial institutions were designed for extraction, not governance; many new states had no functioning bureaucracy, judiciary, or tax system
- **Elite capture**: Colonial-educated elites often replicated colonial power structures with themselves on top
- **Cold War distortion**: Superpower patronage sustained authoritarian regimes and militarized local conflicts

**The debate**: Are post-colonial state failures caused primarily by colonial legacies (Mamdani, Rodney) or by post-independence governance choices (Bates, Collier)? The answer is usually "both, in proportions that vary by case."

## Neo-Colonialism and Dependency

Kwame Nkrumah coined "neo-colonialism" (1965) to describe how newly independent states remained economically dependent on former colonial powers. The dependency school (Frank, Wallerstein, Amin) argued that the global economy structurally disadvantages the periphery:

- **Terms of trade**: Raw material exporters perpetually lose relative to manufactured goods importers
- **Debt dependency**: Loans with conditions that reproduce colonial-era economic structures (structural adjustment programs)
- **Françafrique**: France maintained military bases, monetary control (CFA franc), and political influence across francophone Africa
- **Resource curse**: Natural resource wealth attracts extraction without development (Dutch disease, conflict minerals)

**Counter-argument**: The Asian Tigers (South Korea, Taiwan, Singapore, Hong Kong) and later China achieved rapid development despite starting from colonial or semi-colonial positions. Development is possible — but the structural obstacles are real.

## Self-Determination vs. State Viability

The central tension of decolonization: the right of peoples to determine their own political status (self-determination) vs. the practical requirements for a functioning state (viability). Key questions:

- **Who is "a people"?** (Ethnic group? Linguistic community? All inhabitants of a colonial border?)
- **At what scale?** (If Nigeria can secede from Britain, can Biafra secede from Nigeria?)
- **Uti possidetis juris**: The legal principle that colonial borders become international borders — adopted to prevent endless fragmentation but freezing often-arbitrary boundaries

## Diagnostic Framework

When analyzing a decolonization case:

1. **What was the colonial model?** (Indirect/direct, settler/non-settler, extraction/development)
2. **How mature was the nationalist movement?** (Elite-only or mass-based? Ideological coherence?)
3. **What was the Cold War context?** (Superpower patron? Proxy conflict?)
4. **How did the transition occur?** (Negotiated, violent, or catastrophic?)
5. **What institutions were inherited?** (Functioning bureaucracy? Judiciary? Tax base?)
6. **What post-colonial legacies persist?** (Borders, economic structures, cultural divisions?)
