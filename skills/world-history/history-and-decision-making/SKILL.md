# History and Decision-Making — Thinking in Time

History is not just an academic discipline — it is a tool that policymakers, strategists, and investors use (and misuse) every day. The Munich analogy drove Cold War interventionism. The Vietnam analogy constrained post-Cold War interventionism. The 1929 analogy shaped the response to the 2008 financial crisis. Understanding how to use history well — and recognizing when it is being used badly — is one of the most practical applications of historical knowledge.

This skill draws on `historical-pattern-recognition` for known patterns and bridges to investing/archon for market applications.

## When This Applies

- User asks "what can history teach us about [current situation]?"

## Neustadt and May's Framework (Thinking in Time, 1986)

The most practical method for applying history to decisions:

### The Three-Step Method

**Step 1 — What is Known, Unclear, and Presumed?**
Separate what you actually know from what you assume. Most decision-making failures come from treating presumptions as facts.

**Step 2 — What are the Likenesses and Differences?**
When you invoke a historical analogy, explicitly list:
- What is genuinely similar between then and now
- What is genuinely different
- Which of the differences might matter more than the similarities

**Step 3 — What is the History of the Issue?**
Don't just grab an analogy — trace the actual history of the specific issue. How did this situation develop? What were the key turning points? What options were foreclosed and why?

### The "Goldberg Rule"
Neustadt and May's most powerful insight: when someone says "this is just like [historical event]," ask "**what makes this different from [that event]?**" The differences are almost always more important than the similarities, and the person invoking the analogy has almost always not considered them.

## Common Decision-Making Traps

| Trap | Mechanism | Historical Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Anchoring on one analogy** | A single historical case dominates thinking, crowding out alternatives | Munich (1938) dominated Cold War thinking; every adversary became Hitler |
| **Fighting the last war** | Preparing for the previous conflict, not the current one | French Maginot Line (prepared for WWI trench warfare; Germany went around it) |
| **Availability bias** | Recent or vivid historical events disproportionately influence decisions | Vietnam syndrome constrained US intervention for a generation |
| **Generational forgetting** | Lessons learned by one generation are forgotten by the next | Financial crisis lessons (1929, 2008) fade as those who experienced them retire |
| **Motivated reasoning** | Selecting analogies that support a preferred policy rather than illuminate the situation | Iraq War architects selected analogies (Germany/Japan post-WWII) that supported invasion |

## Scenario Planning with Historical Precedents

Scenario planning (developed at RAND, popularized by Shell) uses historical analysis to construct plausible futures:

### The Method

1. **Identify the key uncertainties**: What factors could go either way?
2. **Research historical precedents** for each uncertainty: When has this kind of thing happened before?
3. **Construct 3-4 scenarios**: Combine different resolutions of the uncertainties into coherent narratives
4. **Stress-test each scenario**: What early indicators would tell you which scenario is unfolding?
5. **Develop robust strategies**: Actions that work across multiple scenarios, not just the most likely one

### Historical Scenarios That Illuminate

- **Power transition**: Is the US-China dynamic more like Britain-Germany (1890-1914, ended in war) or Britain-US (1890-1945, peaceful transition)? What determines which path?
- **Financial crisis**: Is current debt more like 1929 (deflationary spiral), 2008 (contained by intervention), or 1970s (inflationary resolution)?
- **Technological disruption**: Is AI more like the printing press (centuries to full effect), electricity (decades), or nuclear weapons (immediate strategic impact)?

## Intelligence Analysis Methods

Intelligence analysts have developed some of the most rigorous methods for using historical reasoning under uncertainty:

- **Analysis of Competing Hypotheses (ACH)**: List all plausible hypotheses; evaluate each piece of evidence against all hypotheses simultaneously; reject hypotheses inconsistent with the evidence. Prevents anchoring on a single explanation.
- **Structured Analogy**: Systematically compare the current case to multiple historical cases; weight each analogy by structural similarity; synthesize. More rigorous than ad hoc analogy selection.
- **Red Team**: Assign a team to argue the opposing case using historical evidence. Prevents groupthink and forces consideration of alternatives.

## Diagnostic Framework

When applying history to a decision:

1. **What analogy is being invoked?** (Name it explicitly)
2. **What are the differences?** (The Goldberg Rule — always ask this)
3. **What other analogies might apply?** (Never rely on just one)
4. **What is the actual history of this specific issue?** (Trace it, don't just grab a parallel)
5. **Who benefits from this analogy?** (Analogies are not neutral — they frame action)
6. **What would Neustadt and May say?** (Known / Unclear / Presumed)
