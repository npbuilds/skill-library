# Common Historical Fallacies

A spotter's guide to the most frequent reasoning errors in historical thinking. Each entry: definition, spotter heuristic, worked example, fix.

These fallacies appear in user questions, in popular history, in journalism, and (less often, but still) in scholarship. The Scriptorium's job is to name them when they appear — gently in a beginner question, more sharply when the user is ready for it.

---

## 1. Presentism

**Definition:** Judging past actors by present-day moral, conceptual, or evidentiary standards.

**Spotter heuristic:** "Why didn't they just…?" or "How could they not see…?"

**Worked example:**
> Q: "Why did the framers of the U.S. Constitution allow slavery? Didn't they know it was wrong?"
> The framers knew slavery was contested; abolitionist arguments existed in 1787. But the question's framing imports a 21st-century moral consensus that did not yet exist as consensus. Many framers held views — including about race, citizenship, and economy — that emerged from their context. *Diagnosis matters more than verdict:* explain what they thought and why, then let the user pass moral judgment with full information.

**Fix:** Restore the contemporary horizon of moral and conceptual possibility. Then — *and only then* — make the present-day moral evaluation explicit and separate.

---

## 2. Teleology

**Definition:** Treating historical outcomes as the *goal* the past was working toward, rather than as one outcome among possibilities.

**Spotter heuristic:** "The path to X" — implying X was the destination.

**Worked example:**
> "The path to the United States as a global superpower" frames everything from Jamestown forward as preparation. But superpower status is a 1945+ outcome that 17th-century English colonists could not have aimed at. The framing imports the conclusion into the cause.

**Fix:** Reframe in terms of *contingencies*. "How did the United States become a superpower?" — answerable. "The path to superpower status" — teleological.

---

## 3. Whig History

**Definition:** A specific form of teleology that reads the past as inevitable progress toward present liberal-democratic / constitutional / scientific institutions.

**Spotter heuristic:** Heroes (Magna Carta, Glorious Revolution, U.S. Founding) connected by an arc of "expanding freedom"; villains (absolutism, superstition) treated as obstacles defeated.

**Worked example:**
> Macaulay's 1848 *History of England* — the foundational Whig history — narrates English history as the progressive triumph of constitutional liberty. The framing erases reverses, alternative possibilities, and the costs of the institutions it celebrates.

**Fix:** Restore the contingencies and the alternatives that did not win. Liberal democracy is one outcome, not the destination. Cf. Herbert Butterfield, *The Whig Interpretation of History* (1931).

---

## 4. Anachronism

**Definition:** Importing concepts, terms, or categories from one era into another where they don't fit.

**Spotter heuristic:** Modern political/economic vocabulary applied to pre-modern contexts.

**Worked examples:**
| Anachronism | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| "Roman emperor" for Republican Rome | Imposes the post-Augustan Principate concept |
| "Feudalism" applied to Tokugawa Japan | The term has specific European institutional content |
| "Capitalism" applied to 14th-century Italian merchants | Mercantile finance ≠ industrial capitalism |
| "Nation-state" applied to pre-1789 polities | The nation-state form is post-Revolutionary |
| "Race" as a biological category in classical antiquity | Ancient categorizations were largely cultural/religious; biological race is early-modern |
| "Religion" as a separable sphere in pre-modern societies | The category emerges with European modernity |

**Fix:** Use period-appropriate vocabulary. Where modern vocabulary is unavoidable for the user's understanding, flag it ("we use 'capitalism' here loosely; for technical specificity see…").

## 5. Monocausality

**Definition:** Attributing a complex outcome to a single cause.

**Spotter heuristic:** "The real reason X happened was…" (singular).

**Worked example:**
> "The fall of Rome was caused by Christianity." (Gibbon's overstated reading.) Or: "World War I was caused by the assassination of Franz Ferdinand." Or: "The Great Depression was caused by the 1929 stock crash."
> All three identify *triggers* and treat them as *causes*. The full causal account requires layered analysis: immediate (assassination, crash, conversion) + underlying (alliance system, monetary contraction, late-Roman political and fiscal stress) + structural (declining hegemony, gold standard, slave-mode economic exhaustion).

**Fix:** Apply wan-shi-tong's cause-layering protocol: immediate / underlying / structural. Surface at least 2 layers, ideally 3.

---

## 6. Retrospective Determinism (Hindsight Bias)

**Definition:** Treating outcomes that occurred as if they were inevitable from their starting conditions.

**Spotter heuristic:** "Of course X happened — given Y, what else could have happened?"

**Worked example:**
> "The Soviet Union was bound to collapse." In 1985, this was not obvious — most Sovietologists did not predict it. The framing erases the contingency of perestroika, Gorbachev's specific decisions, the 1991 coup attempt, and the alternative paths that almost prevailed.

**Fix:** Restore the counterfactual. What were the live alternatives at each decision point? What would have made a different outcome more likely?

---

## 7. Cherry-Picking

**Definition:** Selecting evidence that confirms a thesis while ignoring evidence that complicates or contradicts it.

**Spotter heuristic:** Single-source claims, claims that "every historian agrees" without naming any historians, claims that ignore the standard counter-cases.

**Worked example:**
> "Empires always collapse from within, not external pressure." Cherry-picks Rome, Ottomans, Soviets while ignoring Aztec, Inca (external), Persia (external + internal), and the survival of empires through external shock (Roman survival of multiple invasions before the final one).

**Fix:** Name the counter-cases. If the thesis is worth defending, it must accommodate or explain them.

---

## 8. Survivorship Bias in Sources

**Definition:** Drawing conclusions from the sources that survived without accounting for what didn't.

**Spotter heuristic:** Claims about a period or population based only on what reached us.

**Worked example:**
> "Medieval European literature was overwhelmingly religious." True of *what survived* — and what survived is overwhelmingly what monasteries copied, which was overwhelmingly religious. The vernacular oral and lay literary traditions are systematically underrepresented because they weren't preserved by the institutions that did the preserving.

**Fix:** Always ask "what would have been preserved here, and what wouldn't?" before generalizing from extant sources.

---

## 9. False Consensus / "Most Historians Believe"

**Definition:** Claiming consensus without naming the historians, the dissenters, or the boundary of agreement.

**Spotter heuristic:** "Most historians believe X" with no citations.

**Worked example:**
> "Most historians believe the Industrial Revolution improved workers' lives." Optimist economic historians (Lindert, Williamson) argue this; the pessimist tradition (Thompson, Hobsbawm) does not. Calling either side "the consensus" is a move in an active debate.

**Fix:** Name the position, name the contender, name the stakes. (The wan-shi-tong orchestrator carries a historiographical-stance reference doc devoted to this problem.)

## 10. The Genetic Fallacy in Historical Sources

**Definition:** Dismissing or accepting a source based purely on who produced it, rather than evaluating its content and corroboration.

**Spotter heuristic:** "The source was biased, so it can't be trusted" or "The source was a participant, so it must be trusted."

**Worked examples:**
> Tacitus is a Roman senator with strong political agendas. Dismissing him as "biased" loses critical evidence. *But* taking him as transparent loses the agenda.
> Conversely: Bartolomé de las Casas is a participant-observer of Spanish atrocities — but his polemical purpose shaped numerical claims (the "Black Legend" debate).

**Fix:** Bias is not a binary. Identify the bias, ask what it predicts about the source's distortions, then read accordingly. Corroborate with independent sources where possible.

## 11. Single-Cause Geographic Determinism

**Definition:** Reducing complex civilizational outcomes to geography or environment alone.

**Spotter heuristic:** "X happened because of [latitude / climate / coastline / soil]" without engagement with intervening institutions, ideas, or contingencies.

**Worked example:**
> Jared Diamond's *Guns, Germs, and Steel* offers powerful long-run geographic explanation but is widely critiqued for monocausality — minimizing institutional, cultural, and contingent factors. Acemoglu and Robinson's *Why Nations Fail* is the most prominent counter, emphasizing institutions.

**Fix:** Geography sets *conditions*; institutions, cultures, and decisions act *within* those conditions. Both layers matter.

---

## 12. The "Great Man" Fallacy

**Definition:** Attributing historical outcomes overwhelmingly to individual leaders' decisions and personalities.

**Spotter heuristic:** Counterfactuals that turn entirely on a single person ("if Napoleon had…").

**Worked example:**
> "If Hitler had not existed, World War II would not have happened." This both overstates individual agency and understates the structural conditions (Versailles, German economic crisis, French/British appeasement dynamics, Soviet/Nazi calculation) that made *some* European war highly likely.

**Fix:** Counterfactuals about individuals should specify what structural conditions would also need to be different. Most historical change is structure × agency, not agency alone.

---

## 13. Reverse Anachronism / "Noble Savage"

**Definition:** Romanticizing past or non-Western societies as innocent, harmonious, or pure in contrast to a corrupted present or West.

**Spotter heuristic:** "Pre-Columbian / pre-Christian / pre-modern peoples lived in harmony with nature / each other / etc."

**Worked example:**
> Pre-Columbian Americas are sometimes idealized as ecologically pristine. The reality includes substantial human modification of landscapes (*terra preta* soils, fire-managed grasslands, intensive agriculture), warfare, and human sacrifice in some societies.

**Fix:** Treat past and non-Western societies with the same analytical seriousness as present and Western ones — capable of complexity, conflict, and moral compromise.

---

## 14. False Equivalence

**Definition:** Treating positions as equally supported when they are not.

**Spotter heuristic:** "Both sides have a point" applied to genuinely asymmetric evidentiary situations.

**Worked example:**
> "Some historians say the Holocaust killed 6 million Jews, others dispute this." False equivalence — the dispute is between mainstream history (overwhelmingly evidenced) and Holocaust denial (politically motivated, evidentially bankrupt). Naming both sides as historiographical positions misrepresents the field.

**Fix:** Distinguish *historiographical disagreement among scholars* from *contestation by motivated outsiders*. The Scriptorium teaches the former; it names and dismisses the latter.

---

## 15. The Fallacy of the Continuous Tradition

**Definition:** Treating present institutions, identities, or practices as having unbroken continuity with ancient origins.

**Spotter heuristic:** "X has been Y for thousands of years." (Almost always false in any strong sense.)

**Worked examples:**
> "The Olympic Games have been held since ancient Greece." The modern Olympics (1896) are an invented tradition with limited continuity to ancient Olympia.
> "Britain has been a constitutional monarchy since Magna Carta." Magna Carta did not establish constitutional monarchy; the modern form is largely 17th–19th century.
> "The Catholic Church has continuous teaching since Peter." Doctrine has changed substantially over time (cf. usury, religious freedom, slavery).

**Fix:** Cf. Hobsbawm and Ranger, *The Invention of Tradition*. Most "ancient" continuities are 19th-century constructions.

## How to Use This Doc

When a fallacy appears in a user question, the Scriptorium's options:

1. **Answer the question, name the fallacy briefly.** Best for casual depth and beginners.
2. **Reframe the question to avoid the fallacy, then answer the reframe.** Best for Learning depth.
3. **Answer + name the fallacy + show how the reframing changes the answer.** Best for Advanced depth and for users who explicitly want methodological coaching.

Never weaponize fallacy-naming against a user. The point is to teach historical thinking, not to score points.
