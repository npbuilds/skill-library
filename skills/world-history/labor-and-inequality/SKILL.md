# Labor and Inequality — Who Works, Who Benefits

Every society must answer two questions: How is work organized? How are its rewards distributed? The answers have varied enormously — from slavery to serfdom to wage labor to the gig economy — but certain patterns recur. Inequality tends to rise in stable, growing economies and fall only through catastrophe (war, plague, revolution) or deliberate political intervention. This is Walter Scheidel's "Great Leveler" thesis, and understanding it is essential to understanding why the history of work is also the history of political conflict.

## When This Applies

- User asks about slavery, serfdom, or labor systems in any historical period
- User asks about inequality — why it exists, how it changes, whether it is inevitable
- User asks about the labor movement, unions, strikes, or workers' rights
- User asks about Piketty, Scheidel, or the economics of distribution
- User asks about the welfare state, social insurance, or redistribution

## Labor Systems Across History

| System | Principle | Period | Key Feature | Ended Because |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Slavery** | Forced labor as property | Ancient – 19th c. | Slaveholder owns the person and their output entirely | Moral movements + economic inefficiency at scale |
| **Serfdom** | Tied to the land | Medieval – 19th c. | Serf cannot leave; owes labor/produce to lord; has some customary rights | State centralization, market penetration, revolution |
| **Guild labor** | Regulated craft production | Medieval – 18th c. | Master-journeyman-apprentice hierarchy; quality control; restricted entry | Industrial competition destroyed the model |
| **Wage labor** | Labor sold by the hour | 18th c. – present | Worker is "free" to sell labor but owns no means of production | (Ongoing; gig economy is latest mutation) |
| **Plantation** | Coerced cash-crop production | 16th – 19th c. | Slavery or debt peonage for export agriculture | Abolition, independence movements |
| **State labor** | Forced/directed by the state | Various | Corvée (ancient), convict labor, Soviet Gulag, Maoist campaigns | State collapse or reform |

**Key insight**: "Free labor" is a relatively recent and geographically limited phenomenon. For most of history, most people worked under some form of coercion. The transition to wage labor was itself contested — early factory workers saw wages as a form of servitude.

## The Long Arc of Inequality

### Piketty's Framework (Capital in the Twenty-First Century, 2014)

**Core thesis**: When the rate of return on capital (r) exceeds the rate of economic growth (g), wealth concentrates. Since r > g is the historical norm (except during the anomalous 1914-1980 period), inequality is the default condition of capitalism.

**The U-curve**: Income inequality in Europe and the US was extreme before 1914, compressed by the world wars and progressive taxation (the "Great Compression," 1914-1980), and has been rising again since 1980 (the "Great Divergence").

**Critics**: Acemoglu and Robinson argue institutions matter more than r vs. g. Milanovic points to globalization effects. Mankiw argues returns to capital reflect productivity, not rent-seeking.

### Scheidel's Great Leveler (2017)

**Core thesis**: Throughout history, significant reductions in inequality have come only from catastrophic events — the "Four Horsemen":

1. **Mass-mobilization warfare**: WWI and WWII destroyed capital, killed elites, empowered labor, and created political conditions for redistribution
2. **Transformative revolution**: The Russian, Chinese, and Cuban revolutions radically redistributed wealth (at enormous human cost)
3. **State collapse**: When states fail, elite wealth evaporates along with the institutions that protected it
4. **Pandemic**: The Black Death (1347-1353) killed 30-60% of Europe's population, creating labor scarcity that doubled wages and ended serfdom in Western Europe

**The disturbing implication**: Peaceful, incremental redistribution has historically been modest and temporary. The major compressions have required destruction on a massive scale. Whether modern democracies can break this pattern is an open question.

## The Labor Movement

The organized response to industrial capitalism:

### The Arc

| Phase | Period | Characteristics | Key Events |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Early resistance** | 1790s-1840s | Machine-breaking, food riots, mutual aid | Luddites (1811-16), Peterloo (1819) |
| **Organization** | 1840s-1880s | Trade unions, cooperative movements, political parties | Chartism, First International (1864), Paris Commune (1871) |
| **Mass unionism** | 1880s-1930s | Industrial unions, strikes, legal recognition | IWW, CIO, British General Strike (1926) |
| **Social compact** | 1945-1975 | Union-management bargaining, welfare state, rising wages | Post-war consensus, Great Compression |
| **Decline** | 1980-present | Deunionization, globalization, gig economy, precarity | Reagan/Thatcher, NAFTA, rise of service economy |

### Why Unions Declined

- **Deindustrialization**: Manufacturing moved to low-wage countries; service jobs harder to organize
- **Political assault**: Reagan firing air traffic controllers (1981), Thatcher defeating miners (1984-85) — demonstrated state willingness to break union power
- **Legal changes**: Right-to-work laws, restrictions on collective action, employer anti-union strategies
- **Globalization**: Capital mobility gave employers the credible threat of relocation
- **Cultural shift**: Individualism displaced collective identity; "meritocracy" framed inequality as deserved

## The Welfare State

The 20th century's answer to the labor question: can you have capitalism without immiseration?

| Model | Examples | Principle | Coverage |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Social democratic** | Sweden, Denmark, Norway | Universal benefits, high taxes, strong unions | Comprehensive cradle-to-grave |
| **Conservative/corporatist** | Germany, France | Insurance-based, tied to employment | Strong for workers, weaker for outsiders |
| **Liberal** | US, UK (post-Thatcher) | Means-tested, residual, market-oriented | Safety net, not universal provision |

**Esping-Andersen's "Three Worlds of Welfare Capitalism" (1990)** remains the foundational typology. The welfare state's future is contested: aging populations, migration, automation, and fiscal pressures all challenge the post-war model.

## Diagnostic Framework

When analyzing labor systems or inequality:

1. **What is the labor system?** (Coerced, semi-free, wage, precarious?)
2. **Who controls the means of production?** (And what is the balance of power between capital and labor?)
3. **What is the inequality trend?** (Rising, stable, declining — and what is driving it?)
4. **What institutions exist to redistribute?** (Unions, welfare state, progressive taxation — and are they strengthening or weakening?)
5. **What are the political consequences?** (Does inequality produce instability, populism, revolution — or acquiescence?)
6. **What would Piketty, Scheidel, and the labor historians each emphasize?**
