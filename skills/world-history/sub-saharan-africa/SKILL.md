# Sub-Saharan Africa — The Deepest Roots

Africa is where humanity began, and African history is the longest continuous history on Earth. Yet in Western education, Africa typically appears only at the moment of European contact — as if the continent had no history before Europeans arrived to "discover" it. This skill corrects that distortion by centering African agency, African state-building, African trade networks, and African intellectual traditions, while also honestly examining the catastrophic impacts of the slave trade and colonialism.

## When This Applies

- User asks about pre-colonial African kingdoms and empires
- User asks about the Atlantic slave trade and its consequences
- User asks about European colonialism in Africa
- User asks about post-colonial African states
- User asks about African connections to the wider world (trade, migration, ideas)

## Regional Timeline

| Period | Key Developments | Connections |
|---|---|---|
| **Deep history** (300,000+ YA) | Human origins; migration out of Africa; earliest tools and art | Africa is where all human history begins |
| **Neolithic/Iron Age** (3000 BCE-500 CE) | Bantu expansion; ironworking; agricultural intensification | Bantu migration spread languages, crops, and technology across the continent |
| **Classical kingdoms** (500-1500) | Ghana, Mali, Songhai (West Africa); Great Zimbabwe; Axum/Ethiopia; Swahili city-states | Trans-Saharan gold trade; Indian Ocean commerce; Islamic connections |
| **Slave trade era** (1500-1850) | Atlantic slave trade; internal transformations; Oyo, Dahomey, Asante | ~12.5 million enslaved people transported; demographic catastrophe |
| **Colonial period** (1884-1960s) | Berlin Conference partition; colonial extraction; resistance movements | Arbitrary borders; extractive institutions; racial ideologies |
| **Independence** (1960s-present) | National liberation; Cold War proxy conflicts; structural adjustment; contemporary growth | State-building with colonial institutional inheritance |

## Correcting the Narrative

### What Western Education Usually Gets Wrong

- **"Africa has no history"**: Hegel's claim. Africa has the longest history of any continent and produced complex states, trade networks, and intellectual traditions for millennia before European contact.
- **"Africa was isolated"**: Africa was connected to the wider world through trans-Saharan, Indian Ocean, and Nile Valley networks for thousands of years.
- **"Colonialism brought development"**: Walter Rodney's "How Europe Underdeveloped Africa" (1972) argues colonialism *extracted* wealth and *destroyed* indigenous institutions. The debate continues (Acemoglu vs. Austin), but the idea that colonialism was a civilizing mission is historically indefensible.
- **"Post-colonial failure is Africa's fault"**: Post-colonial states inherited borders, institutions, and economic structures designed for extraction, not governance. The debate about whether colonial legacies or post-independence choices matter more is real and ongoing.

### What Africa's History Reveals

- **State formation without writing**: Great Zimbabwe, the Luba and Lunda empires, and many West African states operated sophisticated governance systems without writing — demonstrating that literacy is not a prerequisite for political complexity
- **Trade network sophistication**: The Swahili coast was integrated into Indian Ocean trade for over 1,000 years; West African gold financed Mediterranean economies
- **Intellectual traditions**: Timbuktu's university (Sankore) housed hundreds of thousands of manuscripts; Ethiopian Christianity preserved ancient texts; oral traditions encoded deep historical knowledge

## Connected Histories

- **To the Mediterranean/Near East**: Trans-Saharan trade (gold, salt, enslaved people); Islam's spread; Egyptian/Ethiopian connections
- **To the Indian Ocean**: Swahili coast cities (Kilwa, Mombasa, Zanzibar); trade with India, Arabia, China; Bantu migration patterns
- **To the Americas**: The Atlantic slave trade's demographic, economic, and cultural consequences on both sides of the ocean
- **To Europe**: Colonial relationships; independence movements; post-colonial migration; contemporary economic ties

## Diagnostic Framework

When analyzing African history:

1. **What is the African agency?** (Avoid treating Africans as passive objects of external forces)
2. **What trade connections exist?** (Trans-Saharan, Indian Ocean, Atlantic — Africa was never isolated)
3. **What is the state formation pattern?** (Not all societies had states; many had sophisticated non-state governance)
4. **What is the colonial legacy?** (Borders, institutions, economic structures, psychological impact)
5. **What periodization is appropriate?** (African chronology does not map onto European periodization)
