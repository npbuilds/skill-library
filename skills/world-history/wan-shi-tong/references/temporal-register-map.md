# Temporal Register Map

World history operates across **six temporal registers**, and each register changes which wings dominate, which sources are available, and which interpretive cautions apply. This doc maps register → dominant wings → source availability → interpretive cautions, so wan-shi-tong can calibrate the analysis depth and warn the user about register-specific pitfalls.

## The Six Registers

| Register | Span | Defining feature |
|---|---|---|
| Deep | 300,000 BCE – 10,000 BCE | Pre-agricultural humanity; archaeology + paleogenetics primary |
| Ancient | 10,000 BCE – 500 CE | Neolithic → Bronze → Iron; first written records |
| Post-classical | 500 – 1500 CE | Spread of universal religions, agrarian empires (avoid "medieval" — Eurocentric) |
| Early Modern | 1500 – 1800 | Columbian Exchange, gunpowder empires, mercantile capitalism |
| Modern | 1800 – 1945 | Industrialization, nationalism, world wars |
| Contemporary | 1945 – present | Cold War, decolonization, digital age |

## Per-Register Briefings

### ● Deep History (300,000 BCE – 10,000 BCE)

**Dominant wing:** world-systems (specifically `deep-history`).
**Why:** Without writing, traditional political/intellectual analysis collapses. The frame becomes evolutionary, ecological, archaeological.

**Available sources:** Lithic tools, fossil remains, ancient DNA, cave art, isotope analysis, climate proxies.

**Interpretive cautions:**
- Inferences are heavily model-dependent (e.g., "cognitive revolution" debates).
- Beware Eurocentric narratives that treat Sapiens diffusion as a single story.
- Names and identities project poorly — discuss populations and ecologies, not heroes.

**Cross-domain bridge:** worldbuilding (deep-time scaffolds), data-science (ancient DNA, climate proxies).

### ● Ancient (10,000 BCE – 500 CE)

**Dominant wings:** political-history, cultural-history, world-systems, regional-atlas.
**Why:** First states, first cities, first universal religions, first systematic comparative civilization frame (Akkad, Egypt, Indus, Shang, Mesoamerican).

**Available sources:** Inscriptions, papyri, coins, monumental architecture, increasingly literary texts.

**Interpretive cautions:**
- Sources are elite-biased (literacy is rare). Cultural-history must work hard to recover ordinary life.
- Periodization across regions does not align — "classical" maps badly onto China, Mesoamerica, sub-Saharan Africa.
- Avoid conflating "ancient" with "primitive" — Han China and the Roman Empire are technologically and administratively complex.

**Cross-domain bridge:** philosophy-orchestrator (Axial Age thought), worldbuilding.

### ● Post-Classical (500 – 1500 CE)

**Dominant wings:** political-history, economic-history, cultural-history, intellectual-history, regional-atlas.
**Why:** Universal religions consolidate; agrarian empires (Tang, Abbasid, Byzantine, Carolingian); long-distance trade integrates Eurasia.

**Available sources:** Chronicles, religious texts, legal codes, increasingly extensive archaeological record, growing volume of administrative documents.

**Interpretive cautions:**
- The term "medieval" is Eurocentric (implies a "middle" between classical and modern). Use "post-classical" or region-specific periodization.
- Avoid "Dark Ages" framing — it ignores Islamic Golden Age, Tang/Song flourishing, Mesoamerican peaks.
- The Mongol Empire is the great connector — most cross-regional analysis must go through it for the 13th–14th centuries.

**Cross-domain bridge:** intellectual-history → philosophy-orchestrator (Islamic philosophy, Scholastic synthesis).

### ● Early Modern (1500 – 1800)

**Dominant wings:** ALL active. This is the register where every wing contributes substantially.
**Why:** Columbian Exchange creates the first truly global system; gunpowder empires (Ottoman, Safavid, Mughal, Ming/Qing); Scientific Revolution; mercantile capitalism; transatlantic slavery; first global pandemics.

**Available sources:** Vast and increasingly varied — state archives, merchant records, missionary accounts, indigenous sources (where they survive), early printed materials.

**Interpretive cautions:**
- The Great Divergence debate sits here. Don't assume European inevitability — Pomeranz, Allen, Frank all argue contingency.
- Indigenous sources are systematically suppressed in colonial archives. Use historiography to flag the silences.
- Slavery is not a side topic — it is structural to the entire economic register.

**Cross-domain bridges:** investing/archon (early capitalism), worldbuilding (the most "world-buildable" register).

### ● Modern (1800 – 1945)

**Dominant wings:** ALL active, with historiography newly dominant (the discipline of history professionalizes).
**Why:** Industrialization, nationalism, imperialism, world wars, mass politics, mass media, mass death.

**Available sources:** Overwhelming. Statistics, photography, audio, film, mass-produced print, vast state archives, oral history begins.

**Interpretive cautions:**
- Source overload creates selection bias toward "what was preserved" — usually elite, Western, male.
- This is the register where applied-history becomes most dangerous: 20th-century analogies (Munich, Vietnam, Weimar) are powerful and frequently misapplied.
- Beware "Whig history" — the temptation to read 1800–1945 as the inevitable triumph of liberal democracy is strong and wrong.

**Cross-domain bridges:** investing/archon (modern financial system origins), data-science (first systematic statistics).

### ● Contemporary (1945 – present)

**Dominant wings:** All active, with applied-history especially central (these are *living* analogies).
**Why:** Cold War, decolonization, civil rights, environmental crisis, digital revolution.

**Available sources:** Digitally overwhelming. Government documents (with declassification lags), oral histories of living participants, real-time media.

**Interpretive cautions:**
- Recent events resist historicization — too close, too contested, too politically charged.
- Avoid the "End of History" trap (Fukuyama 1989) — the present is not the resolution.
- Narratives of the recent past are often shaped by present political alignments more than by sources.
- Memory studies become central: how do societies *remember* the contemporary past, and how does memory diverge from history?

**Cross-domain bridges:** investing/archon (every market regime), philosophy-orchestrator (recent political thought), neocortex (frontier-scanner for the current decade).

## Cross-Temporal Reasoning

### When a question is trans-temporal

Many of the best history questions span registers ("How have empires fallen?", "How has work changed?"). For these:

1. Pick a primary wing (the thematic axis is constant across registers).
2. Sample 2–3 cases from different registers to surface the pattern.
3. Use historiography to name what is *invariant* (e.g., Turchin's structural-demographic theory) vs. what is *register-specific*.
4. End with a Learn block that generalizes the pattern, with caveats about register limits.

### Anachronism flags

The Chronological Lens Protocol step 4 mandates flagging anachronism. The most common register-import errors:

| Register | Common anachronism | Why it fails |
|---|---|---|
| Ancient | "Roman emperor" used for pre-Augustan figures | Imposes a Principate concept on Republican Rome |
| Post-classical | "feudalism" applied to Japan, China, Islamic world | The term has specific European institutional content |
| Early Modern | "capitalism" applied uncritically | Mercantile ≠ industrial capitalism (Wallerstein's distinction) |
| Modern | "nation-state" applied to pre-1789 polities | The nation-state form is post-Revolutionary |
| Contemporary | "Cold War logic" applied to post-2008 multipolarity | The bipolar structural condition is gone |

When an anachronism is detected, name it and offer the register-appropriate alternative term.
