# Revolutions and Regime Change — When Political Orders Break

Revolutions are not riots that succeeded. They are structural ruptures in the political order, driven by the convergence of fiscal crisis, elite fracture, and popular mobilization. Understanding revolution requires separating the dramatic surface events (storming of the Bastille, the October Revolution) from the deep structural conditions that made those events possible.

## When This Applies

- User asks why a specific revolution happened
- User asks whether a current situation is "revolutionary"
- User compares revolutions across time or place
- User asks about coups, civil wars, or regime transitions
- User asks why some revolutions produce democracies and others produce dictatorships

## The Three Major Frameworks

### 1. Brinton's Anatomy of Revolution (1938)

Crane Brinton studied the English (1640s), American (1776), French (1789), and Russian (1917) revolutions and identified a common sequence:

| Phase | Characteristics | French Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Old regime crisis** | Fiscal bankruptcy, reformist failure, intellectual criticism | Estates-General crisis, 1789 |
| **Moderate phase** | Reformers take power, attempt constitutional change | Constitutional monarchy, 1789-1792 |
| **Radical phase** | Extremists seize control, revolutionary terror | Jacobin dictatorship, 1793-1794 |
| **Thermidor** | Reaction against radicalism, exhaustion, pragmatism | Fall of Robespierre, Directory |
| **Consolidation** | Strong leader restores order, often authoritarian | Napoleon, 1799-1804 |

**Strengths**: The sequence is remarkably durable — the French, Russian, and Iranian revolutions all follow it closely.

**Limits**: The American Revolution skips the radical/Thermidor phases. Many 20th-century revolutions don't reach consolidation (Arab Spring). The model describes but doesn't explain.

### 2. Skocpol's Structural Theory (1979)

Theda Skocpol argued that revolutions are not made by revolutionaries — they happen when structural conditions align:

**Three necessary conditions:**
1. **State fiscal crisis**: The government cannot fund its basic operations (often triggered by war debt or economic shock)
2. **Elite defection**: Landed/commercial elites withdraw support from the regime (often over taxation or exclusion from power)
3. **Peasant/popular autonomy**: Lower classes have organizational capacity independent of the state (village communes, labor unions, religious networks)

All three must coincide. Fiscal crisis alone produces reform. Elite defection alone produces a palace coup. Popular mobilization alone produces a riot. Revolution requires the convergence.

**Key insight**: Revolutionaries don't cause revolutions — they exploit structural crises that would exist with or without them. Lenin didn't create the conditions for revolution; he was prepared when they arrived.

### 3. Fourth-Generation Theory (Goldstone, 2014)

Jack Goldstone's updated framework adds dimensions Brinton and Skocpol missed:

- **Demographic pressure**: Youth bulges, urbanization, and unemployment create a volatile mass of potential participants
- **Elite overproduction** (from Turchin): Too many elite aspirants for available positions creates intra-elite conflict
- **Cross-class coalitions**: Revolutions succeed when elites and popular classes temporarily align against the regime
- **International context**: External support or intervention shapes outcomes (Cold War proxies, social media amplification)
- **Ideology matters**: The available ideological repertoire (liberalism, socialism, Islamism, nationalism) shapes what revolutionaries demand and how they govern

## Revolution vs. Other Political Ruptures

| Type | Key Feature | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Social revolution** | Rapid transformation of state AND class structure | France 1789, Russia 1917, China 1949 |
| **Political revolution** | Regime change without deep social transformation | Glorious Revolution 1688, Portugal 1974 |
| **Coup d'état** | Elite seizure of state, no mass participation | Egyptian military coups, Thai coups |
| **Civil war** | Armed conflict between organized factions | English Civil War, US Civil War, Syria |
| **Reform from above** | Regime transforms itself to survive | Meiji Restoration, Gorbachev's perestroika |
| **Color revolution** | Mass peaceful mobilization, regime concession | Ukraine 2004, Georgia 2003 |
| **Failed revolution** | Structural conditions met but consolidation fails | 1848 European revolutions, Arab Spring |

## Why Revolutions Fail

Most revolutionary situations do not produce successful revolutions. Common failure modes:

- **Insufficient elite fracture**: If the military and economic elites remain loyal to the regime, popular mobilization is crushed (Tiananmen 1989, Bahrain 2011)
- **Coalition collapse**: The cross-class coalition that overthrew the regime fractures once the common enemy is gone (Egypt after Mubarak)
- **External intervention**: Foreign powers prop up the regime or hijack the revolution (Syria, Libya)
- **Institutional vacuum**: The old order collapses but no replacement institutions exist (Libya after Gaddafi)
- **Thermidorian capture**: Moderate or military figures co-opt the revolution before structural change occurs (Egypt's military)

## The Radicalization Dynamic

Why do revolutions eat their children? The radicalization pattern:

1. **Moderates take power** but inherit the old regime's problems (debt, war, opposition)
2. **Moderates fail** to deliver on revolutionary promises fast enough
3. **Radicals outflank moderates** by promising more and mobilizing the most committed
4. **Revolutionary terror** eliminates internal opposition (real and imagined)
5. **Terror exhausts** the population and the revolutionary cadre
6. **Thermidor**: Pragmatists or military seize control, end the terror, and consolidate

This pattern is not inevitable — but it recurs often enough that any revolutionary movement should be evaluated for radicalization potential.

## Diagnostic Questions

When assessing a potentially revolutionary situation:

1. **Fiscal health**: Can the state fund itself? Is it borrowing to cover operations?
2. **Elite cohesion**: Are elites united behind the regime or fracturing?
3. **Popular organization**: Do lower classes have independent organizational capacity?
4. **Ideological alternative**: Is there a coherent alternative vision that could mobilize a coalition?
5. **Military loyalty**: Will the security forces shoot? (This is often the decisive question)
6. **International context**: Will external powers intervene, and on which side?
