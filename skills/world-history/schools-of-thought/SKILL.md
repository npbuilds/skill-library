# Schools of Thought — How Historians Disagree

The same past looks different depending on who is writing the history and what questions they ask. Marxist historians see class struggle; Annales historians see long-term structures; postcolonial historians see imperial power; gender historians see patriarchy. These are not random disagreements — they are systematic methodological traditions, each with its own assumptions, methods, strengths, and blind spots. Understanding them is essential because every history you read was written within one of these frameworks, and knowing the framework reveals what it illuminates and what it hides.

## When This Applies

- User asks "why do historians disagree about X?"
- User asks about a specific school (Annales, Marxist, postcolonial, etc.)
- User encounters competing interpretations of the same event
- User asks about the history of history writing
- User asks about non-Western approaches to history

## The Major Schools

| School | Period | Core Method | Key Figures | Strength | Blind Spot |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Rankean** | 1830s+ | Archival research; "wie es eigentlich gewesen" (as it actually was) | Ranke | Rigorous source criticism; established history as a discipline | Assumes objectivity is possible; ignores power in archive formation |
| **Marxist** | 1840s+ | Material conditions and class struggle drive history | Marx, Thompson, Hobsbawm | Reveals economic forces and inequality invisible to political history | Can reduce everything to class; underestimates culture and ideas |
| **Annales** | 1929+ | Long-term structures (longue duree); total history; quantitative methods | Bloch, Febvre, Braudel, Le Roy Ladurie | Reveals deep forces; crosses disciplinary boundaries | Can neglect agency and events; sometimes inaccessibly abstract |
| **Social history** | 1960s+ | History from below; ordinary people's experience | Thompson, Gutman, Zinn | Recovers voices excluded from political history | Can lose sight of power structures by focusing on experience |
| **Postcolonial** | 1970s+ | Critique of imperial knowledge; subaltern agency; decentering the West | Said, Spivak, Guha, Chakrabarty | Reveals how colonialism shaped knowledge production itself | Risk of relativism; "the subaltern cannot speak" problem |
| **Gender/feminist** | 1970s+ | Gender as analytical category; women's experience and agency | Scott, Davis, Ulrich | Transforms every field by asking "where are the women?" | Early work focused on white Western women; intersectionality corrects this |
| **Microhistory** | 1970s+ | Intensive study of a single case to reveal larger structures | Ginzburg, Davis, Darnton | Vivid, accessible; reveals complexity invisible at macro scale | Risk of unrepresentative cases; generalization is difficult |
| **Linguistic turn** | 1980s+ | Language and discourse constitute reality, not just reflect it | White, LaCapra | Reveals how narrative shapes meaning; challenges naive realism | Can lose connection to material reality; "everything is text" overreach |
| **Digital humanities** | 2000s+ | Computational methods: text mining, GIS, network analysis, visualization | Moretti, White (Stanford) | Reveals patterns invisible to human reading; handles massive datasets | Risk of algorithmic reductionism; requires technical skills |

## Non-Western Historiographical Traditions

The Western schools above are not the only ways humans have organized historical knowledge:

- **Islamic historiography**: Ibn Khaldun's Muqaddimah (1377) — empirical social science; cyclical theory of dynasties; arguably the first modern historian. Islamic tradition also produced extensive biographical dictionaries (tabaqat) and annalistic chronicles.
- **Chinese dynastic historiography**: Each new dynasty commissioned an official history of the previous dynasty. The Shiji (Records of the Grand Historian, ~94 BCE) by Sima Qian established a model followed for 2,000 years. The tradition valued moral exemplarity and cyclical patterns.
- **Indigenous oral traditions**: Not "pre-literate history" but a fundamentally different epistemology. Aboriginal Australian Dreamtime narratives encode tens of thousands of years of ecological knowledge. Oral traditions are performative, communal, and adaptive in ways written history is not.

## How Schools Shape Interpretation

The same event looks different through different lenses. Take the French Revolution:

- **Marxist** (Soboul): Class struggle — the bourgeoisie overthrew the aristocracy
- **Revisionist** (Furet): Not class struggle but political culture — revolutionary ideology itself was the driving force
- **Social** (Hunt): Everyday experience of revolution — festivals, symbols, language
- **Gender** (Scott): The revolution redefined citizenship as male; women were excluded despite participating
- **Postcolonial** (James, CLR): The Haitian Revolution (1791-1804) was as important as the French; the "universal" rights of 1789 excluded enslaved people

## Diagnostic Framework

When analyzing a historiographical tradition or interpretation:

1. **What school is this written in?** (And what are its assumptions?)
2. **What does this framework reveal?** (Its distinctive contribution)
3. **What does it hide?** (Every framework has blind spots)
4. **What would a different school emphasize?** (At least one alternative reading)
5. **What evidence does this framework privilege?** (Archives? Statistics? Oral testimony? Discourse?)
6. **Is non-Western historiography represented?** (If not, why not — and what would it add?)
