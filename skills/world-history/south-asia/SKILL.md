# South Asia — The Subcontinental World

South Asia holds a fifth of humanity and one of history's longest continuous civilizational conversations — yet Western curricula typically reduce it to a colonial backstory. The subcontinent is where four world religions were born, where the deepest-running social institution in recorded history (caste) was built and contested, and where the modern world's largest democracy now runs. Its master question has been live for 4,500 years: is "India" one thing or many — and who benefits from each answer?

## When This Applies

- User asks about Indian history (any period: Harappan, Vedic, Mauryan, Gupta, Sultanate, Mughal, colonial, republic)
- User asks about Pakistan, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Nepal, or Partition
- User asks about caste, Hinduism/Buddhism/Jainism/Sikhism in historical context, or Indo-Islamic civilization
- User asks about the Indian Ocean trade world, the East India Company, or the Raj
- User asks about Gandhi, decolonization in South Asia, or the India–Pakistan rivalry

| **Republics** (1947–present) | India, Pakistan, Bangladesh | Southern growth engines | Gulf labor migration, IT diaspora |

## Regional Distinctives

**What makes South Asian history distinctive:**

- **Caste as social technology**: The varna–jati order is not a timeless essence but a continuously renegotiated system — hardened at identifiable moments (early medieval land grants, colonial censuses) and contested at others (bhakti movements, Ambedkar). Treating it as static is the single most common analytical error.
- **A religious genesis zone**: Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism, and Sikhism all emerged here, and Islam became demographically massive — making religious plurality, patronage, and boundary-drawing a permanent axis of statecraft from Ashoka's dhamma to the secularism–Hindutva contest.
- **The northwest gateway**: Nearly every major arrival — Indo-Aryans, Achaemenids, Alexander, Kushans, Hunas, Ghurids, Timur, Mughals, Durranis — came through the same passes. Geography made the subcontinent invadable but never fully holdable.
- **Palimpsest statehood**: No empire before the Raj governed the whole subcontinent; every "pan-Indian" state (Maurya, Mughal, Raj, Republic) sat atop persistent regional polities. The segmentary-state and layered-sovereignty debates exist because the evidence genuinely underdetermines how deep imperial control ran.
- **Three cosmopolises**: Sanskrit, then Persian, then English each served as a transregional language of power (Pollock's "Sanskrit cosmopolis") — elite linguistic worlds that unified administration and culture without unifying politics.
- **The monsoon ocean**: Predictable seasonal winds made the Indian Ocean history's oldest long-distance trade system; South Asia sat at its center, exporting textiles for two millennia before deindustrialization became the nationalist grievance.

## The Uses of the Past

South Asian historiography is unusually politicized, and honest analysis names the stakes:

- **The Aryan question**: Indo-Aryan migration (mainstream, now genetically supported by Narasimhan et al. 2019) vs Out-of-India indigenism (Hindutva-aligned). The debate is about whether "Hindu civilization" is autochthonous.
- **The drain and deindustrialization**: Naoroji's drain theory through Tharoor's popular restatement vs Tirthankar Roy's institutional revisionism — did Britain underdevelop India, and by how much?
- **Temple desecration**: Eaton's counted-and-contextualized ~80 documented cases vs the civilizational-wound narrative of thousands — the historiographic core of contemporary mosque disputes.
- **Partition responsibility**: Jalal's Jinnah-as-bargainer vs Congress-centric and British-blame accounts.

## Connected Histories

- **To Central Asia**: The gateway passes; Kushan and Mughal states were Central Asian in origin; the Timurid inheritance
- **To Southeast Asia**: Indianization — scripts, Sanskrit, Shaivism, Buddhism carried by traders and brahmins; Chola naval strikes on Srivijaya
- **To East Asia**: Buddhism's export along the Silk Road; Xuanzang's pilgrimage; the 1962 war and contemporary rivalry
- **To East Africa and the Gulf**: Dhow trade, Gujarati merchant diaspora, indenture and modern labor migration
- **To Europe**: Roman pepper trade; the companies; the Raj as the keystone of British world power; the largest modern diaspora

## Diagnostic Framework

When analyzing South Asian history:

1. **Where are the frontiers?** (Who controls how much of the subcontinent — and where do the northwest passes, the Vindhyas, and the Deccan sit in the story?)
2. **How is the social order being made?** (What is varna–jati doing in this period — hardening, loosening, being counted, being contested?)
3. **How is plurality being governed?** (Which traditions get patronage; is the moment syncretic or boundary-drawing?)
4. **Who is arriving, and carrying what?** (Through the passes or on the monsoon winds — conquerors, merchants, missionaries, capital?)
5. **How does this connect to global patterns?** (South Asia is never studied in isolation.)
